## 1. The Sociological Imagination & Empirical Methods

### 1.1 The Sociological Imagination (C. Wright Mills)

The sociological imagination enables the observer to grasp history and biography and the relations between the two within society:
- **Personal Troubles:** Occur within the character of the individual and within the range of their immediate relations with others (e.g. an individual experiencing unemployment in a thriving economy).
- **Public Issues:** Transcend the local environment of the individual and involve institutional crises or structural contradictions (e.g. widespread structural unemployment driven by automation and deindustrialization).

### 1.2 The Social Construction of Reality (Berger & Luckmann)

Knowledge, institutional rules, and social roles are not biological inevitabilities; they are socially produced through a continuous dialectic:
1. **Externalization:** Humans create cultural artifacts, symbols, and practices.
2. **Objectivation:** Those creations confront their creators as an objective, independent social reality.
3. **Internalization:** New generations absorb these objectified social facts through primary and secondary socialization, naturalizing institutional norms.

## 2. The Classical Founders: Durkheim, Weber, and Marx

### 2.1 Émile Durkheim: Social Facts & Moral Cohesion

Durkheim established sociology as an autonomous empirical science with a distinct object of study: **social facts**—ways of acting, thinking, and feeling external to the individual, endowed with coercive power.

1. **Division of Labor in Society:**
   - *Mechanical Solidarity:* Found in traditional, undifferentiated societies with low division of labor; solidarity relies on a strong collective conscience and repressive punitive law.
   - *Organic Solidarity:* Found in modern industrial societies with complex specialized divisions of labor; solidarity emerges from mutual functional interdependence and restitutive civil law.
2. **Anomie:** A state of normlessness occurring when rapid economic or social transformations outpace moral and normative regulation, severing individuals from collective social anchoring.
3. **Empirical Study of Suicide:** Demonstrated that suicide rates reflect varying degrees of **social integration** (egoistic vs altruistic) and **moral regulation** (anomic vs fatalistic), proving individual acts possess macroscopic structural causes.

### 2.2 Max Weber: Interpretive Sociology & Rationalization

Weber founded **interpretive sociology** (*Verstehende Soziologie*), defining sociology as the science that seeks to understand the interpretive meaning of social action (*soziales Handeln*):

1. **The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism:** Traced the psychological impetus of modern rational capitalism to the Calvinist doctrine of predestination and worldly asceticism, wherein systematic economic diligence served as a psychological sign of grace.
2. **Rationalization & The Iron Cage:** Modernity replaces traditional values, emotions, and customs with formal calculability, technical efficiency, and bureaucratic administration. The individual is enclosed in an "iron cage" (*stahlhartes Gehäuse*) of rationalized bureaucratic structures.
3. **Tripartite Model of Legitimate Authority:**
   - *Traditional Authority:* Grounded in sanctity of immemorial customs and patriarchal lineage (e.g. hereditary monarchies).
   - *Charismatic Authority:* Grounded in extraordinary personal devotion to the heroic or prophetic character of a leader (revolutionary, inherently unstable).
   - *Rational-Legal Authority:* Grounded in codified legality, procedural validity, and impersonal bureaucratic office (modern constitutional states).

### 2.3 Karl Marx: Historical Materialism & Class Struggle

Marx argued that the economic mode of production forms the foundational substructure (**base**) that conditions the legal, political, religious, and philosophical forms of consciousness (**superstructure**):

1. **Dialectical Materialism:** History is the chronicle of evolving productive forces coming into conflict with established relations of production:
   $$\text{Forces of Production} \iff \text{Relations of Production}$$
2. **Capitalist Class Relations:** Society polarizes into two primary classes:
   - *Bourgeoisie:* Owners of the means of production (capital, factories, land).
   - *Proletariat:* Wage laborers who possess only their labor power, forced to sell it to survive.
3. **Alienation of Labor (*Entfremdung*):** Capitalist commodity production alienates the worker on four distinct planes: from the product of their labor, from the act of production, from their human nature (*Gattungswesen*), and from fellow workers.

## 4. Social Stratification, Class & Cultural Capital

### 4.1 Pierre Bourdieu: Forms of Capital & Habitus

Bourdieu demonstrated that social stratification is maintained and reproduced across generations through multiple interchangeable currencies of power:

1. **Economic Capital:** Immediately convertible into money; institutionalized in property rights and financial assets.
2. **Cultural Capital:** Exists in three states:
   - *Embodied State:* Long-lasting dispositions of the mind and body (accent, taste, posture, linguistic ease).
   - *Objectified State:* Possession of cultural goods (books, art collections, musical instruments).
   - *Institutionalized State:* Academic credentials and university degrees conferring official social competence.
3. **Social Capital:** Aggregate of actual or potential resources linked to possession of a durable network of institutionalized relationships and mutual acquaintance.
4. **Habitus:** Internalized, durable systems of generative dispositions acquired through primary socialization in a specific social class, guiding unconscious choices, aesthetic tastes, and behavioral styles.
5. **Symbolic Violence:** The tacit imposition of categories of thought and perception by dominant groups upon dominated groups, who perceive the existing hierarchical order as natural, legitimate, and meritocratic.

## 5. Cultural Anthropology, Relativism & Ethnography

### 5.1 Cultural Relativism vs. Ethnocentrism

- **Ethnocentrism:** The tendency to judge other cultures by the standards and norms of one's own culture, often assuming intrinsic superiority.
- **Methodological Cultural Relativism (Franz Boas):** An epistemological axiom mandating that cultural beliefs, practices, and values must be understood within their own historical and ecological context, without prior normative judgment.

### 5.2 Ethnographic Fieldwork & Thick Description

- **Participant Observation (Bronisław Malinowski):** Immersion in the daily life, language, and cultural practices of a community over extended duration, distinguishing what people say they do from what they actually do.
- **Thick Description (Clifford Geertz):** Analysis of culture as an ensemble of semiotic texts. Geertz famously contrasted a physiological twitch of the eyelid with a deliberate wink, conspiratorial wink, or parody of a wink: the task of anthropology is to decipher the dense stratified web of symbolic meaning that transforms physical motion into meaningful cultural action.

## 6. Kinship, Gender, Family & Socialization

- **Kinship Structures:** Lineages organized via patrilineal, matrilineal, or bilateral descent; post-marital residence patterns (patrilocal, matrilocal, neolocal); structural rules prohibiting incest and governing marital alliance (exogamy vs endogamy, Claude Lévi-Strauss).
- **Socialization:** The lifelong process through which individuals internalize cultural norms, values, and social roles.
  - *Cooley's Looking-Glass Self:* Self-conception emerges from imagining how we appear to others, imagining their judgment, and developing an emotional self-feeling.
  - *Mead's "I" and "Me":* The spontaneous individual impulse ("I") versus the socialized internal representation of the community's attitudes and norms ("The Generalized Other").

## 7. Institutions of Social Control, Deviance & Discipline

### 7.1 Michel Foucault: The Panopticon & Disciplinary Power

In *Discipline and Punish*, Foucault traces the historical mutation of sovereign power:
- **Sovereign Power (Pre-Modern):** Public, spectacular, physical violence executed upon the body of the transgressor to reassert sovereign majesty.
- **Disciplinary Power (Modern):** Invisible, pervasive, continuous surveillance operating through micro-practices of timetables, examinations, and spatial partitioning.
- **The Panopticon (Bentham/Foucault):** An architectural apparatus featuring a central watchtower overlooking peripheral annular cells. Because inmates never know when they are being observed, the external gaze is internalized: the prisoner becomes the principle of their own subjection.

## 8. Globalization, World-Systems Theory & Modernity

### 8.1 Immanuel Wallerstein's World-Systems Analysis

Modern global capitalism operates not as a collection of independent sovereign states, but as a single global division of labor characterized by unequal exchange:
- **Core Countries:** High-skill, capital-intensive production, strong state institutions, monopolistic surplus extraction.
- **Periphery Countries:** Low-skill, labor-intensive primary resource extraction, weak state apparatuses, subordinated through global terms of trade.
- **Semi-Periphery:** Intermediate regions buffering the system against structural systemic collapse.
