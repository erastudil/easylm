# Light on a surface, then a picture

This book is light on a surface, then a picture. A person who can read can walk it.

This pack owns aperture, exposure, and print walks. Architecture owns shelter and daylight in a room. Name that door and stop. Electromagnetism owns fields. Name that door and stop. Media owns an ad and a caption as a claim. Name that door and stop.

---

## 1. Light on a surface

Sun hits the white tablecloth on Mira's stall at Reed Point. A kettle sits on the cloth. The cloth under the kettle is darker than the cloth around it. Mira moves the kettle. The dark patch moves with it. The cloth did not change weave. The sun still sits in the same place. What changed is what the sun could reach.

A second cloth, dyed nearly black, lies next to the white one. Same sun. Same hour. The white cloth hurts to look at. The black cloth does not. Both sit in the open. The difference is not the sun. The difference is what each cloth does with the light that lands.

Light that lands on a surface can bounce, or stay, or do some of both. The white cloth sends a large share back toward Mira's eyes. The black cloth keeps a large share. The kettle blocks a patch, so that patch receives less. A picture, later in this book, is a way to keep a trace of that landing. This chapter is only the landing.

How light is a field in motion lives in electromagnetism. Name that door and stop. Daylight that makes a room livable as shelter lives in architecture. Name that door and stop. Mira's stall is a tablecloth in sun, not a room.

The landing has a name once you have seen it. People call the light that reaches a surface **illumination**. The name is a handle for the sun on the cloth, not a second sun. A bright stall and a stall under the ferry roof differ in illumination. They do not differ in the weave of the cloth until the light has done its work.

A small landing can still be a landing. NIST's home page, on 20 September 2026, listed a news row dated 24 August 2026: researchers there supersized a quantum tool to help detect faint photons. Photon is their word for a tiny bit of light. How that detector is built is unverified here. Open that NIST news door when the detector must travel. The stall walk does not need the detector. It needs the cloth, the kettle, and the sun.

Three white napkins and one dark napkin, same sun, same minute. The three white ones bounce more. The one dark one bounces less. 3 and 1 are a count of cloths, not a count of light. Do not add 3 + 1 and call 4 a brightness. Brightness is not a headcount of napkins.

**Check.** Draw the stall. Mark the white cloth, the black cloth, and the kettle's patch. Write which patch receives the least sun. If you marked the white cloth, you counted glare instead of landing. The kettle's patch is the one the sun does not reach. New case: a wet stone and a dry stone on the same step. The wet stone looks darker. Same sun. The water changed what bounced. If you wrote that the sun moved, you moved the wrong object.

## 2. A hole

Mira takes a shoe box, paints the inside black, and pushes a pin through one end. She points the pin at the tablecloth. On the far wall of the box a faint cloth appears, kettle and all, upside down. She did not pour the cloth through the pin. Light from the cloth went through the hole and landed on the far wall.

She makes a second hole with a nail. The picture on the far wall is brighter. The edges are softer. The hole let more light in. It also let light from more directions mix. This chapter keeps the first fact: a larger hole, more light, if the sun and the time hold still.

People name that hole an **aperture**. The name is the pin, the nail, and any later ring that does the same job in a camera. This pack owns the aperture walk. A window that lights a room as shelter is architecture. Name that door and stop. The pin is not a window you live in. It is a hole that times a landing.

Diameter is a length across the hole. Mira measures the pin hole as 1 millimetre across and the nail hole as 2 millimetres across. 2 is twice 1. The opening is a surface, not a line. Double the length across and the opening becomes 2 times 2 = 4 times as large, if the hole stays a circle. Four times the opening, four times the light, if the cloth, the sun, and the time hold. That 4 is arithmetic on a doubling. It is not a camera brand's secret number.

A third box gets two pin holes, each 1 millimetre across. Two equal holes are two of the first opening. 1 + 1 = 2 times the light of one pin, not 4. Two holes are a count of openings. Doubling the length across one hole is a change of size. Do not mix those two moves.

Units for length live in the measurement pack and on NIST as the United States national measurement institute. NIST's home page names that role and marks 1901 as its start, with a 125 year heading on that same page. How a millimetre is fixed is unverified here. Open the NIST door when the unit must travel. Mira's 1 millimetre and 2 millimetres are stall lengths she can compare with a ruler.

**Check.** A hole 3 millimetres across, then a hole 9 millimetres across. 9 divided by 3 is 3, so the length across tripled. 3 times 3 = 9, so the opening is nine times, and the light is nine times if sun and time hold. If you wrote 3 times the light, you scaled the length and forgot the opening is a surface. New case: two nail holes, each 2 millimetres across, in one box. That is 2 times one nail, not 4 times, unless you also doubled a length. Write both numbers. Circle the one that is a count of holes.

## 3. Time

Mira covers the pin with a thumb. The far wall of the box goes dark. She lifts the thumb and counts: one, two. She covers again. The wall received two seconds of the cloth. She repeats, counting to eight. Eight seconds is 8 divided by 2 = 4 times two seconds. Four times the time, four times the light, if the hole and the sun hold still.

The far wall is a sheet of paper that darkens as light lands. Two seconds leaves a faint kettle. Eight seconds leaves a darker kettle. The hole did not change. The sun did not change. The clock changed.

People name the open interval a **shutter** time when a door or a thumb does the covering. They name the whole pile that lands, hole and time together, an **exposure**. This pack owns that walk. Exposure is not a mood about a pretty stall. It is a pile you can change by opening more, or by waiting more, or both.

Keep the pile the same while you change the parts. The nail hole was 4 times the pin in chapter 2. If Mira uses the nail for 2 seconds, she can match the pin's 8 second pile: 4 times the hole and 8 divided by 4 = 2 seconds. 4 times 2 = 8, which is the pin's time. The kettle on the wall should match if the sun held. If it does not match, a part of the walk failed. Write which part, hole or time or sun, before you invent a new name.

A leaf blows across the cloth. Two seconds, the leaf is a smear. The hole was fine. The time was long next to the leaf's move. A smear from motion is a time problem. A smear from a large hole mixing directions is an aperture problem. Split them. Chapter 4 treats a different smear: the wrong distance drawn sharp.

Do not borrow a camera's printed times from memory. 1/125 of a second, and other marks on a dial, are unverified here. They did not appear on the NASA, NIST, Library of Congress, or National Archives fronts used for numbers in this book. Count whole seconds on Mira's stall, or write unverified and open a later door.

**Check.** Mira opens 5 seconds, rests, then opens 15 seconds more with the same hole. 5 + 15 = 20 seconds of landing. If you wrote 15, you kept the second count and dropped the first. New case: three thumb-lifts of 2 seconds each, same hole, same sun. 3 times 2 = 6 seconds of pile. If you added 3 + 2 and wrote 5, you added a count of lifts to a count of seconds. Those sticks do not share a mark.

## 4. Focus

The box sits on the stall. The kettle is 1.2 metres from the pin. The ferry at the dock is much farther. Mira looks at the far wall. The kettle's spout is a clean line. The ferry is a smear. She slides the far wall closer to the pin. The ferry tightens. The spout falls apart.

Light through a small hole can still draw more than one distance. Only some of those distances land as a clean line on a given wall. The wall's place, and the hole's place, pick a distance that comes out clean. The rest smear.

People name that clean distance **focus**. The name is the spout that is a line, not a feeling that the picture is good. A picture can be bright and out of focus. Chapter 2 and chapter 3 already moved brightness. This chapter moves which plane is a line.

A cheap magnifying glass in front of the hole can throw a brighter clean kettle, and a thinner slice of distances that stay clean. How a lens bends a field lives in electromagnetism if you need the field, and in a later optics door if you need the glass law. Unverified here as a formula. Do not finish a focal length in millimetres from memory and call it this stall's law. Write the two distances you can pace: 1.2 metres to the kettle, then a longer walk to the ferry. Pace the ferry if you can. If you cannot, write that the ferry is farther, and stop. Empty is legal. A guessed 50 millimetres of glass is not.

Asha holds a string from pin to kettle: 1.2 metres. She does not have a string to the ferry. She writes 8 metres after pacing along the dock in 1 metre steps, eight of them, from a mark under the box toward the bow. 8 is a paced length, not a camera mark. 8 divided by 1.2 is not a focus law. It is how many kettle-strings fit in the dock walk. Do not treat that ratio as a magic sharpness number.

**Check.** Two planes: a sign at 5 metres, a hill behind it. You may have one clean plane on one wall place. Write which plane you want clean before you slide the wall. If both are clean and you did not change the hole, you have not yet seen the slice, or the hill is not as far as you thought. Pace it. New case: Ren's face 0.8 metres from the pin, the stall sign 4 metres behind him. Pick a wall place for the face. Write that the sign will smear. If you wrote both clean, you used hope as a lens.

## 5. A print

Cole stands in a dim room behind the stall. He takes the paper that sat on the far wall of the box and lays it in a tray. The kettle is there, dark where the sun was bright. He puts a second, fresh sheet under a lamp that shines through that first sheet onto the new paper. The new paper darkens in the opposite way. He lifts it. A kettle he can hold. He pegs it on a line. The box is back on the stall. The cloth is still on the table. The sheet on the line is the thing that travels.

A picture you can hold, on paper or a like surface, is a **print**. A picture on a phone is a cousin. It is not this object. Cole can fold the sheet, mail it, drop it in a puddle. The phone picture does not wrinkle with him. This pack owns the print walk. How an ad uses a picture lives in media. Name that door and stop.

Cole makes 11 sheets from the same first look. He keeps 4. 11 - 4 = 7 sheets do not leave the room. 4 prints travel. The 7 are still pictures. They are not the set he will show. A count of sheets is not a count of kettles in the world. One kettle sat on the cloth. Eleven sheets can hold that one kettle.

A print can be the size of the first sheet, laid against it. A print can be larger if the lamp and a second hole throw a larger landing onto a larger sheet. How many times larger is a length you measure with a ruler on the two sheets. Do not fetch a shop's "enlargement" name and treat it as a law. 2 times the length is 2 times. 2 times 2 = 4 times the paper area. That 4 is the same arithmetic as the hole in chapter 2, now on paper.

The Library of Congress prints and photographs door is loc.gov/pictures. A count of prints, negatives, or digitized files from this paragraph is unverified. Open that door when a historic print must be seen. The National Archives front is archives.gov. A holdings count from this paragraph is unverified. Open that door when a federal photograph must be fetched. NASA's home page lists an Images shelf and an Image of the Day. Those are pictures on a host, not Cole's peg. Use them as doors. Do not call a screen a print.

How long a chemical tray must sit is unverified here. Cole counts his own seconds on this stall. A remembered "ninety seconds in the tray" from another room does not travel into this book as a rule.

**Check.** 11 sheets, 4 kept. Write 7 left in the room. Then write what one kept sheet can prove: that Cole made a landing of that kettle on paper that day. It cannot prove the sun's angle tomorrow. New case: two sheets, one 10 centimetres on a side, one 20 centimetres on a side. 20 is twice 10. The larger sheet has 2 times 2 = 4 times the area. If you wrote 2 times the paper, you scaled a length and called it an area. Measure both.

## 6. Color

Mira lays a red cloth, a green cloth, and a white cloth in the same sun. The white cloth still hurts to look at. The red cloth does not look green. The green cloth does not look red. The sun did not pick a favorite. Each cloth bounced a different share of what landed.

Sunlight is mixed. A cloth can bounce some of the mix and keep the rest. The red cloth sends back the part that looks red to Mira, and keeps much of the rest. The green cloth does the other job. The white cloth sends back a large share of the mix. People name that split **color**. The name is the red cloth next to the green cloth, not a feeling about a pretty stall.

Asha holds a red glass over the pin. The far wall of the box goes dimmer, and the green cloth on that wall goes near-black. The red cloth still draws. The glass let the red share through and kept much of the rest. The hole did not change size. The time did not change. The mix that entered the hole changed.

Do not write a wavelength in nanometres from memory. Those numbers did not appear on the four fronts used for load-bearing figures in this book. Unverified. Open a NIST or other metrology door when a wavelength must travel. The stall walk is red glass, green cloth, white cloth.

A print in color is three landings, or a like split, stacked so the eye reads them as one cloth. How a printer names those inks is unverified here. Cole can still make one black-and-white print of the red cloth. That print can be honest about brightness and silent about red. Silence is legal. Printing the red cloth as if it were the green one is a false color, not a silence.

NASA's home page, on the same day, showed pictures from Hubble, from the James Webb Space Telescope, and from Chandra. Those three hosts do not all catch the same mix. A gallery count is in chapter 8. A color claim about a nebula is a caption job in chapter 9. Do not look at a false-color space picture and say the stall cloths work the same way without a tag. Tag the stall as sun-and-eye. Tag the space picture as the host's mix. Split them.

**Check.** Red glass over the pin, 4 seconds, green cloth on the stall. Write whether that cloth draws light or dark on the far wall next to a red cloth shot the same way. The green cloth should draw darker. If you wrote brighter, you treated the glass as more hole, not as a mix filter. New case: white cloth, no glass, 4 seconds. Then white cloth, red glass, 4 seconds, same hole. The second pile is smaller. If you wrote equal piles, you counted time and ignored the glass.

## 7. A negative as a first look

The paper on the far wall of the box darkens where light lands. The sky over Reed Point is bright. That sky comes out dark on the first sheet. The kettle's shadow on the cloth is dark in the world. That shadow comes out light on the first sheet. Mira holds the sheet up. It is the stall, with bright and dark swapped.

That first swapped look is a **negative**. The name is this sheet, not a mood. A second landing, as in chapter 5, swaps again. Dark sky on the negative lets less lamp through, so the print's sky stays light. Light shadow on the negative lets more lamp through, so the print's shadow goes dark. Two swaps bring the stall back.

Asha draws the stall in four panes: bright sky, white cloth, kettle, shadow. On the negative she marks sky dark, cloth dark-grey, kettle mixed, shadow light. Four panes. Four marks. If she marks the sky light on the negative, she has already printed, or she has not seen the first look.

Some cameras skip the paper negative and keep a file that can invert on a screen. The invert is the same swap. The file is chapter 8's grid. The swap is this chapter. Do not call the file a print. Cole's peg is the print.

A historic negative in a vault is still this object. The Library of Congress prints and photographs door and the National Archives door are the shelves. A count of negatives in either vault is unverified here. Fetch the door. Do not invent a vault total and stand on it.

**Check.** Four bright windows on a dark wall. On the first look, four dark window marks on a light field. If you drew four light windows, you skipped the swap. New case: a dark open doorway in a bright alley. On the first look the doorway should be a light mark. If you drew it dark, you copied the world onto the negative without the swap. Draw both: world, then first look. The two must disagree on bright and dark.

## 8. A grid of samples

Asha rules the far wall into 8 rows and 12 columns of small squares. 8 times 12 = 96 squares. For each square she writes a number from 0 to 8 for how dark the paper went. The kettle's spout is not a line anymore. It is a run of squares with higher numbers than the cloth.

A picture stored as a list of those squares is a **grid of samples**. People also say **pixel** for one square. The name arrives after the ruler. 96 is Asha's count on this wall, not a camera's advertised pile. A shop megapixel figure is unverified here. It did not appear on the four fronts. Write 96, or write unverified.

Crop is a cut of the grid. Asha keeps an 8 by 8 block over the kettle. 8 times 8 = 64 squares kept. 96 - 64 = 32 squares unused. The unused 32 were as real as the 64. Crop does not unsay them. It leaves them out of the traveling grid.

NASA's home page listed a Hubble gallery of 422 images, a James Webb Space Telescope gallery of 39 images, and a Chandra gallery of 24 images. Those three numbers are counts of pictures on those shelves, not counts of squares inside one picture. 422 + 39 = 461. 461 + 24 = 485 pictures across those three listed galleries. 422 - 39 = 383 more Hubble pictures than Webb pictures on those two shelves. Do not turn 422 into a pixel count. A gallery is a pile of pictures. A grid is a pile of squares inside one picture.

Image of the Day on nasa.gov for that front named a July 8, 2026 photograph: engineers watching a COSI detector assembly lifted from a table. That is one picture. How many samples sit inside it is unverified here. Open the Image of the Day door when the picture must be seen. Open NASA Images when you need the larger shelf.

**Check.** 10 rows by 14 columns. 10 times 14 = 140 samples. If you wrote 24, you added 10 + 14. Addition is a join of two lengths, not a grid. New case: crop a 6 by 6 block from 10 by 14. 6 times 6 = 36 kept. 140 - 36 = 104 unused. If you wrote 36 as the whole picture, you let the crop erase the rest of the wall.

## 9. What the caption claims

Mira pegs a print of the 07:40 ferry at Reed Point. Cole pins a slip under it: "Reed Point ferry, 07:40, 12 April, bow to the left." Asha pins a second slip: "Hope returns to the river." A stranger who only reads Asha's slip cannot find the bow, the clock, or the dock. A stranger who reads Cole's slip can walk to the dock at 07:40 and look.

The line under a picture that says what the picture is, is a **caption**. This chapter owns that line as a claim about a landing of light. Media owns an ad and a caption as a claim in a report, a feed, or a paid block. Name that door and stop. Stay here when the job is: does this line match the light that landed, or does it sell a fog.

Cole's slip names a place, a clock, a date, and a side. Those four can fail. The bow might face right. The clock might read 07:55. Failure is useful. Asha's slip cannot fail in that way, because hope has no bow.

NASA's home page captioned a launch picture: NASA's SpaceX Crew-10, Friday, March 14, 2025, 7:03 p.m. EDT, Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center, with NASA astronauts Anne McClain and Nichole Ayers, JAXA astronaut Takuya Onishi, and Roscosmos cosmonaut Kirill Peskov, a six month mission. Those names, that clock, that pad, and that date are a caption a stranger can test against other NASA doors. A slip that said "the future lifted off in glory" is Asha's fog in a space costume. Keep the 7:03 p.m. EDT. Drop the glory, or move the glory off the caption.

A second NASA caption on that front: a NASA F-15 flying next to a NASA F/A-18 on July 2, 2026, from Spokane, Washington, to Washington, D.C., credit NASA/Jim Ross. Place, date, two aircraft, a path, a name on the maker. If a slip drops Jim Ross and adds "a nation soars," the soaring is not a testable chair. Put the aircraft back.

International Observe the Moon Night, on that same NASA front, asked people to look at the Moon on Sept. 19, and said the event has run since 2010. A picture of the Moon that night still needs a caption that names the Moon, the night, and the place the camera stood. "Wonder" is not a place.

**Check.** Write two slips for Mira's ferry print. One must name Reed Point, 07:40, and the bow. One must name a mood and omit the dock. Hand both, without the print, to a person who has not seen the river. Ask where to stand at 07:40. Only Cole's slip should produce a dock. New case: the COSI picture dated July 8, 2026, engineers and a detector assembly lifted from a table. Write a caption that keeps July 8, 2026, and the lift. Write a second that says "science dreams upward." Circle the second. That circle is a claim the photograph cannot carry.

## 10. A check

Walk one picture from landing to caption, with new numbers, then open two official doors on a picture you did not make.

Mira's stall, late day. Hole 4 millimetres across. Time 8 seconds. The cloth is white. The kettle is 1.2 metres from the pin. She wants the kettle clean, the ferry smeared. Cole makes 5 prints and keeps 2. 5 - 2 = 3 stay in the room. Caption: "Kettle on Mira's stall, Reed Point, 16:20, 4 millimetre hole, 8 seconds."

Change one part. Hole 8 millimetres across. Length across doubled. Opening 2 times 2 = 4 times. To keep the pile, time becomes 8 divided by 4 = 2 seconds. If Cole's second print is much darker than the first, the 4 times failed, or the sun moved, or the thumb counted wrong. Write which. Do not invent a fourth cause named "the camera's soul."

Asha grids the 2 second print at 5 rows by 8 columns. 5 times 8 = 40 samples. She crops 4 by 4 over the kettle: 16 kept, 40 - 16 = 24 unused. The caption still names the stall. The crop is not a new place. If she writes "ferry at night" under the kettle crop, the caption has left the light.

Now the doors. Door one: NASA Image of the Day, and the Images shelf on nasa.gov. The front used for this book named a July 8, 2026 COSI lift, a Hubble gallery of 422 images, a Webb gallery of 39, a Chandra gallery of 24, a Crew-10 launch at 7:03 p.m. EDT on March 14, 2025 from Launch Complex 39A, and a July 2, 2026 F-15 with an F/A-18. Write one of those pictures. Copy the host's caption chairs: date, place, who, what. If a chair is empty on the host, write empty. Do not fill it from a diner.

Door two: loc.gov/pictures, the Library of Congress prints and photographs shelf, or archives.gov, the National Archives front. A count of pictures on either door is unverified here. Open the door. Write what it actually shows: a search box, a collection name, a photograph with a caption, or a page that will not load. If it will not load, write unverified and stop. Do not paste NASA's 422 into the Library of Congress. Those 422 belong to the Hubble gallery row on NASA.

NIST remains the measurement host: 1901, 125 years on that front, and the 24 August 2026 faint-photon row. Use NIST when a unit or a detector claim must travel. Do not use NIST to caption the ferry.

If NASA and the Library of Congress disagree on what a picture is of, print both tags. Do not average two captions into a third. The move is the same as two holes and two times: keep the parts. Architecture still owns the room. Electromagnetism still owns the field. Media still owns the ad. This book still owns the landing, the hole, the time, the clean plane, the print, the mix, the first swapped look, the grid, and the line under the sheet.

**Check.** On paper, two columns. Column stall: 4 millimetre hole, 8 seconds, then 8 millimetre hole, 2 seconds, 5 prints, 2 kept, caption with 16:20. Column NASA: one named picture from the list above, with its date and place, or unverified. If the two columns share the same words, you copied one object onto the other. Pull them apart until a stranger can see which light landed on Mira's cloth and which light landed on a NASA camera.

New case: Webb 39 and Hubble 422. Write 422 - 39 = 383. That 383 is a difference of gallery counts on NASA's front. It is not 383 more stars. If you wrote "383 worlds," you turned a shelf count into a sky. Put the 383 back on the shelf, and put the sky back in the pictures.
