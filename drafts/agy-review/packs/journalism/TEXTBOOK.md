# A reported fact

You can already keep a page. The media pack owns a report, an ad, and a feed. This book is smaller: one fact that someone put in public, with a name on who saw it, and a way to fix it if it was wrong.

The object is that reported fact. Not a mood about the press. Not a tour of famous papers. One sentence a stranger can go and test.

Public general education.

This pack owns sourcing and correction walks. For a report as a page-object, an ad, or a feed, name the media door and stop.

---

## 1. A reported fact

The crane on Dock 4 in Harborton stopped at 14:10 on Tuesday. A dock clerk wrote that sentence on a pad. A neighbor later said the harbor felt cursed. Only one of those two lines sends a stranger to a dock, a clock, and a machine.

Hold them side by side. The crane sentence names a place, a machine, a time, and a state. The curse sentence names a mood. A person can walk to Dock 4 at 14:10 and look at the hook. No walk settles a curse.

People name the first kind of line a **reported fact**. The name arrives after you already have the crane and the fog. A reported fact is one thing a named person is willing to be wrong about, put where other people can read it.

A reported fact can fail. The hook is moving at 14:10. The clerk wrote the wrong dock. Failure is not a scandal yet. Failure is how the next sentence earns its keep. A fog cannot fail, because it never stood.

A reported fact is not the event. The crane did what the crane did. The pad is later. Two pads can disagree about the minute and the crane still sat. Chapter 6 treats the fix. This chapter only asks you to see the stand.

The media pack already taught you to see a report as a page in a pile with ads and feeds. Stay here when the job is the stand inside the page: one testable line, not the layout around it.

Study taught you to bring a question to a page. The question for this object is: what event is this line claiming, and what would make the line false. If you cannot name both, you have a mood with a dateline.

**Check.** Write two lines about a ferry that missed the 16:20 slot at Quay Street. One names the boat, the dock, and the slot. One says the waterfront feels unreliable. Hand both to a person who has not seen the harbor. Only the first line tells that person what to go and see.

New case: a library clock. "The reading-room clock on Elm stopped at 11:05" is a reported fact. "Time in this town means nothing" is a fog. Look at the clock. If the hands sit at 11:20, the first line is false and useful. The second line has nowhere to land.

## 2. Who saw it

Lena stood on the catwalk of Dock 4 and watched the crane's hook hang still. Samir watched the same hook from the glass booth twenty meters inland. A rider on the 14:05 ferry heard someone shout that the crane was down. Three mouths. They are not the same kind of mouth.

Lena had a line of sight. Samir had a line of sight from a different angle. The rider had a shout. If you print the shout as if it were Lena's eyes, you have moved the body that did the seeing.

The person whose senses, or whose signed paper, the line rests on is a **source**. People also say **attribution** for the short tag that names that person in the sentence: Lena, a crane operator on Dock 4, said the hook hung still at 14:10. The tag is not decoration. It is the address of the eyes.

A source can be a person. A source can be a page with a stamp, which chapter 8 treats. A source is not a feeling that "people are saying." If you cannot point at who saw it, you do not yet have a source. You have weather.

Distance matters. Lena was on the catwalk. The rider was on the water. "Close" is not a number. Twenty meters is. Write the distance you have. If you do not have one, write that you do not. Empty is legal. Filling the empty chair with a guess is how a shout becomes an eyewitness.

A named job is not a substitute for eyes. "A harbor official" who was in a meeting inland at 14:10 did not see the hook. The job can get you a record. It cannot get you the catwalk. Ask: were you there, or did someone tell you.

The Library of Congress newspapers door in `LINK_INDEX.md` is the shelf for historic US papers. A count of titles, issues, or digitized pages from this paragraph is unverified. Open that door when you need to see how a paper in another year tagged who spoke. Do not invent a byline style from memory and call it law.

**Check.** Write Lena's line with a tag that names her job and where she stood. Write the rider's line with a tag that names the shout, not the hook. If both tags read as if the speaker saw the hook, you have merged the mouths. Pull them apart until a stranger can tell which body was on the catwalk.

New case: a fire alarm in the warehouse on Quay Street. Three people: a night guard who pulled the handle, a baker two blocks away who heard the bell, and a cousin who heard the baker. Write three tags. Only one of those three may be allowed to say "I pulled the handle." If your baker tag says that, the baker has borrowed the guard's hands.

## 3. Two sources

Lena saw the hook hang. Samir saw the same hook from the booth. Their lunches were separate. Their clocks were separate. If Lena is wrong about the minute, Samir can still be right, or also wrong, on his own clock. That is two sources.

Now sit three people at the same table at 13:00. Tess says the crane will fail. Omar repeats Tess. Nia repeats Omar. At 14:30 all three tell a reporter the crane failed. You have three mouths and one source. The extra two are copies.

People name the first picture **independent sources**. The name is a handle for two lines of sight that do not share a lunch. Two is not a magic charm. Two copies of Tess are still Tess. The test is whether one can fail without taking the other with it.

A second source can be a paper, not a person. Samir's bound log, stamped, is a second line if Lena never touched the book. If Lena dictated the line and Samir only copied it, the book is Lena again. Ask who put the ink down.

Disagreement is information. Lena says 14:10. Samir's log says 14:40. You do not average 14:10 and 14:40 into 14:25 and print the average as the fact. You print both, with tags, and you go to the next door. Chapter 6 is the fix if one of them later loses. Chapter 10 is two official doors on the same public sitting. The move is the same: do not blend.

"Several residents" is not two sources. Name two, or write that you have a chorus and stop. A chorus can still be true. It cannot do the work of two lines of sight.

**Check.** On paper, draw two columns. Column A: Lena on the catwalk, Samir in the booth, separate clocks. Column B: Tess, Omar, Nia, one lunch. Mark which column can survive Lena being wrong about the minute. If you marked B, you counted mouths instead of lines of sight.

New case: a closed clinic on Elm. A posted sign in the glass says closed Tuesday. A recorded message on the clinic phone says closed Tuesday. A neighbor on the stoop says the nurse told her. The sign and the message can be two doors if different people recorded them. The neighbor is a third mouth on the nurse. Write which two you would print, and which one you would tag as a retell.

## 4. A quote

Lena said, "The motor cut at 14:10. I was on the catwalk." Those are her words, in the order she said them. A reporter who writes "Lena felt the machine die around mid-afternoon from a high perch" has written a different sentence. The motor, the time, and the catwalk have been swapped for a feeling, a fog of hours, and a perch.

Marks around a person's words, kept in that person's order, make a **quote**. A sentence that keeps the sense and drops the marks is a **paraphrase**. Both can be honest. They are not the same object. A quote can be checked against a recording or a notebook. A paraphrase can be checked against the sense, not the music.

Shortening is legal if you do not change the stand. "The motor cut at 14:10. I was on the catwalk. The wind was from the east." If the east wind is not the stand, you may drop it. If you drop 14:10 and keep the wind, you have changed the stand and kept the weather.

Do not fix a quote into the sentence you wish you had. Lena said "cut." If you print "failed catastrophically," you have put your adjective in her mouth. Put your adjective outside the marks, and tag it as yours, or cut it.

A quote still needs a source tag. "The motor cut at 14:10" with no name is a floating sentence. "Lena, a crane operator on Dock 4, said, 'The motor cut at 14:10'" has a mouth and a place. Chapter 2 already asked for the mouth. This chapter asks you not to dress the mouth in your clothes.

C-SPAN is the door in `LINK_INDEX.md` for public proceedings on video. The live front lists National Politics, History, and Nonfiction Books as its three shelves, and it lists America's Book Club on Sundays at 18:00 Eastern and Pacific. A quote from a hearing must be checked against the video when the video exists. A wording you remember from a clip you cannot point at is unverified. Open the door. Do not invent a timestamp from this paragraph.

**Check.** Copy Lena's two sentences inside marks. Then write a paraphrase that keeps the motor, 14:10, and the catwalk, and drops the marks. Then write a third sentence that swaps 14:10 for "around mid-afternoon." Circle the third. That circle is the stand you broke. If you cannot see the break, you are not watching the working words.

New case: a council member on the hall steps says, "I will vote no on the spillway." Print that inside marks. A paraphrase that says "the member voiced concerns about the project" has dropped the no. Put the no back, or do not paraphrase.

## 5. A headline

The Quay Ledger printed seven words above Lena's story: CRANE DEATHS ROCK HARBORTON. The body under those words said the hook hung still, no one was hurt, and the dock closed for two hours. A rider who only read the seven words went home and told a cousin that people had died. The cousin now holds a fact the body never stood on.

The short line above a story, meant to be read first, is a **headline**. Its job is to name the stand so a stranger can decide whether to read the next sentence. Its failure mode is to sell a larger stand than the body earned.

Count the work. "Crane on Dock 4 stopped at 14:10; no injuries" names the machine, the dock, the time, and the empty injury chair. "CRANE DEATHS ROCK HARBORTON" names deaths the body does not have. Seven words can lie while two hundred under them tell the truth. Readers often keep the seven.

A headline can be too small. "Harbor news" above Lena's story hides the crane. A headline can be too large. "All Harborton cranes fail" above one crane on one dock is a new claim. The body counted one hook.

Tone is a working part. ROCK is doing work. It turns a stopped motor into a blow. If the body is a two-hour close with no injuries, ROCK is an adjective in a costume. Cut it, or move it into a column that is allowed to argue.

The media pack owns the page as a mix of news, ads, and feeds. Name that door for the mix. Here the object is the one line that can outrun the body.

**Check.** Write two headlines for the same body: hook still at 14:10, no injuries, dock closed two hours. One must be true of those three facts. One must add a death or a town-wide failure the body does not have. Hand both, without the body, to a person who has not read the story. Ask what happened. If both answers match the body, your false headline did not do enough damage, and you have not seen the object yet. Rewrite the false one until the answers split.

New case: the Elm clinic. Body: closed Tuesday, Oak open 09:00 to 17:00, cards still work. Headline A: "Clinic closed Tuesday; Oak open 09:00-17:00." Headline B: "Town health system collapses." Write what a parent at the door does after each. If both parents still go to Oak, Headline B did not change the act, and you wrote a mood that no one used.

## 6. A correction

Wednesday's paper still said 14:10. Thursday's harbor log, stamped and signed, said 14:40. The reporter had Lena's notebook and had not yet seen the log. Friday's paper prints a short box: "An earlier account said the Dock 4 crane stopped at 14:10 Tuesday. The harbor log, line 18, says 14:40. The time in Wednesday's account was wrong."

That box is a **correction**. People also say **retraction** when the whole stand comes down, not one chair. The picture is the same: you already spoke in public, the stand failed a check, and you speak again in public, in the same place or a place as easy to find, naming what was wrong and what you now stand on.

A correction that hides is not a correction. A line in 6-point type on page 6, when the error ran as seven words on page 1, does not meet the reader where the error met them. Put the fix where the false line lived, or say in the box where the false line lived.

Do not spend the box on your feelings. "We regret any confusion" does not move 14:10 to 14:40. The working words are the old number, the new number, and the door that beat the old number. Confusion is a mood. 14:40 is a time.

A correction is not a smear of the source. Lena can still have been on the catwalk. Her watch can still have been wrong. Print that, if you have it. Do not print "Lena lied" unless you have a door for a lie. A wrong minute is a wrong minute.

If you never check, you will never correct. That is not purity. That is a paper that cannot fail because it never stood. Chapter 1 already said failure is how the next sentence earns its keep. This chapter is the next sentence.

**Check.** Write the Friday box for the 14:10 / 14:40 miss in two versions. Version A names Wednesday, 14:10, 14:40, and line 18 of the log. Version B says "We regret any confusion about harbor times." Give both to a person who kept Wednesday's paper. Ask what time they should now write in the margin. Only A should produce 14:40. If B produces 14:40, you smuggled the number in and the regret sentence was not the object.

New case: the Elm clinic. Wednesday's headline said the clinic was closed all week. Thursday a clerk shows a sheet: closed Tuesday only, open Wednesday through Saturday 09:00 to 17:00. Write the box. If your box says "hours may vary," you replaced a false stand with a fog. Put Tuesday, and the five open days, in the box.

## 7. Who paid

The shipyard on Quay Street buys a quarter page in The Quay Ledger every Friday. The quarter page shows a dry dock and a phone number. On the same Friday, the news column on page 1 is about the Dock 4 crane, which the shipyard owns. A reader who cannot see that the shipyard paid for the quarter page, and that the shipyard owns the crane, cannot test whether the money sat next to the stand.

Four chairs sit around a reported fact. Who owns the paper. Who pays the reporter. Who paid for space next to the story. Who is named in the story. Empty chairs are legal. Write them empty. Filling them with "everyone knows they are honest" is a fog.

People name the money around a story **funding**, and they name a clash among those chairs a **conflict of interest**. The names are handles for chairs you can already see. A conflict is not a proof that the stand is false. It is a reason to go and get a second door. Chapter 3 already asked for two sources. Money is one more reason to ask.

An ad is a different object. The media pack owns the ad, the report, and the feed as page-objects. Name that door for what an ad is. Stay here for a narrower walk: can the quarter page, or the owner, or the wage, change which minute you print.

A paper that will not name who bought the quarter page is hiding a chair. A paper that prints "sponsored" above a block the shipyard wrote is showing the chair. Showing is the work. You still check the crane.

Subscriber money is also money. If 400 households pay, those households are a chair. They can want a hero crane or a villain shipyard. Wanting is not a source. Lena's eyes are a source.

The FCC front door in `LINK_INDEX.md` is the United States host for broadcast and communications rules. That live front lists headquarters at 45 L Street NE, Washington, DC 20554, and it lists a Chairman, Brendan Carr, with Commissioners Anna M. Gomez and Olivia Trusty. A count of all commission seats from this paragraph is unverified. Open the door when a radio or television license, a comment filing, or a commission document must travel. Who paid for a newspaper column is not on that front. Do not pretend a broadcast rule answers a print chair.

**Check.** Draw four chairs for Friday's Ledger: owner, reporter wage, shipyard quarter page, crane on Dock 4. Mark which chairs the shipyard sits in. If you marked only the quarter page, you missed the crane. If you marked all four, you put the shipyard in the reporter's wage without a door. Write what you actually have.

New case: a radio hour about the Elm clinic, paid for by the clinic's fundraising desk, with no tag on air. Write the missing tag in one sentence a listener could hear. Then write the same hour with the tag. The facts about Tuesday may be identical. The listener's next check is not.

## 8. A public record

Samir keeps a bound book. Each line has a time, a dock number, a short act, and his initials. Line 18, Tuesday, reads: 14:40, Dock 4, crane motor cut, S.M. The book sits on a shelf the harbor master will copy for a stranger who asks. That shelf is doing a different job from Tess at the diner.

A page a public desk must keep, and must show under a rule, is a **public record**. A diner story is not. A leaked photo of the page can still be a copy of a record, or it can be a crop. Ask to see the page, the stamp, and the desk that claims it.

Federal records in the United States live at the National Archives. The front door names the host as the U.S. National Archives and Records Administration and lists the phone 1-866-272-6272. It also lists a Center for Legislative Archives, an Office of the Federal Register, presidential libraries, and a page called National Archives by the Numbers. How many boxes sit in a vault is not on that front. Any holdings count from this paragraph is unverified. Open the numbers row in `LINK_INDEX.md` when a count must travel.

A bill, a vote, a hearing transcript: Congress.gov is the door for the living federal legislature. A bill number or a vote count from this paragraph is unverified. GovInfo is the door for official US publications. FOIA.gov is the door to ask a federal desk for a record it has not already posted. Those three are finder's doors and filing doors. They are not Samir's book. Pick the desk that owns the page you need.

A public record can be wrong. Samir can write 14:40 when the clock on the wall said 14:10. The stamp does not make the minute true. It makes the page fetchable. You still need chapter 3 if two records disagree. You still need chapter 6 if you already printed the wrong one.

The FCC's public notices are records of that commission. On 16 September 2026 the front listed a notice that the FCC deactivated disaster reporting and a tool it named MDRI for Hurricane Lowell. That line is a document on that door. A diner claim that "they shut all the radios" is not that line. Copy the notice, or do not upgrade the diner.

**Check.** Write what line 18 can prove, and what it cannot. It can prove that Samir wrote 14:40, Dock 4, motor cut. It cannot prove Lena's watch. Then write what a diner sentence can prove: that Tess spoke. If your two lists match, you have not split the desk from the diner.

New case: a military service record. The Archives front lists "Get My Military Record" as a task. Do not invent a form number from this chapter. Open that door. If the door will not open, write unverified and stop. A cousin's memory of a discharge year is not the record.

## 9. A document vs a rumor

Two papers sit on the same table. One is line 18, initials, stamp. One is a napkin that begins "everyone at the diner says the crane was sabotaged." Both are marks on a surface. Only one has a desk, a rule, and a way for a stranger to fetch the same marks tomorrow.

A **document** is a page you can point at, with a maker, a date, and a home. A **rumor** is a claim that travels without that home. The napkin can be honest. Honesty does not make it fetchable. Tomorrow the napkin is in the bin and "everyone" has no address.

The test is not dignity. A stamped page can be a lie. A rumor can later match a stamped page. The test is: can a stranger get the same page without knowing Tess. If the answer is no, you have a rumor. Tag it as a rumor, or do not print it as a fact.

"They" is a rumor costume. They shut the radios. They hid the log. Name the desk, or write that you cannot. "Officials" is a thinner costume. Samir is a name. The harbor master's office is a desk. "Officials" is a fog in a lanyard.

A video can be a document. C-SPAN's front listed a House sitting as Event 447285, 21 September 2026, 10:30am Eastern, on C-SPAN. That row is a page you can open. A cousin who says "I heard they screamed in the House" is a rumor until the clip is on the table. Play the clip. Write who spoke. If the clip will not play, write unverified. Do not fill the silence with the cousin.

A document can quote a rumor. Line 18 does not. A police blotter that says "caller reported sabotage" is a document about a call, not a document about sabotage. Keep the chairs. The call happened. The sabotage is still empty.

**Check.** Put the napkin and line 18 on the table. Write one sentence each that a stranger could still test on Friday. Line 18's sentence should send the stranger to the harbor master's shelf. The napkin's sentence should send the stranger to Tess, if you still have Tess. If both sentences send the stranger to "the harbor," you have merged the objects.

New case: Hurricane Lowell. The FCC notice dated 16 September 2026 is a document about disaster reporting and a tool named MDRI. A social post that says "the government turned off weather" is a rumor that used that notice as fuel. Write both. If your rumor sentence contains MDRI and the date, you have quietly turned the post into the notice. Take those words out of the post and leave them on the FCC line.

## 10. A check: two doors on the same event

Open two doors on one sitting of a public body, and write what each door can prove. Do not blend them.

Door one: C-SPAN. The live front listed a US House sitting as Event 447285, dated 21 September 2026, at 10:30am Eastern, labeled C-SPAN. That row can prove that this host scheduled a House sitting at that clock time under that event number. If the video is there, the video can prove who was in the frame and what words were said, the way chapter 4 asked you to check a quote. If the video is not there, the row is a listing, not a transcript.

Door two: Congress.gov. That door is the legislature's own shelf for bills, votes, and texts. A bill number, a vote count, or a spoken line from this paragraph is unverified. Open it for the same date. Write what it shows for 21 September 2026. If it shows a measure the video never names, you have two different objects. If it names a measure the video shows, you have two doors on one sitting.

If the two doors disagree, do not average them. Print both, with tags. The move is chapter 3, done with hosts that own the fact instead of with Lena and Samir. The Archives, the FCC, Congress.gov, C-SPAN, and the Library of Congress newspapers door are not one pile. Each owns a different page.

A second sitting, for practice, is not the House. The FCC front listed a September 2026 Open Commission Meeting on 30 September 2026, in the Commission Meeting Room at FCC Headquarters, 45 L Street NE, Washington, DC 20554. A social post that says the meeting is "sometime next week in Virginia" is a different claim. The address on the FCC door is the District of Columbia line, not Virginia. The date is 30 September 2026, not a fog of next week. Write both. Circle the door that owns the room.

C-SPAN also listed the 81st session of the United Nations General Assembly as 22 to 28 September, and a first-day block of world leaders on 22 September 2026 at 09:00 Eastern. Those numbers are from that front. A diner claim that "the UN meets in Harborton next week" is a rumor. Do not move the 09:00 block to Quay Street because the town wants a show.

The Library of Congress newspapers door remains the shelf for a historic paper you can name. The Archives front remains the shelf for a federal record you can fetch, with 1-866-272-6272 on the page as the host's phone. Neither door, on the fronts used for this book, yielded a count of holdings or of digitized issues this chapter will stand on. Unverified. Fetch again when the count must travel.

You now have the object: a reported fact with a mouth, a second line of sight, marks around other people's words, a headline that does not outrun the body, a correction that moves the number, chairs for money, a public page, and a split between a document and a rumor. The last check is two official doors on the same event. Walk them. Write what each can prove. Stop.

**Check.** Open the C-SPAN row for Event 447285 and the Congress.gov door for 21 September 2026. On paper, two columns. Column C-SPAN: event number, 10:30am Eastern, House, whether a video plays. Column Congress: whatever bill, vote, or calendar line the live page shows, or the word unverified if it will not show. If your two columns are identical words, you copied one door onto the other. Pull them apart until a stranger can see which host owns which line.

New case: the FCC meeting on 30 September 2026 at 45 L Street NE. Write the door's date and street. Write a rumor that moves the meeting to Virginia and drops the day. Then write one sentence that would correct the rumor in public, using chapter 6's box: old place, new place, named door. If your box says "please ignore online chatter," you spent the box on a mood. Put the street in.
