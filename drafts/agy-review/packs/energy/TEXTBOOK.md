# Work, heat, fuel, and a grid

You can already add, take away, multiply, share, name a percent, and keep a unit on a number. This book is the next act: a crate that moves, a pan that warms, a can that stores a burn, a town's wires that share a path. People also call that whole family **energy**. The family name can wait until you have seen the members.

The living SI names for the joule and the watt, and the sentences that fix those units, live in the measurement pack and on NIST and BIPM. This book names that door and stops. The walks here are a kilowatt-hour on a meter, a fuel can you can count, and a grid you can draw.

Four official pages were opened for this sitting on 20 September 2026: the U.S. Energy Information Administration at eia.gov, the U.S. Department of Energy at energy.gov, the National Institute of Standards and Technology at nist.gov, and the National Renewable Energy Laboratory at nrel.gov. EIA, DOE, and NIST returned. NREL's page did not open. The host name did not resolve. A number that did not appear on the three pages that returned is marked unverified, or it is a worked case invented for the arithmetic. Paper first.

EasyLM. Public general education.

## 1. Work

A crate sits on the mill floor. Nia pushes it 8 metres along a painted line. The crate starts on a chalk mark and ends 8 metres away. Her shoulders feel the push. The floor now holds the crate in a new place.

She leans on a second crate. That crate does not move. Her shoulders still feel a push. The chalk mark under the second crate is the same mark. The tiredness is in Nia. The second crate did not change place.

When a push, a pull, or a lift actually moves a thing through a distance, that delivered change is **work**. The everyday word "work" covers the lean and the move. This chapter uses the move. No move, no work on that crate.

A longer path with the same push is more work. 8 metres, then another 7 metres on the same push: $8 + 7 = 15$ metres of path, so 15 of those path-units of work on the crate if the push stayed the same. A harder push along the same 8 metres is more work than a gentler push along those 8 metres. Two equal crates, each moved 8 metres with the same push, are twice one crate.

Lift is the same object in the vertical. Twelve sacks, each lifted from the floor onto the same shelf. If one sack through that one lift is the yard's unit for this afternoon, twelve sacks are 12 units. Two people split the pile evenly: 6 units each. The shelf did not care who lifted. The count is the lifts that happened.

Hold a 20 kilogram bag still for 3 minutes. The bag does not rise and does not travel. Work on the bag from that hold is none. Arms still spend something. That spend is in the body, as warmth you can feel, which is heat in the next chapter, not a move of the bag.

The SI unit of this delivered pile is the joule. How the joule is fixed lives in the measurement pack and on the NIST SI door. Unverified here. Do not finish that sentence from memory. Bills in later chapters count a different writing of the same family, the kilowatt-hour, because that is the mark the meter writes.

**Check.** Five sacks, two trips each from floor to shelf. $5 \times 2 = 10$ lifts. If each lift is one yard-unit, the work on the sacks is 10 of those units. A new case: a pallet jack travels 6 metres, rests, then travels 9 metres more with the same push. Path $6 + 9 = 15$ metres. Another: you stand still holding a toolbox for 4 minutes. The toolbox did not move. Work on the toolbox from the hold is none. If you wrote 4 minutes of work on the box, you counted tiredness as a move.

## 2. Heat

A pan of stew sits on a burner. The burner is hotter than the stew. You wait. The stew is hotter than it was. Nothing in the pan had to roll across the floor. The change arrived because one thing was hotter than the other.

Energy on the move because one body is hotter than another is **heat**. The pan, the stew, and the air above the pan can each hold a pile. Heat is the crossing, not the pile sitting still, and not the number on the wall thermometer.

The thermometer is a temperature reading. Temperature is a different object. Measurement owns that stick. Name that door and stop. A room at 21 and a room at 18, both on the same scale, differ by 3 degrees of that scale: $21 - 18 = 3$. That 3 is not a count of heat. It is a gap on a mark. Do not add a temperature to a kilowatt-hour. The two counts do not share a stick.

A steady flame under three equal pots of the same stew. One pot sits 4 minutes, one sits 8 minutes, one sits 12 minutes. The 8 minute pot received twice the time of the 4 minute pot. If the flame held still, it received twice the heat. The 12 minute pot received three times the 4 minute pot. The thermometer on the stew does not have to read three times as high. Heat added is not a percent of the temperature number.

A thermos slows the crossing. The stew inside stays hot longer because less heat leaves. The thermos did not rename the crossing. It throttled it. An open window on a cold night is the opposite throttle: the room's pile crosses to the street faster.

Heat moves from hotter to less hot on its own. A red burner warms a cool pan. A cool pan does not, on its own, make a red burner. You can pump heat the other way only with a machine that spends something else, a refrigerator. That spend is work, or a plug, not a free climb.

**Check.** Two pans, same stew, same flame. One for 6 minutes, one for 18 minutes. The second received three times the time, so three times the heat if the flame held. A new case: a radiator at 70 on its scale, a room at 19 on that same scale. Heat crosses from radiator to room. The gap is $70 - 19 = 51$ degrees of that scale. That 51 is still not a joule count. Another: a freezer at $-18$ and a kitchen at 22, both Celsius on the same dial. Heat crosses into the kitchen from the warm room toward the cold box unless the machine spends to pump it out. If you added $-18$ to 22 and called 4 a heat total, you added two temperatures. Stop.

## 3. Fuel

A can on the shed floor holds lamp oil. Oren pours a little into a lamp, lights the wick, and reads. Light and warmth come out of the lamp. The can is lighter by the pour. The oil was a store. Burning released it.

A store you can release, by burning or by a like reaction, is **fuel**. Wood in a stove. Gas in a bottle. Coal in a bunker. Petrol in a tank. The can, the bottle, the bunker, and the tank are the objects you can count before anything burns.

EIA's homepage, fetched for this sitting, groups the country's tracked stores under petroleum and other liquids, natural gas, coal, nuclear and uranium, and renewable and alternative fuels. Electricity has its own heading on that page. Electricity is how a plant's store is sent down a wire, not a second can on the shed floor. Nuclear sits with uranium on that list: a different kind of store from lamp oil, still a store EIA counts.

Volume is not the energy in the volume. Eighteen litres in the can. A lamp uses $0.6$ litres on a long evening. $18 \div 0.6 = 30$, so 30 of those evenings if nothing spills. After 11 evenings: $11 \times 0.6 = 6.6$ litres used, and $18 - 6.6 = 11.4$ litres remain. That arithmetic counts litres. How many kilowatt-hours hide in a litre of that oil is a door number. EIA's Units and Calculators page is that door. Unverified here. Do not finish a litres-to-kilowatt-hours factor from memory.

NIST's homepage, fetched for this sitting, points to a page named "How Do You Measure Fuel at the Gas Pump?" The pump sells a measured volume. The living rules for that volume live on that NIST page and with the Office of Weights and Measures. The page itself was not opened in this sitting, so pump tolerances are unverified here. A litre on the pump is still a litre, not a joule.

On 18 September 2026 EIA's Today in Energy asked what goes into diesel prices. The page said the price of distillate fuel oil, often sold as diesel, is driven by the price of crude oil, retail margins, distribution costs, taxes, and crack spreads, the indicator EIA uses for refining margins. Tight global supplies of distillate and elevated crude prices had driven prices higher in recent months, on that page. No dollar-per-gallon figure from the chart is printed here. The chart ran weekly average U.S. retail diesel prices from 1 January 2007 through 14 September 2026. Fetch the Gasoline and Diesel Fuel Update when a price must travel.

**Check.** A bottle of 14 litres. A heater uses $0.7$ litres a night. $14 \div 0.7 = 20$ nights. After 8 nights: $8 \times 0.7 = 5.6$ litres used, $8.4$ litres remain. A new case: two cans, 9 litres and 5 litres, poured into one tank. $9 + 5 = 14$ litres. Another: a pump receipt for 32 litres, and a claim that 32 litres is 32 kilowatt-hours. Stop. Volume is not the billed pile. Open EIA Units and Calculators for a factor, or write unverified. If you treated 32 litres as 32 of anything on the electric meter, you mixed two sticks.

## 4. A wire

A lamp stands on a table. A cord runs from the lamp to a wall. Oren clicks the switch. The lamp lights. The cord did not burn. Nothing poured from a can at the table. Something was delivered along the cord.

A metal path that can carry that delivery from a socket to a lamp, a kettle, or a motor is a **wire**. The wire is not the pile. It is the path. A dry road is not the grain. It is how the grain reaches town.

Three equal lamps on one cord. Each lamp the same. The cord now feeds three. Unscrew one lamp: the cord feeds two. $3 - 1 = 2$. Four lamps, then two unscrewed: $4 - 2 = 2$ remain. The path did not change length. The load on the path did.

A cord that feeds a heavy load can warm. That warmth is leftover energy in the wire, the waste heat of chapter 8, not light you wanted. A thin extension under a heater is a path asked to carry more than it can stay cool doing. Warm is a warning. Smoke is a stop.

A fuse, or a breaker, is a gate on the path. Too much on the wire, the gate opens, the path dies, the lamps go dark. The gate is not the fuel. It is how the house refuses a fire.

What moves in the metal has a school name, electric current, and an SI unit, the ampere. Those names live in the measurement pack and on NIST. This chapter does not finish the ampere sentence. You can still count lamps on a cord, and you can still feel a warm plug, without that sentence.

A battery in a torch is a small store at the other end of a short wire. The torch does not need the town. The store still empties. Six cells, each used up after the same number of evenings, last $6$ of those piles. Two torches sharing one cell do not make a second cell.

**Check.** Five equal lamps on one cord. Two unscrewed. $5 - 2 = 3$ lamps still drawing. A new case: one kettle and two lamps on a kitchen socket. Unplug the kettle. Two lamps remain. Another: a warm plug behind a heater on a thin cord. Unplug. The warmth was leftover in the path. Do not cover the plug and keep the heater. If you added the 5 lamps to the 2 unscrewed and wrote 7 still on, you counted the removed load as extra path.

## 5. A grid

Sable street has 40 houses. Each house has wires. The wires join a thicker wire on the pole. The pole wire joins a still thicker run out of town, and that run joins plants that turn fuel, falling water, wind, or sunlight into the thing the wire can carry. The shared network is a **grid**.

One house can keep a can of oil. Forty houses cannot keep the town's evening in a cupboard the way Oren keeps lamp oil. Some stores exist: batteries, pumped water, a tank of gas at a plant. The grid as lived is still mostly this: make the delivery in the same hour it is used. If the plants and the path cannot match the hour, lights fail.

EIA's homepage, fetched for this sitting, lists an Hourly Electric Grid Monitor for the U.S. 48 states. Open that monitor for the hour you care about. This book does not print a megawatt from memory. On 10 September 2026 EIA posted final 2025 Annual Electric Generator Report data. The posting is a date and a product name. The generator table itself was not opened, so no plant count and no fuel mix is printed here.

DOE's homepage, fetched for this sitting, listed grid work in the same week: on 19 September 2026, actions on the Carolinas' grid in hot weather, and a move to keep Indiana coal plants operating to support grid reliability; on 17 September 2026, an action on the Mid-Atlantic grid under anticipated stressed system conditions. Those lines are what the department posted. No megawatt figures from those articles are printed here. The articles were not opened. DOE also lists Power Marketing Administrations among its organizations, and lists EIA itself. The national laboratories sit on that same homepage.

Forty houses, each using $1.1$ kilowatts this hour: $40 \times 1.1 = 44$ kilowatts on the feeder for this hour. Ten of those houses each switch on a 2 kilowatt heater: extra $10 \times 2 = 20$ kilowatts. The feeder now carries $44 + 20 = 64$ kilowatts. The plants and the wires have to match 64, not 44, for that hour. The street did not grow. The use did.

A break in the path is a blackout for everyone downstream of the break. A plant that stops is a missing source. The grid is both. A house that runs a torch on cells is off that shared path for the minutes the torch lasts. The town is still on it.

**Check.** 25 houses at $0.8$ kilowatts: $25 \times 0.8 = 20$ kilowatts. Eight houses add a 1.5 kilowatt kettle at once: $8 \times 1.5 = 12$ kilowatts extra. The feeder sees $20 + 12 = 32$ kilowatts for that minute. A new case: 12 houses at 2 kilowatts is 24 kilowatts. If a line can carry 20 kilowatts and you ask 24, 4 kilowatts have nowhere honest to go. The gate should open, or the voltage sags, or a wire warms. Another: a claim that the U.S. grid mix this hour is a remembered percent. Open EIA's Hourly Electric Grid Monitor. A remembered percent is not the hour.

## 6. Light

Evening on Sable street. Nia clicks a lamp. The room is no longer dark. She can see the table. The lamp also warms her hand if she holds it close. Two products left the wire: light she can see, and heat she can feel.

Energy you can see by is **light**. Sunlight at the window is the same family without the cord. A skylight open for 8 hours used 0 from the wire for that daylight. The room still lit. The sun paid.

A mark on a bulb, 8 watts, 9 watts, 40 watts, is how fast that lamp uses energy while it is on. People name that rate **power**. The SI name for the unit is the watt. How the watt is fixed lives in the measurement pack and on NIST. Unverified here. NIST's homepage, fetched for this sitting, shows the NIST-4 Kibble watt balance as a picture of the national measurement institute at work. The picture is not the defining sentence. Fetch the SI door for the sentence.

A bigger watt mark, same hours, is more energy from the wire. An 8 watt lamp and a 40 watt lamp, both on for the same evening, are not equal draws. $40 \div 8 = 5$, so the 40 watt lamp draws five times the 8 watt lamp in those hours. Five 8 watt lamps together draw the same as one 40 watt lamp: $5 \times 8 = 40$.

Count lamp-hours before you count money. One lamp on for 5 hours is 5 lamp-hours. Three lamps on for 4 hours: $3 \times 4 = 12$ lamp-hours. Two hours of the same three: $3 \times 2 = 6$ lamp-hours, half of 12. The next chapter writes those hours against the watt mark and lands on a kilowatt-hour. This chapter keeps the seen thing: hours of light, and the mark on the glass.

A lamp that makes more of its draw into light, and less into leftover heat, is a better lamp for seeing. An old glowing-wire bulb is a heater that happens to shine. The leftover heat is still energy. It is not the job you bought the bulb for. Chapter 8 names that leftover.

Daylight numbers, how much sun a roof sees, live at NREL among other doors. NREL did not open in this sitting. Solar resource figures are unverified here. The door is still in the index.

**Check.** Four lamps, 3 hours. $4 \times 3 = 12$ lamp-hours. A new case: one 8 watt lamp and one 40 watt lamp, same 2 hours. The 40 watt lamp used five times the 8 watt lamp. Another: a room lit 6 hours by a window, lamp off. Wire energy for that light is 0. If you billed 6 kilowatt-hours for the window, you charged the sun as a socket.

## 7. A bill in units

The meter on Nia's porch is a counter. At the start of the month it shows 19208. At the end it shows 19641. The house used $19641 - 19208 = 433$ of the unit the meter writes.

That unit is a **kilowatt-hour**. A thousand watts running for one hour is one kilowatt-hour. One hundred watts running for ten hours is the same one: $0.1 \times 10 = 1$. Two kilowatts for three hours: $2 \times 3 = 6$ kilowatt-hours. The name is a rate times a time. Kilo is a thousand, from numeracy. An hour is the clock hour you already use.

This pack owns that walk. Write power in kilowatts. Write time in hours. Multiply. The product is kilowatt-hours. A watt mark on a bulb is already a power. 8 watts is $8 \div 1000 = 0.008$ kilowatts. Five hours: $0.008 \times 5 = 0.040$ kilowatt-hours.

A 1.5 kilowatt heater for 6 hours: $1.5 \times 6 = 9$ kilowatt-hours. A 1.8 kilowatt kettle for 10 minutes: 10 minutes is $10 \div 60 = \frac{1}{6}$ hour, and $1.8 \times \frac{1}{6} = 0.30$ kilowatt-hours. A fridge marked 0.12 kilowatts, if it ran the whole 24 hours, would be $0.12 \times 24 = 2.88$ kilowatt-hours. Real fridges cycle off. The 2.88 is an if-it-ran ceiling, invented for the multiply, not a measured fridge.

The bill multiplies the meter count by a price per kilowatt-hour, then may add a connection charge. Invented for this porch, not a national average: $0.17$ dollars per kilowatt-hour, and a $9.40$ connection charge.

| line | number |
|---|---:|
| meter start | 19208 |
| meter end | 19641 |
| kilowatt-hours | 433 |
| energy charge at $0.17 | 73.61 |
| connection | 9.40 |
| total | 83.01 |

$433 \times 0.17$: $400 \times 0.17 = 68.00$, and $33 \times 0.17 = 5.61$, so $68.00 + 5.61 = 73.61$. Then $73.61 + 9.40 = 83.01$. If those lines disagree, a place slid or the connection was added twice.

EIA tracks residential use in the Residential Energy Consumption Survey, listed on the homepage fetched for this sitting. The survey tables were not opened. No U.S. average household kilowatt-hour figure is printed here. $433$ is Nia's invented porch. A price series for electricity lives at EIA. Unverified here as a cents-per-kilowatt-hour number.

If you need the same pile written in joules, open the measurement pack and the NIST SI door. This chapter stays with the meter.

**Check.** Meter 27310 to 27755. $27755 - 27310 = 445$ kilowatt-hours. At $0.16$ dollars: $445 \times 0.16 = 71.20$. Plus $8.50$ connection: $79.70$. A new case: 2.4 kilowatts for 5 hours is 12 kilowatt-hours. Undo: $12 \div 5 = 2.4$ kilowatts. Another: 900 watts is $0.900$ kilowatts, for 2 hours is $1.800$ kilowatt-hours. If you wrote 1800 kilowatt-hours, you forgot that kilo already sat in the watt pile and multiplied by 1000 extra.

## 8. Waste heat

The toaster browns two slices. The kitchen is warmer than it was. The brown is the job. The extra warmth in the room is leftover. Leftover energy, still energy, that missed the job you wanted, is **waste heat**.

It did not vanish. It changed shape. The toast holds some. The slot of the toaster holds some. The air holds some. A hand over the slot feels the leftover.

A lamp that shines and warms is the same split: some light, some leftover heat. A charger that is warm while a phone fills: some into the battery, some into the brick. A motor that runs a fan and is hot at the casing: some into moving air, some into the case. A plant that burns fuel to turn a shaft dumps leftover heat into a river or a tower. The dump is not a moral. It is the part that did not become the delivery on the wire.

You never collect the whole store of a fuel into the one job you named. Some becomes leftover warmth, some becomes sound, some becomes light you did not want. A slogan that says "nothing is wasted" is false if the job was toast and the kitchen got the rest. A slogan that says "the leftover disappeared" is also false. The leftover is in the room.

Toy numbers for the arithmetic, not a measured toaster. 10 units into the slot. 3 units brown the bread. 7 units warm the kitchen. $3 + 7 = 10$. The 7 are waste heat for the toast job. They are still heat for the kitchen. In winter you might have wanted that 7. In summer you pay again to pump them out.

Nia's 9 kilowatt-hour heater sits in the room it is meant to warm. Almost all of those 9 kilowatt-hours become heat in that room, which is the job. The same 9 kilowatt-hours in a toaster would be mostly waste heat for a lighting job, and a success for a heating job. Waste is relative to the job, not a third kind of energy.

Plant efficiency figures, how many units of fuel become units on the wire, live at EIA and at DOE laboratories. Those tables were not opened. No percent is printed here.

**Check.** 12 units in. 5 do the job. Leftover $12 - 5 = 7$. A new case: 9 kilowatt-hours in a room heater that is the job. Leftover against that job is near 0 in the room, if the heat stayed in the room. Open a window and some of the 9 leaves. Another: a warm charger, a full battery, a cooler room than the charger. The warmth in the charger is leftover against the filling job. If you wrote that leftover as "gone," you lost the pile. If you wrote it as a second full battery, you invented a store.

## 9. A machine that turns

A wheel sits in the millrace. Water hits the paddles. The wheel turns. A shaft runs from the wheel into the mill. Stones turn. Grain becomes meal. The water's fall became a turn, and the turn became the job.

A machine that takes a flow, a burn, a wind, or a plug, and makes a shaft spin, is a **machine that turns**. A water wheel. A wind turbine, which is a wheel in air. A steam turbine, which is a wheel driven by hot gas. An electric motor, which is a shaft fed by a wire. A generator is the motor's sibling: a shaft in, a delivery onto a wire out.

Oren's bicycle dynamo is the small version. He pedals. The headlamp lights. For those minutes he is the fuel. Stop pedaling, the lamp dies. The town's plants are many dynamos, some fed by steam from a burn, some by falling water, some by wind, some by sunlight on a different kind of machine that does not need a shaft. This chapter keeps the shaft.

Gears change how many turns. The mill wheel turns 40 times in a minute. After the gears, the generator shaft turns 1200 times in that same minute. $1200 \div 40 = 30$. Thirty turns of the shaft for each turn of the wheel. The pile did not multiply by 30 for free. The shaft is harder to turn. You traded slow-and-strong for fast-and-light, or the other way. Count turns if you want a check. Do not treat 30 as extra energy.

DOE's homepage lists the U.S. Department of Energy National Laboratories as a family of shops that take these machines apart and rebuild them. NREL sits in that family for renewable machines. NREL did not open in this sitting. A capacity factor, a rotor diameter, a peak watt of a named turbine, unverified here.

Eight turns of a hand crank raise a bucket 2 metres. Sixteen turns, same bucket, same 2 metres, would mean the crank was halved in lift per turn, not that the bucket rose 4 metres. Watch the bucket. The job is the rise, not the count of turns for its own sake.

A motor on a wire is the grid reaching a shaft in the house: a fan, a washer, a pump. The meter still counts kilowatt-hours. The shaft is how those hours became a spin.

**Check.** A wheel at 50 turns a minute, a shaft at 1500 turns a minute. $1500 \div 50 = 30$. Same 30 as the mill above, new numbers. A new case: 12 turns raise a bucket 3 metres. 6 turns on a different gear raise the same 3 metres. The second gear lifts more per turn. Another: a dynamo lamp that stays lit after the bicycle stops. That lamp has a store, a cell, or you misread the switch. A spinning machine without a store dies when the spin dies.

## 10. A check against a meter door

A number that must travel to another person, another town, or another year has to come from a host that owns it. Arithmetic you can redo by hand does not need a door. A national total, a fuel energy content, a grid mix, a pump tolerance, an SI definition, or a laboratory rating does.

Walk:

1. Name what moved: a crate, heat across a pan, fuel leaving a can, a delivery along a wire, light in a room, a shaft, leftover warmth.
2. Name the unit on the paper. Kilowatt-hours for the electric meter. Litres or gallons for a pump volume. Volume is not kilowatt-hours until a door gives a factor.
3. Redo the arithmetic: end take away start, rate times hours, price times kilowatt-hours.
4. If the number is a definition, a national series, a mix, or a factor, open the door.
5. If the door and a remembered line disagree, believe the door. If the door will not open, write unverified and stop building on it.
