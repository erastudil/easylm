# Ten minutes with a body

You can already read. This book is the next ten minutes with a body that is hurt, stopped, or poisoned, before a trained crew arrives.

The object is those ten minutes. One body. One room. One set of hands that have not yet been to a class this week. How a lung fills, how a clot forms, how a bone knits over weeks: those objects live in the health pack. Name that pack and stop.

This pack owns the first-aid door walks. A press count, a bleed step with a minute on it, a poison number: those come from a living card, or the line says unverified. A ratio remembered from a poster stays off this page.

EasyLM is public general education.

---

## 1. Stop and look

Someone is on the kitchen floor. Your feet already want the next step. Hold them.

Look at the room first. Steam over a pot. A knife still on the board. Water on tile. A cord in the puddle. A dog that will bite a stranger who kneels. A car in a garage with the motor on. If you become the second body on that floor, no one is looking.

Count what can hurt you. Count the bodies. Then you may touch.

People name that first act **scene safety**. The name is a handle for the look you already did. The look is the work. The name is how a class writes it on a card.

After the room, look at the body. Eyes open or shut. Chest moving or still. Blood on the floor or not. Talking or silent. You are collecting three facts you can say into a phone: where, how many, what you see.

A second person in the doorway is a tool. Send them to the door. Keep your own eyes on the body and the room. If you are alone, the look still comes first. Ten seconds of looking is not cruelty. It is how you stay a helper.

A wet bathroom with a dryer in a puddle is a different room from a dry hallway. In the hallway you may kneel. In the bathroom you stay on dry floor until the cord is dead. Unplug from a dry place, or wait for a crew that can. Touching a live cord to be brave makes two patients.

**Check.** A garage. A mower still running. A person down beside it. Write the order of three acts: stop the mower, look at the floor for fuel and blades, then go to the person. If you wrote "run to the person" as act one, you skipped the room.

New case: a sidewalk after rain, a person at the bottom of 12 stone steps, a bicycle in the path. Move the bicycle off the path so the next rider does not add a second body. Then look at the person. If you cannot move the bicycle without stepping on the person, leave it and stand where you can warn the next rider. The object is still the look.

## 2. Breathing

A person on a park bench. Head tipped. You watch the chest. You put your ear near the mouth. You feel for air on your cheek.

Air in and out, chest rising, is **breathing**. No air, chest still, the pump that moves blood may have stopped too. The health pack owns how that pump is built. This chapter owns the next minutes: is air moving, or is it not.

If the chest rises while you watch, air is moving. Stay. Keep looking. Talk to them. If they answer, they are breathing well enough to talk. If they do not answer and the chest still rises, they are breathing and not awake. That pair of facts goes into the call in chapter 9.

If the chest does not rise, you need the living CPR card. This sitting opened the American Heart Association CPR door at `cpr.heart.org`. That page names **CPR** as the emergency work done when the heart has stopped beating. It says CPR done at once can double or triple a cardiac arrest victim's chance of survival. It offers Hands-Only CPR as two steps and points to a video. The fetched page did not print those two steps, and it did not print a press count, a depth, or a ratio of presses to breaths. Those figures are unverified here. Open that door. A remembered 30-and-2 stays off this page.

The same host prints Heartsaver courses for people with little or no medical training, and it points to Guidelines for CPR and ECC and to 2024 First Aid Guidelines. This sitting did not open those inner guideline pages. Treat every count on those pages as unverified until you fetch them.

A class with a manikin is how a press becomes a skill. This book is the map to that class, not a substitute count.

**Check.** Same park bench. You watch long enough to see the chest rise 3 times. Write: breathing, yes. Stay. Call if they will not wake.

New case: the chest is still for the whole time you watch. Write: breathing, no. Call. Then open the living CPR card before you press. Do not invent a press count from a poster. If the AHA door will not open, write unverified next to any count you were about to use, and still call.

A third case: 2 people on a blanket, 1 chest moving, 1 chest still. You now have two facts. The still chest is the first body for CPR once the card is in your hands. The moving chest still needs a watcher.

## 3. Bleeding

A cutting board. A knife. A forearm with a hole. Blood is leaving the body through that hole.

The work of the next minutes is to keep the blood in. Take a clean cloth. Put it on the hole. Press with a flat hand. If the cloth soaks, put another cloth on top. Do not take the first cloth off to look. Looking opens the hole again.

People name that work **bleeding control**. The name arrives after the cloth and the press. How many minutes to press, when a tight band above a limb is the next step, how far above a wound that band sits: those are load-bearing steps. This sitting did not fetch a bleed card with those figures. Unverified here. Open the Red Cross first-aid door, the AHA first-aid guidelines door, or the class card in your hand. A remembered minute count stays off this page.

This sitting opened the Red Cross first-aid class door and the host refused the page. Treat every Red Cross count as unverified until that door opens for you.

Blood on the floor is a fact about the room. Wipe it later. The hole is the object now. If the person can press their own hole, let them. Your hands are then free to call.

A bleed from the nose while the person is sitting and talking is not the same job as a bleed from a thigh that will not stop. Both get a cloth. The thigh that will not stop also gets a call. If bone is in view, still press around what you can press. Do not push a bone back in. That last act belongs to a crew.

**Check.** A kitchen knife, a finger, one clean dish towel. Press on the finger, not on the wrist. The hole is on the finger.

New case: a bike crash on Oak, a shin with a hole, a cotton shirt. Press on the shin. If the shirt soaks, add a second cloth. Write whether you lifted the first shirt to check. If you did, you opened the hole. A third case: 2 holes, palm and scalp. You have 2 hands. Press the larger stream first, then get a second person or a second cloth on the other hole. If you press both weakly, both still run.

## 4. A break

A skateboard. A wrist that now bends where a wrist does not bend. The person will not take weight on it. The line of the arm is wrong.

Leave it still. Support it as it lies, with a folded jacket, a book bag, the person's other hand. Do not pull it straight to see if it "goes back." Do not ask them to lift a pan to test it. The ten minutes are still and supported.

People name a bone that has lost its line a **fracture**. The name is a handle. You cannot see the bone from the sidewalk. You can see the line, the swelling, the refusal to use the part. Treat what you see. A class card may add a sling, a splint, a way to pad. This sitting did not fetch those steps. Unverified here.

If the skin is open over the wrong line, you now have a hole and a break. Cloth on the hole. Do not push bone. Still do not straighten.

A joint that looks wrong can be a bone or the joint itself. You do not have to name which. Still and supported is the same ten minutes for both. The crew names the film and the bone.

Shoes and rings can become traps as a part swells. If a ring is already tight and you cannot slide it, leave it. Forcing a ring over a wrong line is a second injury. Note the ring for the crew.

**Check.** An ankle after a curb. The person will not stand on it. Sit them. Support the ankle as it lies. Write "still" as the act. If you wrote "walk it off," you used the break as a test.

New case: a finger bent sideways after a door. Do not yank. Pad. Call if the line stays wrong or the skin is open. A third case: 8 stairs, a person sitting on the third stair, a knee that will not bend. Do not carry them down the remaining 5 stairs to "get them comfortable." Bring the jacket to them. The stairs are part of the room you already looked at.

## 5. Burn

A pan. A forearm. Skin that is angry, hot, and changing color. The heat is still in the skin.

Move the person off the stove. Dead the flame or the element if you can do it without becoming a second burn. Then cool the skin with water. Ordinary cool tap water is the first act you can see.

How many minutes of water, how cold the water may be, whether ice is allowed, what cream or butter to refuse: those are load-bearing steps. This sitting did not fetch a burn card with those figures. Unverified here. Open the first-aid doors in `LINK_INDEX.md`. A remembered minute count, and a remembered kitchen myth about butter, stay off this page.

People name damaged skin from heat, chemical, or current a **burn**. Kitchen heat, a chemical splash, and a live cord are three different rooms. Kitchen heat gets cool water and a call if the patch is large, on the face, on the hands, or on a child. A chemical splash keeps the bottle and walks to the poison door in chapter 7. A live cord is a scene problem from chapter 1. Do not grab the person until the cord is dead.

Clothing that is stuck to burned skin stays. Cut around it if you must free a part. Pulling stuck cloth pulls skin. Jewelry that will trap a swelling hand is a note for the crew, same as the ring in chapter 4.

A small red patch that hurts, the person talking, water already running: stay with the water and watch. A patch that is white, charred, or painless on a large area: call. Pain is not the only measure. A painless large burn can be the worse one. The living card owns the size rules. Unverified here.

**Check.** Steam from a kettle, a wrist, a sink. Cool water on the wrist. Write what you did not smear on it.

New case: a splash of oven cleaner on a forearm. That is not kettle steam. Keep the bottle. Stop. Chapter 7 owns the number you call. A third case: 1 palm, red, the person can talk and make a fist. Water. Watch. If the same palm blisters while you watch, you still have a burn. You now also have a reason to open the living burn card and to call if the card says so.

## 6. Choke

A cafeteria. A grape. A cough that will not finish. Hands at the throat. The face is changing color.

If they can cough, air is still moving. Let them cough. Stay. Do not slap a back that is already moving air out. Cough is the body's own press.

If they cannot make a sound, cannot cough, cannot breathe, the tube is blocked. Call. Then you need the living choke card: the trained sequence of back blows and abdominal thrusts, and the counts that go with them. This sitting did not fetch those counts from AHA or from Red Cross. Unverified here. A remembered five-and-five stays off this page.

People name a blocked airway **choking**. The name is the handle. The fact you can see is sound or no sound, cough or no cough, air or no air.

A child, a grape, a hard candy, a coin, a piece of meat: the object in the tube may differ. The ten minutes do not include fishing blindly with fingers you cannot see. A finger sweep of a mouth you cannot inspect can push the object farther in. If you can see the object and lift it out without pushing, that is a different act. If you cannot see it, keep to the living card.

If the person drops and the chest is still, you have left choking and entered chapter 2. Call. Open the CPR card. The two cards sit next to each other on the same hosts.

**Check.** Four chairs at a lunch table. One person coughing hard, able to nod. Write: air is moving. Let them cough. Stay.

New case: the same table, no sound, hands at the throat, 3 other people watching. You call. You send one watcher to the door. You open the living choke card. If you cannot fetch it, write unverified next to any thrust count you were about to perform from memory, and still call. A third case: a toddler, a grape, a cough that produces the grape onto the table. Air is moving again. Watch. If the cough stops and the chest is quiet, you are back to no air.

## 7. Poison

A laundry room. A child. A pod on the floor. A wet mouth. Keep the pod. Do not guess a home cure. Do not finish a dose from a memory of milk, salt water, or a finger down the throat.

This sitting opened `poison.org`. That page prints a phone number for a poison center: **1-800-222-1222**. It also points to the webPOISONCONTROL tool. Both, the page says, are free, expert, and confidential. If the person collapses, has a seizure, has trouble breathing, or cannot be awakened, that same page says: call **911** now.

Those two numbers are the living poison card for this sitting. Other countries keep other numbers. If you are not in the system that page describes, those digits are unverified for you. Find the local door.

The page lists top poison categories as shares of cases: medications 56.7 of 100, cleaners 9.3, cosmetics 5.5, supplements 4.9, pesticides 4.7, plants 3.6, food and drink 1.7, bites and stings 0.9, batteries 0.3, carbon monoxide 0.2. A story that says "most household poisons are plants" loses to that list. Medications were the large pile on the page this sitting fetched.

The same host prints that swallowed batteries burn through a child's esophagus in 2 hours, and that 20 mm, 3-volt lithium coin cells, about the size of a nickel, are the most hazardous because they are big enough to get stuck and burn faster. Those three figures (2 hours, 20 mm, 3 volts) came from that page. A smaller cell you cannot measure is still a battery. Call. Do not wait 2 hours to see.

Liquid nicotine, the page says, is very poisonous if swallowed. Carbon monoxide is named there as an invisible killer: alarms in sleeping areas, no grill or gasoline tool indoors, no car running in an attached garage, no generator tight against the house. Inner first-aid steps for each substance live on the host's first-aid-for-poisonings door. This sitting did not open that inner door. Unverified here.

People name a harmful swallow, splash, breath, or sting a **poisoning**. The bottle, the plant, the pill, the battery, the wasp: bring the name of the thing to the call. The living tool will ask.

**Check.** A laundry pod, a wet mouth, a child who is awake and crying. Keep the packet. Call 1-800-222-1222 or use the web tool the page names. Write the two facts you will say: what, and that the child is awake.

New case: a pill bottle, 8 tablets missing from a count of 24, an adult who is sleepy but can talk. Keep the bottle. Same number. 24 take away 8 is 16 still in the bottle, so 8 are the missing pile. Say 8, the name on the label, and sleepy-but-talking. A third case: a 20 mm, 3-volt lithium coin cell, a child, a swallow you did not see but a battery is gone. Call. The page printed 2 hours to burn. Do not use those 2 hours as a wait. If they collapse or cannot be awakened, the same page already told you to call 911.

## 8. Heat and cold

A July parking lot. A person sits on a curb. Confused. Skin hot. You ask the day of the week and they cannot say it.

Move them out of the sun. Shade. Sit. Loosen extra layers. If they can drink, small sips of water. If they cannot drink, or cannot answer, or fall, call.

A January bus stop. Wet gloves. The person was shivering and then the shiver stopped. Move them out of the wind. Dry clothes if you have them. Skin-to-skin under a coat is heat you can give without a machine. If they will not wake, call.

People name the July job **heat illness** and the January job **cold injury**. Inner numbers (a body temperature that counts as an emergency, a minute count for warming or cooling) live on CDC pages. This sitting opened `cdc.gov` and got the agency home, not a heat card and not a cold card. Those inner figures are unverified here. Open the CDC door. A remembered 104, a remembered 95, stay off this page.

Heat that will not sweat, with confusion, is a different ten minutes from heat that sweats and can still joke. Both get shade. Confusion gets a call. Cold that has stopped shivering is a different ten minutes from cold that still shakes. Both get dry and shelter. The stop of the shiver gets a call.

Do not rub hard on white, numb fingers or toes as a cure. Do not put a confused hot person back into a closed car "to rest." The car is still the parking lot. The shade you want is a building, a tree, a bus.

A sports field can fool you. A player who sits and does not answer a simple name is not "tired." Shade. Call. A player who drinks and talks and wants the next point still needs shade and a watcher. One sitting is not a diagnosis. It is ten minutes.

**Check.** A soccer field in July. One player sits, will not answer their own name. Shade. Call. Write the fact you will say: hot day, not answering, one person.

New case: a bus stop in January, wet gloves, no shiver left, the person can still say the street. Dry. Shelter. Call anyway, because the shiver stopped. A third case: 3 walkers, 1 lagging, 2 still talking. The lagging walker is the body in this chapter. Do not average the three. The two who talk are your extra hands.

## 9. Call

You have looked. You have one body. You need a second trained set of hands.

In the United States, the poison.org page fetched for this book prints **911** for collapse, a seizure, trouble breathing, or a person you cannot wake. That is the emergency number this sitting has on a door. Other countries keep other numbers. If you are not in that system, 911 is unverified for you. Find the local door before you need it.

Say where you are. Say how many bodies. Say what you see: breathing or not, bleeding or not, awake or not. Say a poison, a burn, a crooked limb, heat, or cold if that is the room. Then answer the questions the dispatcher asks. Stay on the line unless they tell you to go.

A second person in the doorway is the one you send to the street to wave. You stay with the body. If you are alone, the call is still the call. Put the phone on speaker. Keep your eyes on the chest.

A workplace, a school, a pool, a stadium may have a box on the wall that shocks a stopped heart back into a rhythm. People name that box an **AED**. This sitting did not fetch the button sequence. Unverified here. If a dispatcher or a labeled lid tells you to open it, follow the lid. The living voice on the box is the card for those minutes.

A class card, a Heartsaver eCard, a workplace poster: none of those is the call. The call is the number spoken into a working phone, with a place attached.

**Check.** You are at 14 Elm. One person, chest still. You say: 14 Elm, one person, not breathing. That is a complete call.

New case: 2 people after a crash on Oak, 1 bleeding from a shin, 1 walking and talking. You say: Oak at the market, two people, one bleeding, one walking. If you say only "an accident," the crew has no count. A third case: a laundry room, a child, a pod, the child awake and crying. If they are awake and breathing, poison.org printed the poison number, not 911, for that pair of facts. If they then collapse, the same page moves you to 911.

## 10. A check against the living card

This book's object, used: ten minutes with a body, walked to a living card, with every load-bearing count fetched or marked unverified.

The method is the same as the study pack's habit for a number, applied to a body. Open the host that owns the step. If the host and a memory disagree, keep the host. If the host will not open, write unverified and still call.

Do this on paper with a case this book has not used yet.

A workshop at 9 Pine. A person on the floor. A bottle of solvent on its side. A cut on the palm. A quiet chest. A second person in the door. Write the order.

1. Stop and look. Solvent on the floor is a scene. Do not kneel in the puddle. Right the bottle if you can do it from dry floor, or leave it and keep the label.
2. Breathing. Chest still. That fact goes into the call.
3. Bleeding. Palm hole. Cloth and press once you can reach from dry floor. Do not leave the still chest to wash a palm in the sink.
4. Poison. Keep the bottle. If they are collapsed, poison.org already printed 911. If they later wake, the poison number is 1-800-222-1222.
5. Call. 9 Pine, one person down, not breathing, a solvent bottle, a cut on the palm. Send the person in the door to the street.

Now open the doors. AHA for the press work. Poison.org for the bottle. CDC if heat or cold is also in the room. Red Cross for a first-aid class. This sitting: AHA home opened, poison.org opened, CDC home opened, Red Cross refused. Write which doors opened for you today.

A press count you cannot fetch is unverified. Write that word on the paper next to the blank. Do not fill the blank from a poster in a hallway. A bleed minute you cannot fetch is unverified. A burn minute you cannot fetch is unverified. A heat temperature you cannot fetch is unverified.

**Check.** On paper, four columns: fact you saw, act in the room, door, number on the door. Fill one row for the still chest at 9 Pine. Fill a second row for the solvent bottle. Fill a third row for the palm. If a cell is empty because a door would not open, write unverified in that cell.

New case, second workshop: the chest is now rising, the person is talking, the palm is the only hole, the bottle is closed. The call may change. The poison number may be enough if they stay awake. The still-chest row from the first workshop does not copy here. You are looking at this body, this minute.

A third case: you remember a ratio from a class two years ago. Write it in the margin as memory. Open the living card. If the card prints a different count, keep the card and mark the margin. If the card will not open, the margin stays memory, not a step.

That is the object of this book, used: ten minutes with a body, a look, a call, and a door, with physiology left in the health pack and every remembered ratio left off the page.
