# Charge, current, and field

You already know a push, a mass, and how a motion changes. Mechanics taught those three. This book is the next act: a piece of tape that shoves another piece without touching, a wire that carries that shove as a walk, and the prepared space around both.

People also call the whole family electromagnetism. The family name can wait until you have seen the members.

The living SI names for the coulomb, the ampere, the volt, and the tesla, and the sentences that fix those units, live in the measurement pack and on NIST and BIPM. This book names those doors and stops. Force as an object lives in mechanics. This book names that door and stops. A kilowatt-hour on a meter and a bill for a house live in the energy pack. This book names that door and stops.

The walks here are charge, current, and field.

Public general education.

## 1. Charge

Tess peels two strips of tape from the same roll, hangs them a thumb apart, and lets go. The strips swing away from each other. Nothing visible sits between them. Her fingers did not stay in the gap. The air in the room did not shove the strips the way a hand shoves a crate.

She peels a third strip and brings it near a strip she has not rubbed. Those two swing toward each other. Same bench, two stories: some pairs push, some pairs pull.

Whatever the tape is carrying comes in two kinds. Two of the same kind push. Two of different kinds pull. That carried amount is **charge**.

Rubbing does not make charge out of empty air. It moves charge from one body onto the other. A cloth starts even. After the rub, the rod holds 8 units of one kind and the cloth holds 8 units of the other kind. $8 - 8 = 0$ if you add them with a sign: one kind written $+$, the other written $-$. The pile on the pair did not grow. It split.

Write the two kinds as signed numbers and they add. A bead at $+5$ and a bead at $-2$ held together act as $+3$. A bead at $+4$ and a bead at $-4$ held together act as $0$. Neutral is not "no stuff." Neutral is both kinds present in matching piles, or both absent.

You cannot take an ordinary object and shave charge down forever. The pile comes in whole counts of a smallest unit. That smallest unit is the **elementary charge**. NIST keeps its size with the CODATA 2022 values on the Fundamental Physical Constants page. Last update to data content on that page: May 2024. The number itself is unverified here. Open the door. Do not finish that sentence from memory.

A rubbed balloon that clings to a wall still carries a net pile. The wall, for a moment, has the other kind waiting near the surface. Same two kinds. Same push and pull.

**Check.** A rod gives 7 units to a bead. The rod is down 7. The bead is up 7. Net for the pair: $0$. A new case: beads at $+6$ and $-2$ touch and share evenly. Total $4$, two beads, $+2$ each. Another: someone writes that rubbing created 14 new units, 7 on each body, both of the same kind. Stop. The pair would then push the rest of the room and the arithmetic would have invented charge. If you added $7 + 7$ and called 14 a conserved total of one kind, you dropped the signs.

## 2. A field around a charge

Bram parks a tiny charged bead on a thread, far from other charged things. He walks a second tiny bead, also charged, in a slow circle around the first. At every point the second bead feels a shove. The shove has a size and a heading. He can map both without leaving the second bead there.

The first bead prepared the space. The map of that preparation is an **electric field**. The field is not a second kind of charge. It is what the second bead feels, written as a number and an arrow at each point, whether the second bead is present or not.

People draw the arrow the way a small positive test charge would be shoved. MIT OpenCourseWare 8.02, Physics II: Electricity and Magnetism, Spring 2019, puts that picture on the course page: a circle marked $+$ with arrows pointing out, and a circle marked $-$ with arrows pointing in. Out from plus. In toward minus. That is the heading rule.

Stand twice as far from a single small charge, and the field is one fourth. Stand three times as far, and the field is one ninth. The fall is the square of the extra distance. Invented for the arithmetic, not a lab reading: 20 field-units at 1 step. At 2 steps: $20 \div 4 = 5$. At 4 steps: $20 \div 16 = 1.25$. Undo: $1.25 \times 16 = 20$. If those two walks disagree, a square was skipped.

The leftover factor that turns a charge and a distance into a field-unit is a constant on the NIST door. Unverified here. You can still check the one-fourth rule without that factor.

A charge sitting in a field is shoved. How hard is charge times field, in the units you chose. That shove is a force. Force as an object lives in mechanics. Name that door and stop. The multiply is the electromagnetism piece: 3 charge-units in a field of 5 field-units feel $3 \times 5 = 15$ of those force-units, along the field if the charge is positive, against the field if the charge is negative.

Two charges both prepare the space. At a point, add the arrows. If they point the same way, the lengths add. If they point opposite ways, the lengths subtract. A point halfway between $+8$ and $+8$, equal distances, has two equal arrows opposed. Sum $0$. The beads still shove each other. The midpoint is quiet.

**Check.** Field 12 at 1 step from a point charge. At 2 steps: $12 \div 4 = 3$. A new case: test charge $+4$ in a field of 7, product $28$ force-units with the field. Flip the test charge to $-4$: same 28, opposite heading. Another: two equal positive charges, a point twice as far from one as from the other, along the line that joins them, outside the pair. The nearer charge's field is four times the farther one. They do not cancel. If you added 12 and 3 and called 15 the field at twice the distance from one charge, you added two different points.

## 3. Current

A lamp sits on the bench. A wire runs from a cell to the lamp and back. Bram closes the last clip. The lamp lights. He opens the clip. The lamp dies. Charge was already in the metal. Sitting charge did not light the lamp. Charge walking past a cut in the wire did.

How much charge walks through a chosen cut in a chosen time is **current**. Current is a rate. Algebra already lets you write a rate as a pile divided by a time.

Eighteen charge-units through a cut in 6 seconds: $18 \div 6 = 3$ charge-units per second. Same 18 in 9 seconds: $18 \div 9 = 2$ per second. A slower walk, a dimmer lamp if the lamp is the only load and the cell holds still.

People draw the arrow as the way positive charge would walk, from the cell's plus end through the lamp toward the minus end. In a metal, the walkers are the other kind, and they walk the other way. The current arrow is still the plus-walk. One arrow. Two stories about who is moving. Do not add them as two currents.

A break anywhere in a single loop stops the walk everywhere in that loop. Charge does not jump a gap of air at the voltages on this bench. Four clips in a loop, one of them open: current $0$. Close the last clip: the walk is the same at every cut, because charge is not piling up in the wire. What enters a short piece in a second leaves that piece in that second.

The SI name for the unit of this rate is the ampere. How the ampere is fixed lives in the measurement pack and on the BIPM ampere door. Unverified here.

A thick wire and a thin wire in the same loop still carry the same current if they are the only path. The thin stretch may warm. Warmth is leftover energy in the metal, which energy owns as waste heat. This chapter keeps the walk.

**Check.** 14 charge-units through a cut in 7 seconds: $14 \div 7 = 2$ per second. A new case: $0.5$ per second for 16 seconds is $8$ charge-units through the cut. Undo: $8 \div 16 = 0.5$. Another: a loop with current 3 per second at the lamp, and a claim that the return wire carries 0. Stop. One loop, one walk. If you wrote 3 out and 0 back, you stored 3 charge-units per second in the lamp. The lamp would not hold that pile.

## 4. A magnet

A needle on a pin turns until one painted end points along the bench. Tess brings a second bar near that painted end. The needle swings away. She flips the bar. The needle swings in. The bar has two ends that are not the same.

Two ends, two kinds of meeting: same ends push, different ends pull. A body that does this, even with no wire and no cell, is a **magnet**. The prepared space around it is a **magnetic field**.

Cut the bar in half. Each piece still has two ends. You do not get a lone end. Magnetic field lines, in the picture people draw, leave one end and return to the other. They close. Electric field lines, from chapter 2, start on plus and end on minus. That difference is the whole of this paragraph.

A coil of wire with a current also turns the needle. Twelve turns, current on: the needle jumps. Current off: the needle settles back to the room's heading. The coil is a magnet while charge walks. Turns add if they face the same way. 4 turns with the same current are twice 2 turns, if the coil is otherwise the same. $4 \div 2 = 2$.

The Earth is a magnet. The painted end of the needle is seeking one of the Earth's magnetic ends. Sailors named that end north. The name is older than the field picture. Do not treat "north" as a pile of magnetic charge. No such pile has shown up.

NIST does not keep a magnetic-charge constant next to the elementary charge. The constants page is still the door for the electric pile and for the leftover factors in the field rules. Magnetic charge as a number is not on that page because the object is not on the bench.

**Check.** Two bars, painted end to painted end: they push. Flip one bar: they pull. A new case: a coil of 8 turns, then a coil of 2 turns, same current, same shape. The 8-turn coil is four times the 2-turn coil as a magnet, $8 \div 2 = 4$. Another: someone snaps a bar and writes "one north piece, one south piece." Pick up either piece. Each still has two ends. If you counted the snap as a way to isolate one kind, you treated a magnet like the tape in chapter 1. It is not that object.

## 5. A changing field and a loop

A loop of copper sits on the bench with no cell in it. A meter on the loop reads 0. Tess holds a magnet still in the mouth of the loop. Still 0. She pulls the magnet out. The meter kicks, then returns to 0. She holds the magnet still on the table. 0. She shoves the magnet in. The meter kicks the other way, then 0.

A magnetic field that sits does not drive a current in that loop. A magnetic field that changes through the loop does. The change writes an electric field around the loop. If the loop is a wire, charge walks for as long as the change lasts. That brief walk is **induction**.

Faster pull, bigger kick. Two equal pulls, one in half the time of the other: the faster pull is twice the change-per-second, so twice the kick if the loop is the same. More turns, bigger kick. A 6-turn coil and a 2-turn coil, same magnet, same pull: $6 \div 2 = 3$, so three times the kick if each turn sees the same change.

The kick fights the change. Pull the magnet out, and the loop's current tries to keep the field that was leaving. Push the magnet in, and the loop's current tries to keep the field from growing. The loop is conservative. It spends a walk to slow the change you are making with your hand. Your hand does extra work on a fast pull. That extra is mechanics' work. Name that door.

A transformer is two coils sharing a changing field and not sharing a wire. Charge does not walk from one coil into the other. The field in the middle is the messenger. This pack owns that field walk. A house meter still counts a different object. Energy owns the bill.

**Check.** A 10-turn coil, then a 2-turn coil, same magnet pulled the same way. Kick ratio $10 \div 2 = 5$. A new case: magnet parked in the coil for 8 seconds. Kick $0$ for those 8 seconds. Another: two equal pulls, times 4 seconds and 1 second. The 1-second pull is four times the change-per-second. Four times the kick if the coil is unchanged. If you added 10 turns to 2 turns and wrote 12 as the ratio, you added turns instead of sharing them.

## 6. Light as that field in motion

A radio mast on the hill shakes charge back and forth. Each shake is a current that starts and stops. A current that changes writes a magnetic field that changes. A magnetic field that changes writes an electric field around it, the same writing as the loop on the bench. That new electric field is itself changing, so it writes a magnetic field again.

Once the two fields are writing each other, they do not need the mast. They walk away through empty space. What arrives at a distant aerial, or at an eye, is **light** in the wide sense: an electromagnetic wave. The colours you see are one narrow band of that family. Radio is a slower wiggle of the same two fields. The heat you feel from a coal, standing to one side, is a faster wiggle of the same two fields.

MIT 8.02 states the course in three sentences on its page: electric and magnetic fields arise from charged particles; charged particles also feel forces in electric and magnetic fields; Maxwell's equations, in addition to describing this behavior, also describe electromagnetic radiation. The third sentence is this chapter. Chapter 8 names those four equations as rules. This chapter keeps the walk.

Empty space is enough. Night sky, no air worth counting, and starlight still arrives. A wire is not required. A wave on water is a different object. Do not import the pond.

How often the wiggle repeats, in one second, is a frequency. Double the frequency, twice as many crests pass a post in that second. 80 crests, then 160 crests, in the same second: twice the frequency. In empty space the product of frequency and the length of one wiggle stays one number: the speed of this wave. NIST keeps that speed with the 2022 CODATA values. Unverified here as a figure. Measurement owns the metre and the second that make the speed a reading.

A claim that light is a stream of charge walking from the lamp to your face fails on a simple fact: the space between you and the lamp does not slowly fill with tape-charge. The fields cross it.

**Check.** 50 crests in 2 seconds is 25 per second. A new case: 25 per second for 4 seconds is 100 crests. Another: a room lit by a window, no lamp, no wire to the sun. The light still arrived. If you wrote that sunlight needed a cord from the hill mast, you mixed chapter 3's walk with this walk.

## 7. A simple circuit

A cell, a lamp, and two clips sit in a drawer. Tess clips one wire from the cell's plus end to the lamp, and one wire from the lamp back to the minus end. The lamp lights. She opens a clip. The lamp dies. The closed path is a **circuit**.

Three numbers you can write on that path. How hard the cell pushes, from plus to minus, is **voltage**. How much charge walks is current, from chapter 3. How much the lamp fights the walk is **resistance**. For a lamp that holds still, voltage is current times resistance.

Invented for the arithmetic, not a shop rating: push 24, resistance 8, current $24 \div 8 = 3$. Undo: $3 \times 8 = 24$. If those two disagree, a place slid.

Two lamps in one loop, one after the other, share one current. Resistances add. Two lamps of 5 and 3, series: resistance $5 + 3 = 8$. Same 24-push cell: current $24 \div 8 = 3$. Each lamp sees its share of the push: $3 \times 5 = 15$ on the first, $3 \times 3 = 9$ on the second. $15 + 9 = 24$. The cell's push was spent around the loop.

Two lamps side by side, each with its own path from plus to minus, share one voltage. Currents add. Two lamps of resistance 8 and 8 on a 24-push cell: each path $24 \div 8 = 3$, total current $3 + 3 = 6$. Opening one path leaves the other lit. Series is not that story. Open one series lamp and the whole loop dies.

The SI names volt and ohm live in measurement. Unverified here as definitions. A house bill in kilowatt-hours lives in the energy pack. This chapter stops at the loop on the bench.

**Check.** Push 18, resistance 6, current $18 \div 6 = 3$. A new case: series 4 and 12 on a 32-push cell. Total resistance 16, current $32 \div 16 = 2$. Shares of push: $8$ and $24$. $8 + 24 = 32$. Another: two equal paths of resistance 10 on a 20-push cell. Each current 2, total 4. If you added 10 and 10 and then divided 20 by 20, you treated two side-by-side paths as one long path.

## 8. Four rules

A charge at rest has already shown you a prepared electric space. A magnet and a coil have already shown you a prepared magnetic space. A pulled magnet has already shown you a changing magnetic field writing an electric field around a loop. A radio mast has already shown you the two fields writing each other with no wire at the far end.

Four facts, one each:

1. Charge is the source of electric field. More charge in a region, more electric field pointing out of that region. Plus starts a line. Minus ends a line. Empty of charge, the electric lines that enter a region leave it.
2. Magnetic field lines close. The magnetic field pointing out of a region is matched by the magnetic field pointing in. No magnetic charge on the bench, so no leftover.
3. A magnetic field that changes through a loop writes an electric field around the loop. That is chapter 5, written as a rule for every loop, wire or not.
4. A current through a loop's mouth, and also an electric field that changes through that mouth, writes a magnetic field around the loop. A steady current is a magnet. A changing electric field is a magnet too, even with no charge walking.

Those four are **Maxwell's equations**. MIT 8.02 splits the long course into Electrostatics, Magnetic Fields and Forces, and Maxwell's Equations. The third module is this list, including the radiation of chapter 6. This pack does not copy that course. It names the door.

Algebra is enough to use the four as checks. A sitting charge, no current, no change: only rule 1 does work. A sitting magnet, no change: rule 2, and rule 4 with current 0 and electric change 0, so no new electric loop. A magnet pulled through a coil: rule 3. A wire with a steady current next to a compass: rule 4 on the current piece. Light in empty space: rules 3 and 4 with no charge and no current at the point you are watching, only the two fields changing.

If a claim needs magnetic charge, it is fighting rule 2. If a claim needs a sitting magnet to drive a sitting loop forever, it is fighting rule 3, which asked for a change.

**Check.** Compass next to a live wire: rule 4. A new case: magnet yanked from a 5-turn loop: rule 3. Sitting tape-charge on a stand: rule 1. Another: someone says starlight needs a magnetic charge at the star so that rule 2 can launch the wave. Stop. Rule 2 forbids the leftover. Rules 3 and 4 launch the wave. If you invoked rule 1 for a compass next to a current, you looked for tape-charge in a magnet story.

## 9. Energy in the field

Tess pushes two beads that carry the same kind of charge closer. The beads fight. Her hand moves them through a distance. That delivered change is work, which mechanics owns. She lets go. The beads fly apart. The work she put in comes back as motion. While she held them close, the work was not in her hand. It was in the space between the beads.

Energy can sit in a field. Two plates, a small gap, charge moved from one plate to the other: an electric field in the gap, and a pile stored there. People name that pair of plates a capacitor when they mean the store. The name can wait. The gap is the object.

The first bit of charge is easy to move. The last bit fights a stronger field. The average push is half the final push. Energy stored is average push times charge moved. Invented: final push 8, charge moved 10. Average push $8 \div 2 = 4$. Energy $4 \times 10 = 40$ work-units. Half of final push times charge is the same sentence: $\frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 10 = 40$. If you wrote $8 \times 10 = 80$, you billed every bit of charge as if it had felt the final field from the first moment.

Discharge the plates through a lamp. The 40 work-units leave the gap. In the toy with no leftover heat in the wires, the lamp receives 40. Real wires warm. Energy owns that leftover.

A changing magnetic field stores and hands on energy the same way, without a charge walking the whole path. Chapter 5's two coils share a field, not a wire. The messenger carries the pile.

A wave of chapter 6 carries energy with it. Starlight warms a stone. No cord. The fields that arrived held the pile.

A house meter still writes kilowatt-hours. That walk, and the bill, live in the energy pack. This chapter stops at the gap and the wave.

**Check.** Final push 12, charge moved 4. Average 6. Energy $6 \times 4 = 24$. Undo: $\frac{1}{2} \times 12 \times 4 = 24$. A new case: final push 5, charge 8, energy $20$. Another: two beads of the same kind, held at 2 steps, then at 1 step. The closer hold took more work. That extra now sits in the tighter field. If you said the extra vanished when her hand let go and the beads were still held by a peg, you lost the pile. The peg is still doing the job her hand did.

## 10. A check

A charge constant or a speed of light comes from the NIST constants door. A force law comes from mechanics. A porch kilowatt-hour comes from energy. Arithmetic you can redo by hand stays on paper.

Walk:

1. Name what you are holding: charge on a body, a field at a point, a current through a cut, a magnet, a changing field through a loop, a wave, a closed path of wire, energy in a gap.
2. Redo the arithmetic with a new number. Inverse square: four times the distance, field to one sixteenth. Rate: pile over time. Circuit: push equals current times resistance. Store: half of final push times charge.
3. If the number is a definition or a constant, open NIST or BIPM. If the number is a force, open mechanics. If the number is a kilowatt-hour on a porch, open energy.
4. If the door and a remembered line disagree, believe the door. If the door will not open, write unverified and stop building on it.

Elementary charge and the speed of light sit on the NIST constants door. This book does not print those rows. Measurement owns the CODATA walk.

MIT 8.02, Spring 2019, is the lecture door: fields from charges, forces on charges in fields, Maxwell's equations, radiation. OpenStax University Physics Volume 2 is the long undergrad walk. The details page is the door. This pack does not copy that book. BIPM keeps the ampere with the SI brochure. Measurement owns the brochure walk.

**Check.** 21 charge-units through a cut in 7 seconds: $21 \div 7 = 3$ per second. A new case: field 16 at 1 step, field at 2 steps is 4. At 4 steps: $16 \div 16 = 1$. Another: push 28, resistance 7, current 4. Series 7 and 7 is 14, current $28 \div 14 = 2$ on that same push. A fourth case that needs a door: someone quotes the elementary charge from memory and builds a later number on it. Open NIST. If the door will not open, write unverified. A fifth case with no door: two beads $+9$ and $-3$ held together act as $+6$. That is adding. If you fetched NIST to add 9 and $-3$, you walked into the wrong house.
