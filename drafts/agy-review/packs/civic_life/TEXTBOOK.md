# A town, a vote, an office you can find

You can already read, and you can keep a page. This book is a town, a vote, and an office you can find.

The object is the walk from a street to a desk that does a named job. Not a survey of a constitution. Not a feeling that you are a citizen because you heard a speech.

EasyLM is public general education. The living civics pack in this library is the undergrad survey of a constitution. This book names that door and stops. Stay here when the task is to find the hall, the clerk, the ballot, the tax bill, the court room, or the person who can stop a desk.

---

## 1. A town

Walk the square in Stoneferry. A clock sits on a brick hall. A window in the hall faces the square. Behind the glass a person stamps paper from 09:00 to 16:00. Ferry Street runs to the river. Mill Road runs to the old mill. Elm holds a library. Court Street holds a room with a bench and a clock of its own.

You can point at those buildings. A visitor who lands at the river can be told: the hall is the brick with the clock, the window is the one with the line, the court is the east door on Court Street. The map is made of doors a body can use.

People name that mapped place a **town**. The name is a handle for the square and the jobs, not for a mood about home. Some places say city, village, borough, or township. The walk does not change: streets, a hall, a person with a stamp, a rule about who pays for the lights.

Stoneferry sits inside a larger map. The pothole on Ferry Street is the town's job. A marriage record may live at a county desk in Ridge County, ten miles inland. A driving license may live at a state desk. A passport lives at a national desk. Four boxes, nested. You do not need the founding theory to pick the box. You need the job.

I fetched USA.gov this sitting. The front door's title is "Making government services easier to find." The page says USA.gov helps you locate and understand government benefits, programs, and information. That is the national front door for a stranger who does not yet know which box holds the job. The living civics pack surveys how those boxes were written into a constitution. This chapter only needs the nested map.

A town that exists on a sign and nowhere as a desk is a painting. If you cannot name a window or a person who stamps, you have found a name on a map.

**Check.** Write one sentence that sends a stranger from the river to the clerk's window in Stoneferry. The sentence must name a street or a building a body can see. Then write a second sentence that says "the community is close-knit." Hand both to a person who has never been here. Only the first sentence gets them to the glass.

New case: a passport. Do not send the stranger to the clerk's window. Open USA.gov. On the front door I fetched, "Get or renew a passport" sits in the first four "How do I" tasks. The town hall does not print passports. The nested map just earned its keep.

## 2. A rule written down

A sheet on the clerk's glass says: no parking on Ferry Street after 20:00. A second sheet in a binder behind the glass says the same thing, with a date, a vote of the council, and a stamp. A third voice on the square says everyone knows you cannot park there. Only two of those three will hold when a neighbor points at your truck at 21:10.

The voice on the square is a rumor. The sheet on the glass is a notice. The binder is the rule the town agreed to keep. People name a town rule of that kind an **ordinance** when the town's own council adopted it and the clerk filed it. A rule the state wrote is often called a **statute**. A rule the nation wrote, and put in the United States Code, is a federal statute. The picture is the same: words on a page a stranger can fetch, with a who, a date, and a file number.

I fetched Congress.gov this sitting. The live page did not yield a body I could quote. The URL still stands as the door for a federal bill and a federal law. I did not count the bills. Any count of "how many laws" from this paragraph is unverified. Open the door when the rule is national. Open the clerk's binder when the rule is Stoneferry's parking hour.

USA.gov, on the front door I fetched, has a topic "The U.S. and its government" at `/about-the-us`, which the page describes as a place to learn about U.S. laws and history, and to contact elected officials and federal agencies. It also lists "Laws and legal issues" at `/laws-and-legal-issues`. Those are finder's doors. They are not the statute itself. The statute lives on the legislature's site, in the clerk's binder, or on the United States Code door in `LINK_INDEX.md`.

A rule you cannot find is a rumor with a costume. "We have always done it this way" is not a file number. Ask to see the page. If the clerk cannot produce the page, you do not yet have the rule. You have a habit.

Write the parking rule so a stranger can test it. "No parking on Ferry Street after 20:00" names a street, an act, and a time. "Be considerate about parking" names a mood. A truck at 21:10 settles the first. It cannot settle the second.

**Check.** Copy the Ferry Street line. Change 20:00 to 18:00. Write what a truck at 19:10 does under each version. The two answers must differ. If they do not, the hour was not doing work.

New case: the mill pond. Someone on the square says no one may fish there on Sunday. Ask for the page. If the binder has no Sunday line, the square's voice is not the rule. If the binder has the line, copy the file number and the date. A number you cannot point at is unverified.

## 3. A vote

Twelve people sit in the hall on a Monday. The question on the sheet is whether to repair the mill pond's spillway. Seven raise a hand. Four keep it down. One sits on both. The clerk counts 7 and 4 and writes both numbers. The repair will be done because 7 is more than 4. The person who sat on both did not add to either pile.

That act, a counted choice that binds the room, is a **vote**. The paper or machine that holds one person's choice is a **ballot**. A day when a town, a state, or a nation puts offices or questions to the people who may choose is an **election**. The list of people allowed to hand in a ballot is a **register**. Those names are handles for the hands, the paper, the day, and the list. You already saw all four.

A vote is not a cheer. A cheer is loud. A vote is counted. If the clerk writes "the room felt positive" and skips 7 and 4, the town has a mood and no decision. If the clerk writes 8 and 4, the pile is a lie. Count again in public. The number has to match the hands.

Who may hand in a ballot is a rule, not a feeling. I fetched Vote.gov this sitting. The live page did not yield a body I could quote. The URL still stands as the door to register in the United States. USA.gov's front door, which did yield a body, puts "Check my voter registration" first among its four "How do I" tasks, and lists a topic "Voting and elections" that covers how to register, where a voting location is, and how presidential elections work. Open those doors for the living steps in your state. Do not invent a residence day or an identification rule from this chapter. Those numbers are unverified here.

The living civics pack surveys who a constitution says may vote, and which offices a nation elects. This chapter's object is smaller: a choice, counted, that a clerk can write down, on a list a person can join.

Stoneferry in this book lets a person on the register vote for the five seats on the council, and on a yes-or-no question the council puts on the ballot. A person who is not on the register can still sit in the hall and speak when the sheet says so. Speaking is not a vote. The hands are the vote.

**Check.** Twelve people. 7 yes, 4 no, 1 silent. Write the two piles. Circle the pile that wins under a simple more-yes-than-no rule. Then move one yes to no. Write the new piles. If your circle does not move, you are not watching the count.

New case: 6 yes, 6 no. A tie. Stoneferry's posted rule in this book says a tie fails, so the spillway stays as it is. Your real town may write a different tie rule. Open the clerk's binder for that town. The 6 and 6 here are a teaching pair, not a statute.

## 4. An office

Behind the glass sits a person with a stamp, a clock, and a list of hours. The person can file an ordinance, take a ballot, copy a record, and refuse a request that names no record. The person cannot lock you in a room, cannot write a new tax by themselves, and cannot hear the fence case on Ferry Street. When the calendar hits the last day of the term, another person can sit in the same chair.

That chair, with a job, a limit, and a way to fill it again, is an **office**. The person in the chair is an **officer** for the length of the term. The town clerk is one office. A seat on the five-person council is another. A sheriff in Ridge County is another. A judge in the east wing is another. "The government" is a fog that hides which chair you need.

An office is a desk, not a throne. A throne lasts until death or revolt. A desk in Stoneferry lasts until the term ends, or until a rule the town already wrote removes the person. The stamp stays. The person changes.

Hours are part of the office. 09:00 to 16:00 on the clerk's window is a limit you can test. A desk that claims every hour, including the hour it is closed, is selling a fog. Come at 16:30 and the glass is dark. The office still exists. It is not in session.

USA.gov, on the front door I fetched, lists "contact elected officials and federal agencies" under "The U.S. and its government." That is how you find a national chair you cannot walk to on Ferry Street. Congress.gov is the door for the chairs that write federal bills. The White House door in `LINK_INDEX.md` is the front of the national executive chair. I did not fetch that page this sitting. Staff counts and visiting hours are unverified here.

Name the job before you hunt the person. "I need the person who files the parking rule" sends you to the clerk. "I need someone in charge" sends you in a circle. The study pack taught you to bring a question to a page. Bring a job to a hall.

**Check.** Write three chairs in Stoneferry: clerk, council seat, judge. Next to each, write one act that chair may do and one act it may not do. If two chairs share the same "may" line, you have not split the jobs yet. Split them until a stranger could stand in the right doorway.

New case: the sheriff wants the Ferry Street parking hour changed from 20:00 to 18:00. The sheriff can ask. The sheriff cannot stamp the binder. The council votes. The clerk files. If your sentence has the sheriff filing the ordinance, the chairs have merged. Pull them apart.

## 5. A meeting

A sheet on the hall door names a night, a room, and a list of things to talk about. Monday. 19:00. The council chamber. Item 1: minutes of the last sitting. Item 2: the spillway. Item 3: a request to move the Ferry Street hour. The door is unlocked at 18:45. Twelve chairs for the public face five chairs for the council. A clock on the wall is the same clock you can see from the square.

That sitting, with a posted list and a room a stranger can enter, is a **meeting**. The posted list is an **agenda**. The written record of what was said and what was counted is the **minutes**. A short turn at the microphone for a person who does not hold a chair is **public comment**. You already saw the sheet, the room, the notes, and the turn. The names are handles.

A meeting that happens in a kitchen, with no sheet on the door, can still decide a family dinner. It cannot spend the town's $24,000 spillway pile in this book. Stoneferry posts the sheet 48 hours before the night. That 48 is this town's teaching number, not a statute for your town. Many states write a public-meeting rule with their own hours. I did not fetch a state legislature page this sitting. Open your state's legislature door, or the clerk, for the living hour. Unverified here.

The agenda is a promise about what the room will touch. If the spillway is not on the sheet, the council in this book does not vote the spillway that night. A surprise vote on a pile of money, with no sheet, is how a stranger in the chairs becomes a decoration. Ask to see the sheet before the night. Take a copy.

Minutes are not a novel. They name who sat, who spoke, what was counted, and what failed. "A lively discussion was had" is a mood. "Spillway repair: 4 yes, 1 no. Adopted." is a record. The clerk keeps the record with the binder from chapter 2.

Public comment is a turn, not a vote. Stoneferry in this book gives 3 minutes. The clock on the wall does that job. If you use the 3 minutes to ask for a number the clerk already has, you spent the turn. If you use it to add a fact the council did not have, the turn did work.

**Check.** Write an agenda with three items for next Monday at 19:00. One item must be the spillway. One must be minutes. One must be the Ferry Street hour. Then cross out the spillway and write what the council may still vote that night. If you left the spillway in, you did not watch the sheet.

New case: you arrive at 19:20. Item 1 is done. Item 2 is being counted. You may sit. You may not restart item 1 because you were late. The meeting is a clock, not a waiting room that pauses for each arrival.

## 6. A local tax

The town wants $480,000 this year for roads, a clerk, and street lights. The money does not fall on the square. It is taken, by a rule, from people and places the rule names, and it is spent on the jobs the rule names. That taking, for a public job, is a **tax**. When the town is the one doing the taking, people name it a **local tax**. A state tax and a national tax are cousins in other chairs. This chapter stays with the hall.

A fair-share stick, for teaching, is to split $480,000 across 240 houses as if each house paid the same. $480,000 / 240 = $2,000. Each house would owe $2,000 under that stick. Real towns rarely use that stick. They keep a list of land and buildings and a value for each, and they share the bill by those values. The person who keeps the list is often called an **assessor**. The share written on your bill is an **assessment** in ordinary talk, and a **levy** when the town names the pile it must raise. You do not need those names to do the $2,000 sum. You need them when you walk to the desk that wrote your number.

USA.gov, on the front door I fetched, lists a topic "Taxes" at `/taxes`, described there as filing federal income tax, how to pay, and how to check a refund. That door is the national income-tax finder. It is not Stoneferry's $2,000 teaching bill. Do not send a pothole bill to the federal refund page. The local bill lives at the hall or at a county desk. I did not fetch a Stoneferry bill, because Stoneferry is this book's town. Your real bill is on your real town's door. Unverified here.

Add the spillway. The repair costs $24,000. $24,000 / 240 = $100. Under the equal-house stick, each house would now owe $2,100. The $100 is visible. A fog version of the same fact is "we all have to chip in a bit for the pond." A bit is not $100. Write the $100.

A tax without a named job is a taking with the lights off. "The town needs more money" does not tell you whether the $100 is a spillway, a clock, or a party. The agenda from chapter 5, the ordinance from chapter 2, and the bill should name the same job. If they do not, ask the clerk to point at the line.

The personal finance pack, if you open it later, is your own money on a page. This chapter is the town's bill. They meet when you pay. They are not the same object.

**Check.** Start with $480,000 and 240 houses. Write $2,000. Add $24,000. Write $2,100. Then change the house count to 200 and redo the first share only: $480,000 / 200 = $2,400. If your pencil still says $2,000, you reused a number that belonged to 240 houses.

New case: 3 houses. House A is listed at $100,000, house B at $200,000, house C at $100,000. The town needs $4,000. The list totals $400,000. A's share is 100/400 of $4,000, which is $1,000. B's share is $2,000. C's share is $1,000. The equal-house stick would have charged $1,333.33 and leftover cents of fog. The value stick charges B more because B's line is larger. Your real town's formula is on that town's door. This 3-house grid is a teaching case.

## 7. A court you can walk into

A neighbor on Ferry Street says your fence sits on their lot. You say the fence sits on yours. The clerk cannot settle it. The council cannot settle it by a show of hands. You need a room where a person who does not own either lot hears both stories, looks at a map, and writes a decision that both of you must live with.

That room is a **court**. The person on the bench is a **judge**. A small pile of money or a small dispute, heard on a local docket, is often called **small claims**. The names arrive after the room. You already walked Court Street in chapter 1.

Stoneferry's east wing takes the fence. You fill a one-page form. You pay a filing sum the clerk posts on the glass. In this book that sum is $35. The $35 is a teaching number for this town, not your county's living fee. Unverified for any real counter. You get a date. You bring the map, the photos, and one neighbor who saw the posts go in. You do not bring a speech about what a good neighbor is. The judge can see a post. The judge cannot weigh a mood.

A court is not a counter for revenge. It is a clock, a map, and a person whose job is to pick a side of a line or a pile of money, under rules already written. If you want the parking hour changed, that is still the council. If you want the fence line picked, that is the court. Wrong room, no decision.

The Supreme Court of the United States sits at the far end of a long stack. Its door is in `LINK_INDEX.md`. I did not fetch that page this sitting, so I am not printing a sitting calendar or a count of justices. Those numbers are unverified here. The living civics pack surveys that court as part of a constitution. This chapter's object is the room you can walk into on Court Street.

Traffic on Ferry Street may have its own docket: a ticket, a date, a window. A ticket is a claim by the town that you broke a written rule. You may pay. You may appear and contest. You may not tear the ticket up and call that a decision. The court, or the clerk of that court, is the desk that closes the claim.

**Check.** Write the fence on a page: two lots, one line, one row of posts. Name the room that picks the line. Then write the parking hour from chapter 2 on the same page. Name the room that changes the hour. The two rooms must differ. If you sent both to the judge, or both to the council, you merged the jobs.

New case: the $35 filing sum. The clerk's glass in this book says $35. You bring $20. The form does not go in. The court is not open to you yet, because the posted sum is part of the door. If your real county's glass says a different sum, believe that glass. Fetch it. Do not carry $35 from this paragraph into a real line.

## 8. Who may stop whom

A badge can knock on a door on Ferry Street. A badge cannot write the tax. A council can vote the parking hour. A council cannot lock the neighbor in a room. A clerk can refuse a form that names no record. A clerk cannot hear the fence. A judge can pick the fence line. A judge cannot repair the spillway with the town's $24,000.

Those edges are the object. People name the split **separation of powers** when they talk about a constitution. The living civics pack is the undergrad survey of that split as founding law. This chapter keeps the walk: each chair may stop some acts, and may not stop others. You already have the chairs. Here you watch the stops.

A paper from a judge that lets a person with a badge open a locked drawer, or take a person to a court, is a **warrant** in ordinary talk. The picture is the paper, the judge, the drawer. I am not printing a founding amendment from memory. Open the living civics pack for that text. Open the court on Court Street if you need a living paper. A badge with no paper, in a place the law says needs a paper, is a chair stepping over its edge.

Voters stop a council by replacing it on the next election day. That stop is slow. It is still a stop. A council in Stoneferry that wants a clerk gone must use the removal rule the town already wrote. A kitchen mood does not remove a clerk. Minutes, an agenda, a vote. Chapter 5 again.

A higher stack can stop a lower one when the written rule says so. A state statute can overrule Stoneferry's parking hour if the statute says the state owns that hour. A federal statute can bind the state when the national rule reaches that act. Which stack wins is a legal question the living civics pack surveys, and a court can be asked to pick. This book does not pick a live case. It names the move: when two rules collide, you do not average them. You fetch both pages and, if they still collide, you go to a court.

USA.gov's "Complaints" topic, on the front door I fetched, is a finder's door for complaints involving government agencies, telemarketers, products and services, travel, housing, and banking. That door does not replace a warrant, a vote, or a court. It is one more way to find a desk that may already have a job of stopping a named act.

**Check.** Draw four boxes: clerk, council, badge, judge. Write one stop in each box. Then swap the four stops so each lands in a wrong box. Read the swapped page aloud. The jobs should sit in a knot. Put the stops back.

New case: the sheriff, at 21:10, wants your truck on Ferry Street towed under the 20:00 rule. The rule is in the binder. The hour has passed. The tow is the badge doing the council's written hour, not the badge inventing an hour. If the binder still says 20:00 and the sheriff tows at 19:10, the badge has stepped over the edge. The court is the room that can say so.

## 9. A request in writing

You want the town's copy of the spillway vote. You can ask at the glass. You can also write. A dated page with your name, the record you want, and how you want it (a copy, or a look at the binder) survives a closed window and a forgotten conversation. The clerk stamps the page. You keep a copy.

That page is a **request in writing**. When the record belongs to the town or the state, people often say **public record**. When the record belongs to a federal agency, the living name of the written ask is **FOIA**, short for a federal statute whose door is FOIA.gov in `LINK_INDEX.md`. I did not fetch FOIA.gov this sitting. I am not printing a deadline in days from memory. Open that door for a federal record. Open the clerk for Stoneferry's minutes.

One ask per page. "All documents about the pond, the mill, the fish, and anything else of interest" is a net. A net is how a request sits in a pile until someone guesses. "Minutes of the council meeting of Monday 7 April, item 2, spillway vote, including the 4 yes and 1 no" is a record. The clerk can find a record. The clerk cannot find a net.

Date the page. Sign it. Write an address the clerk can answer. If you want paper, say paper. If you want a file sent, say the file kind the clerk already uses. Keep your copy. A request you cannot produce later is a conversation you lost.

USA.gov, on the front door I fetched, lists "Complaints" as its own topic, and "Laws and legal issues" as a topic for vital records, child support, and finding legal help. Those are finder's doors. They do not write the letter for you. The letter is this chapter's object.

A request is not a vote, not a warrant, and not a tax. It does not command the council to repair the spillway. It asks for the page that already exists. If the page does not exist, the clerk should say so in writing. "We do not have minutes of 7 April" is an answer. Silence is not an answer. If silence is what you get, the next desk may be the council, a public-records officer, or a court. Name the job before you climb.

The National Archives, whose front door I fetched this sitting, is where the nation keeps records that have left the agencies and entered the archive. The live page yielded a title, "National Archives," and not a body of counts I could quote. Inner counts are unverified. If the paper you want is a national record already archived, that door is the hunt. If the paper is last Monday's minutes, the clerk is the hunt. Do not send the clerk a founding parchment question. Do not send the Archives a pothole.

**Check.** Write a six-line request for the 7 April spillway minutes. Line 1: today's date. Line 2: your name. Line 3: the clerk of Stoneferry. Line 4: the record, with the 4 yes and 1 no. Line 5: copy on paper. Line 6: a street or an electronic address for the answer. Then cut line 4 down to "everything about the pond." Read both. Only the first line 4 can be fetched.

New case: you want a federal pamphlet on passports. Do not write the Stoneferry clerk. Open USA.gov, where "Get or renew a passport" is already a "How do I" on the front door I fetched. If you need a federal record that is not a pamphlet, open FOIA.gov and write the agency that holds it. Wrong desk, no page.

## 10. A check: find the office

Stand on Ferry Street with one job in your mouth: the pothole in front of number 14. Name the job, not the fog. "Someone should do something" is a fog. "The town should fill the pothole on Ferry Street at number 14" is a job. A job can be walked.

If the job is the town's, walk the hall or open the town's own site. The clerk's window is the first glass. If the clerk does not fill potholes, the clerk can name the public-works desk. Take the name. Write it. If the job is a county record, go inland to Ridge County. If the job is a state license, go to the state desk. If the job is national, open USA.gov.

USA.gov, fetched this sitting, is the national finder's door. Congress.gov is the door for a federal bill. Vote.gov is the door to join a register. The National Archives is the door for the nation's kept paper. The living civics pack is the door for the undergrad constitution survey. This book's door is the walk you just did.

Do the walk once on paper before you do it with your feet.

Job: pothole at 14 Ferry Street. Box: town. Desk: clerk, then public works. What you bring: the number 14, a photo if you have one, the date you saw the hole. What you do not bring: a speech about all roads.

Job: join the register. Box: state rules, national finder. Desk: Vote.gov, and USA.gov's "Check my voter registration." What you bring: your name and where you live. What you do not bring: a guess at a residence-day number this book did not fetch.

Job: a passport. Box: nation. Desk: USA.gov's passport task, which sat in the first four "How do I" lines on the front door I fetched. What you bring: the steps on that door. What you do not bring: the Stoneferry stamp.

Job: the spillway minutes of 7 April. Box: town. Desk: clerk. What you bring: the six-line request from chapter 9. What you do not bring: a net.

Job: the fence line. Box: court. Desk: the east wing on Court Street. What you bring: the map, the $35 teaching sum or the living sum on the glass, the date. What you do not bring: a council petition.

**Check.** Pick one job from the five above. Write the box, the desk, and what you bring, without looking back. Then look back. If your desk is "the government," you failed the check. Name a window.

New case: a neighbor on Mill Road wants the national constitution explained as a course. Do not start a founding survey on the clerk's glass. Name the living civics pack and stop. The clerk still files the parking hour. You still know which door is which. That split is the whole of this book.
