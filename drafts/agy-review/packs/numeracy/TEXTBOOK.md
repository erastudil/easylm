# Counting, place, share, and measure

If you can read this sentence, you can begin. Each chapter adds one move. Paper and a pencil do the work. The app's calculator may check a sum after you have tried it.

---

## 1. Names for how many

Hold out your fingers. Each finger is one. Two hands are ten ones.

We need names so we can talk when the pile is bigger than our hands. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine. After nine, we have run out of single marks. We bundle ten ones and call that bundle **ten**. Ten tens is **one hundred**. Ten hundreds is **one thousand**.

Write a pile as a row of digits. The rightmost digit is the leftover ones. The next digit left is how many tens. The next is how many hundreds.

243 is two hundreds, four tens, and three ones.

That is **place value**: the same mark, 2, means two, or twenty, or two hundred, depending on which chair it sits in.

**Check.** Write 508 in words. Five hundreds, no tens, eight ones. The zero is a chair that must stay empty so the 5 stays in the hundreds place.

---

## 2. Putting piles together

You have 7 and you are given 5. Count on from 7: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12. The new pile is 12.

When a place fills past nine, you bundle. 8 ones and 5 ones are 13 ones, which is 1 ten and 3 ones. If you already had 4 tens, you now have 5 tens and 3 ones: 53.

The written move is **addition**. The sign is $+$. The result is the **sum**.

```
  47
+ 36
  83
```

7 ones and 6 ones are 13: write 3, carry 1 ten. 4 tens and 3 tens and the carried ten are 8 tens.

**Check.** Addition may be done in either order: $47 + 36 = 36 + 47$. Undo it by taking the added pile back off. $83 - 36$ should return 47. If it does not, a place was bundled wrong.

---

## 3. Taking a pile off

You have 12 and you give away 5. Count backward, or think of 5 as the piece that was added to 7 to make 12. What remains is 7.

The written move is **subtraction**. The sign is $-$. The result is the **difference**.

If a place has too few, unbundle from the left. 30 take away 4: the ones place is empty, so open one ten into ten ones. Now you have 2 tens and 10 ones. 10 take away 4 is 6. Two tens remain. The difference is 26.

**Check.** Put the taken pile back. $26 + 4$ must be 30.

---

## 4. The same pile, again and again

Three bags of four apples is four, and four, and four. That repeated add is **multiplication**. The sign is $\times$. Three times four is twelve.

An array makes the same fact visible: three rows of four dots. You may also see four rows of three. Order does not change the count: $3 \times 4 = 4 \times 3$.

A place-value shortcut: $7 \times 10 = 70$. Multiplying by ten slides every digit one chair to the left and parks a zero in the ones place. Multiplying by one hundred slides two chairs.

**Check.** $6 \times 8$: six eights. $8 + 8 = 16$, plus 8 is 24, plus 8 is 32, plus 8 is 40, plus 8 is 48. Also $5 \times 8 = 40$ and one more 8 is 48.

---

## 5. Sharing a pile

Twelve apples in three bags, the same number in each bag. How many per bag?

You are undoing multiplication. $3 \times \text{?} = 12$. The missing number is 4. The written move is **division**. The sign is $\div$. $12 \div 3 = 4$.

Sometimes the pile does not split evenly. 13 apples in three bags: each bag gets 4, and 1 apple is left over. We write $13 \div 3 = 4$ remainder 1. Later, that leftover will become a fraction.

**Check.** Multiply back. $4 \times 3 + 1 = 13$.

---

## 6. A share of a whole

Cut a loaf into two equal pieces. One piece is one of two. We write $\frac{1}{2}$.

The bottom number, the **denominator**, is how many equal pieces the whole was cut into. The top number, the **numerator**, is how many of those pieces you are holding.

$\frac{3}{4}$ is three pieces of a whole that was cut into four.

Same amount, different cuts: $\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{6}$. You cut each piece smaller and hold more of them. The loaf did not change. That is an **equivalent fraction**.

To add shares of the same whole, the cuts must match. $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}$: cut the half into two quarters. Two quarters plus one quarter is three quarters: $\frac{3}{4}$.

**Check.** Draw the loaf. Shade. Count.

---

## 7. Tenths that sit to the right of a dot

Cut a whole into ten equal pieces. One piece is $\frac{1}{10}$. We also write $0.1$. The dot, the **decimal point**, marks the ones place. The first chair to the right is tenths. The next is hundredths. The next is thousandths.

$0.25$ is 2 tenths and 5 hundredths, which is $\frac{25}{100}$, which is $\frac{1}{4}$.

Money is already this system. One dollar is the whole. A dime is $0.10$. A cent is $0.01$.

**Check.** $0.3 + 0.12$. Line up the dots. 3 tenths is 30 hundredths. 30 hundredths and 12 hundredths are 42 hundredths: $0.42$.

---

## 8. Hundredths with a name

17 out of every 100 of the same whole. Write $\frac{17}{100}$, or $0.17$, or $17\%$. The third form is **percent**: per hundred.

A shop takes 20% off a $50$ price. 20 out of 100 is $\frac{1}{5}$. One fifth of 50 is 10. The discount is $10$. The new price is $40$.

**Check.** 20% of 50 two ways: $0.20 \times 50 = 10$, and $\frac{20}{100} \times 50 = 10$.

Do not add percents of different wholes as if they were the same pile. 10% of 100 and 10% of 10 are 10 and 1.

---

## 9. The other direction

A number line has a mark for zero. To the right, the counts you already have: 1, 2, 3. To the left of zero, the same steps the other way: $-1$, $-2$, $-3$.

Those marks are **negative numbers**. They are how we name a hole, a debt, a temperature below the chosen zero, a step backward.

Adding a negative is walking left. $5 + (-2) = 3$. Taking away a negative is walking right, because you are removing a left-step: $5 - (-2) = 7$.

**Check.** A thermometer at $3$ falls 5 degrees. $3 - 5 = -2$. Rise 2 degrees from there: $-2 + 2 = 0$.

---

## 10. How much of a thing, with a unit

"3" is not a length. "3 meters" is.

A **unit** is the agreed stick. The living international stick for length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance, and light is the SI, published by the BIPM. The US also uses miles, pounds, and gallons in daily talk. Convert before you add. 3 meters plus 50 centimeters is 3.50 meters, not 53 of anything.

NIST keeps the US legal conversions. One inch is exactly $25.4$ millimeters. One pound-mass is exactly $0.45359237$ kilograms. Fetch those doors when a number must travel.

**Check.** A shelf is 2 feet 6 inches. 2 feet is 24 inches. Total 30 inches. $30 \times 25.4 = 762$ millimeters.

---

## 11. Compared to what

Two bags, one with 2 red and 3 blue, one with 4 red and 6 blue. The second bag is a larger pile of the same mix. 2 to 3 is the same comparison as 4 to 6.

That comparison is a **ratio**. We write $2:3$ or $\frac{2}{3}$.

A **rate** is a ratio with units on both sides: 60 miles in 1 hour, 3 eggs per batch, 8 dollars per hour.

To scale a recipe, keep the ratio. If 2 cups of water go with 1 cup of rice, then 6 cups of water go with 3 cups of rice.

**Check.** Cross-multiply when two fractions claim to be the same: $\frac{2}{3}$ and $\frac{4}{6}$. $2 \times 6 = 12$ and $3 \times 4 = 12$. Equal products, equal ratios.

---

## 12. Area and volume as counting

A rectangle of 4 tiles by 3 tiles holds 12 tiles. That count is **area**. For a rectangle, area is length times width. Name the unit: square centimeters, square meters.

A box of 4 by 3 by 2 unit cubes holds 24 cubes. That count is **volume**. For a rectangular box, volume is length times width times height. Name the unit: cubic centimeters, liters. One liter is $1000$ cubic centimeters. BIPM owns the SI name.

**Check.** A room 5 m by 4 m. Area $20$ square meters. A rug 2 m by 3 m covers 6 of those 20.

---

## 13. Order of moves

When a line has more than one sign, people need a shared order so $2 + 3 \times 4$ is not a fight.

Do the multiply and divide as they appear, left to right. Then do the add and subtract, left to right. Groups in parentheses go first, because they are a pile you wrap.

$2 + 3 \times 4 = 2 + 12 = 14$. Not 20.

**Check.** Rewrite with a wrap if you mean the other reading: $(2 + 3) \times 4 = 20$.

---

## 14. A number you can trust on paper

Estimate first. $198 + 203$ is near $200 + 200 = 400$. If your careful sum is 301, a place was lost.

Last-digit check: ones of 8 plus ones of 3 are ones of 11, which ends in 1. The sum must end in 1.

Undo. Every add has a subtract. Every multiply has a divide.

When the number is a constant or a legal conversion, fetch NIST or BIPM. Do not finish that sentence from memory.
