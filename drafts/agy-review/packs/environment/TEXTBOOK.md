# Air, water, soil, and climate as a place you already live in

You can already read. This book is air, water, soil, and climate as a lived system: the tap, the garden, the dump, the rain on Mill Street, the same rain written down for ten years.

The object is one place you can walk. Pell Creek in this book is that place. A kitchen at 8 Willow. A bridge with a stick in the water. A school can that catches rain. A plant at the bend that takes what the drains send. Not a survey of the planet. Not a feeling that "the environment" is a mood.

The living earth_sciences pack in this library is the undergrad survey of rocks, plates, and the air as a science. This book names that door and stops. Biology owns cells and bodies. Geography owns maps of the whole Earth. Stay here when the task is to walk air, water, soil, and long weather as they sit on one street.

EasyLM is public general education.

---

## 1. Air

Stand on Mill Street at 07:10. A diesel truck idles at the dye works gate, even though the works is closed. You cannot see the four kitchen-lengths of air between you and the truck. You can smell them. Your eyes water. You walk two blocks to Willow and the smell thins. The same lungs, two blocks, two different rooms of air.

That room you move through, and take into your chest, is **air**. The name is a handle for what you already walked. You do not need a layer cake of the sky to use the handle. The undergrad stack of those layers lives in earth_sciences. Stop there.

Air is not empty. The truck put something into it. The wood stove at 8 Willow will put something into it in January. A burn pile behind the mill will put ash and a sharp smell into it on Saturday. You live in the mix. If you cannot smell a difference between the gate and Willow, you still live in the mix. Smell is one test. It is not the only test. A gas with no smell can still be in the room.

I fetched the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency home this sitting, at `epa.gov`. The page names the agency as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. It points at clean air, land, and water as work the agency claims. It did not print an air-quality index, a fine-dust limit, or a safe hour to run on Mill Street. Those figures are unverified here. Open that door when a number about outdoor air has to hold. A remembered color chart from a phone app is not the door.

The same home names careers, grants, and drinking-water pages. It does not replace your nose on Mill Street. The nose tells you the truck is here. The door tells you who writes a rule about what the truck may put out. Chapter 9 owns the putting-out. This chapter owns the room you are already in.

A closed window is a choice about which air you keep. In January the stove at 8 Willow makes the kitchen warm and the indoor air thicker. Open the window and Mill Street comes in. Close it and the stove's air stays. Both are air. They are not the same room.

**Check.** Write two rooms: the dye-works gate at 07:10, and the kitchen at 8 Willow with the window shut and the stove lit. For each room, write one fact you can get without a lab: smell, sting in the eyes, or steam on the glass. If both rooms got the same fact, you did not split them yet.

New case: Saturday, a burn pile, wind toward the school. The school is four blocks downwind. Write whether the school's air is the gate's air or Willow's air. If you wrote "the town's air" as one blob, walk the four blocks on paper until the two ends differ.

## 2. Water

Turn the tap at 8 Willow. Cold water hits the sink. Fill a clear jug to the 2-litre mark. The water looks empty. It is not empty. It came through a pipe. The pipe came from a plant, or from a well, or from a tank on a hill. Someone, somewhere, decided this jug was fit to drink.

That moving, filling stuff, in the jug and in the creek, is **water**. Rain is water. The barrel behind the house is water. Pell Creek under the bridge is water. The three are not the same jug. The tap is a promise about treatment. The barrel is last week's rain plus leaves. The creek is whatever the hills, the streets, and the plant at the bend sent it.

I fetched EPA's home this sitting. A strip on that page says you can learn how water gets to your drinking-water tap every day, the different types of public water systems, and other basic information about drinking water safety. The inner walk is at `/ground-water-and-drinking-water`. This sitting did not open that inner page. Counts of systems, and the legal limits for what may be in the tap, are unverified here. Open that door when the jug's promise has to be a rule, not a hope.

The same home says EPA announced nearly $1 billion in new funding to states to address PFAS in drinking water, and that it issued two proposed rules for public comment that uphold the National Primary Drinking Water Standards for PFOA and PFOS. Those two names, PFOA and PFOS, are chemicals the tap rule is currently fighting over. The page did not print the allowed amount in a litre. That amount is unverified here. If a neighbor says "the tap is fine because it looks clear," the look is not the standard. The standard lives on the EPA door.

A second jug: 2 litres from the rain barrel. Same volume as the tap jug. Different history. Do not mix them in the kettle and call the mix "water from 8 Willow." The kettle does not undo the leaves.

**Check.** Two jugs, 2 litres each. Label one tap, one barrel. Write one use that is safe to try with the tap jug in this book (drink, cook). Write one use for the barrel jug that does not need the tap's promise (garden, wash the step). If you drank the barrel because both jugs were clear, you trusted a look over a history.

New case: a well behind the barn, 8 metres from the south dump's fence. The well water is cold and clear. Distance is not a lab. Open the EPA drinking-water door before you call the well a tap. Until that door, or a test you can name, the well is unverified as drinking water in this book.

## 3. Soil

Kneel in the garden at 8 Willow. Push a trowel in. The first 20 centimetres come up dark, damp, and full of last year's roots. Below that the stuff turns paler and more like grit. A worm is in the dark layer. Nothing is moving in the grit you can see.

That layered stuff plants stand in, and that rain soaks into, is **soil**. Dirt on your shoes is soil that left its layer. Dust on the sill may be soil that the wind stole, or ash from the burn pile. The garden's dark 20 centimetres is the working bed in this book. The pale grit under it is still ground. It is a poorer bed.

I fetched the U.S. Geological Survey home this sitting, at `usgs.gov`. The page says the USGS began in 1879 to study the nation's lands and resources, and that today it monitors, analyzes, and predicts Earth's changing systems. It says a Cooperative National Geologic Map now covers 50 states and U.S. territories. That map is the national picture of what the ground is made of. This sitting did not open the map. What lies under 8 Willow on that map is unverified here. Open the USGS door when the question is the ground as a record. The trowel is still the walk for this garden.

The undergrad story of how rock becomes soil lives in earth_sciences. Name that pack and stop. This chapter needs the trowel, the 20 centimetres, and the fact that rain which hits the garden either soaks, sits, or runs to the street.

Pour a litre of the barrel water on a dry patch. Watch. If it disappears in a minute, the soil took it. If it ponds, the soil is already full or sealed. If it runs to Mill Street, the garden just sent water, and whatever was on the soil, toward Pell Creek. That run is how a garden joins a river. Chapter 8 will pick it up.

**Check.** Dark layer 20 centimetres. Pale grit under it. Write which layer you would plant a lettuce in, and why, using only what the trowel showed. If you wrote "soil" as one word with no depth, you skipped the cut.

New case: the south dump's edge, no garden, a crust on top, a sweet-chemical smell after rain. Do not trowel that crust into the lettuce bed. The dump's ground is a different layer with a different history. USGS can map the land. EPA can name a contaminated site. This sitting opened neither a dump record nor a soil-chemistry page. What is in that crust is unverified here.

## 4. A living place

Walk from the garden to the bridge. Cress in the ditch. A heron on the far gravel. Mosquitoes in the still pocket under the willows. A child fishing with a string. The tap, the soil, the air on Mill Street, and this wet edge are one place doing work for lungs, roots, and a stomach.

People name a place where living things share air, water, and soil a **living place**. Some classes say habitat. The class word is a handle. The walk is the work. You already have the garden, the ditch, the heron, and the child. You do not need a tree of kingdoms. Biology owns that tree. Name it and stop.

Kill the living place and the tap still runs for a while. The garden still looks like dirt. The heron leaves first. The mosquitoes may boom if the still pocket stays and the fish go. A living place is not a painting of nature. It is who is still here, using the same three rooms you just named: air, water, soil.

Pell Creek's mill pond used to hold fish you could count from the dam. In this book the pond is green in August and the fish are gone from the dam's eye. That is a fact about this pond, not a law for every pond. A neighbor who says "the pond is fine, it is still wet" is naming water and skipping the living place. Wet is not the same as lived-in.

The USGS home, fetched this sitting, lists Ecosystems as a research area and Water as real-time data. This sitting did not open the ecosystems page. Counts of herons, lists of mussels, and a score for "health" of Pell Creek are unverified here. Open USGS when the living place needs a count the eye cannot finish. Open EPA when a rule about dumping or air is the next step. Do not average a feeling of green into a score.

**Check.** Write three users of 8 Willow's block: a lettuce, a person at the tap, a mosquito under the willows. Next to each, write which room it uses most today: air, water, or soil. If all three got "the environment," you hid the rooms.

New case: the mill pond in August, green, no fish from the dam. Write one thing that is still there (water, algae you can see) and one thing that left (the fish you used to count). The two lists must differ. A third case: 12 lettuce plants, 1 rabbit, a fence with a hole. The rabbit is now a user of the soil room. Fix the hole or count 12 minus however many the rabbit took. Do not call the hole "balance."

## 5. Waste

Tuesday. Three trucks back up to the south dump. Bags, a cracked tub, a wet mattress, a can of something with no label. The town paid the trucks. The trucks left the bags. The bags do not vanish. They sit in a pile that rain will touch.

What you throw, pour, bury, or burn, once it has left the house, is **waste**. The dump is one door for solid waste. The drain at 8 Willow is another door: soapy water, food bits, whatever someone poured. That drain heads toward the plant at the bend, or toward a tank, or, if a pipe is broken, toward the ditch. Smoke from the Saturday burn pile is waste in the air. Chapter 1 already smelled it.

Waste is not a moral. It is a pile with a next stop. If you cannot name the next stop, you do not yet have the waste walk. "Gone" is not a stop. The truck was a move. The dump is a stop. Rain on the dump is a next move, into soil and, later, into water.

EPA's home, fetched this sitting, names remediating contaminated sites next to ensuring clean drinking water as work that "improves human health and the environment." It also names brownfield investments in a news line. This sitting did not open a Superfund list or a brownfield map for Pell Creek. Whether the south dump is a listed site is unverified here. Open EPA when a pile needs a legal name. The Tuesday trucks are still a count you can do with your eyes: 3.

A cracked tub in the creek is waste that skipped the dump. Pull it out if you can do it without becoming a second problem (chapter 1 of first aid owns the look at the room). The tub in the current is now the river's problem, which is chapter 8.

**Check.** Three trucks on Tuesday. Write the next stop for a bag in truck 1: the south dump. Then write the next stop after rain hits that bag: soil, ditch, or creek. If you wrote "away," you stopped one move too soon.

New case: a can with no label, poured in the sink at 8 Willow "to get rid of it." Name the door it used (the drain). Name the plant it may reach (the bend). Do not name the creek as clean because you cannot see the can. A third case: 1 wet mattress, 2 bags of leaves, 1 cracked tub. Leaves can go to the garden's soil if they are only leaves. The mattress and the tub cannot. Split the pile. A mixed pile is how the garden inherits the dump.

## 6. Weather as lived

The school keeps a metal can on a post, open to the sky. After one night's storm the janitor pours the can into a measuring glass: 12 millimetres. Mill Street is wet. The ditch is loud. The child at the bridge says the water is higher. You did not need a satellite to know it rained. You needed the street, the can, and a number on the glass.

That day's mix of rain, wind, heat, and cloud, as a body meets it, is **weather**. Weather is what you cancel a picnic for. Weather is the 12 millimetres. Weather is the wind that pushed Saturday's burn toward the school. Weather is the January morning the stove at 8 Willow stays lit.

I fetched `climate.gov` this sitting. The host sent me to NOAA's climate page at `noaa.gov/climate`. That page names weather, oceans, coasts, satellites, fisheries, and research as work the agency does. It did not print today's temperature for Pell Creek, a wind speed, or a rain total. Those figures are unverified here. The National Weather Service door in `LINK_INDEX.md` is the living forecast door. Open it for the next 12 hours. Do not finish a warning from a memory of last April.

NASA's home, fetched this sitting, listed a story titled "Summer Goes Out With a Heat Dome" on its Earth Observatory line. This sitting did not open that story. How hot the dome was, where it sat, and who it harmed are unverified here. The title is still a clue: heat that parks over a region is weather you can feel on a street, and it is also a thing NASA photographs from above. Use the street first. Then open the NASA Earth door if you need the picture from above.

The 12 millimetres is this school's teaching catch for one night, not a record for your town. If your school's can is empty after a night you thought was wet, believe the can, or find the leak in the can. A story about rain that cannot fill a can is a story.

**Check.** Night storm. Can reads 12 millimetres. Bridge water is higher. Write the two facts as two sentences. Do not merge them into "it was a big storm." The can is a depth. The bridge is a creek.

New case: the can reads 0 millimetres, the street is dry, the forecast on the NWS door said rain. The door was a forecast. The can is the catch. Keep both. Mark the forecast wrong for that night, or mark that the rain fell somewhere the can did not sit. A third case: 3 days, 4 millimetres, 0 millimetres, 9 millimetres. The three-day total is 13 millimetres. Day two was dry. A total is not a day.

## 7. Climate as long weather

The school has a drawer of rain sheets. Each sheet is one month of morning pours from the same can. Ten years of sheets sit in the drawer. One night's 12 millimetres is a line on one sheet. The drawer is the long view.

People name the long pattern of weather, in one place, over years, **climate**. Climate is not tonight. Climate is whether Pell Creek's summers in the drawer are mostly dry sheets or mostly heavy sheets. Climate is whether January now needs the stove at 8 Willow more nights than it did on the oldest sheets. One hot afternoon is weather. Ten years of hotter afternoons, if the sheets and a trusted thermometer say so, is the start of a climate claim.

A claim about climate needs a long record from a door that keeps one. NOAA's climate page, fetched through `climate.gov` this sitting, did not print a degree of warming, a year of record, or a millimetre of sea-level rise. NASA's home names a Climate Change door at `science.nasa.gov/climate-change` and an Earth Data door at `science.nasa.gov/earth/data`. This sitting did not open those inner doors. Every degree, every ppm, every "hottest year" you remember from a headline is unverified here. Open those doors. If two official pages disagree, say so. Do not average them.

NASA's home also printed that 1,000 museum visitors used NASA sea-level science on World Ocean Day. That 1,000 is a visitor count, not a sea-level number. It tells you NASA teaches sea level to the public. It does not tell you how many millimetres the sea rose at any tide gauge. Unverified, the millimetres, until you fetch a gauge door.

Pell Creek's drawer is a local climate tool if the can, the glass, and the morning hour stayed the same. If someone moved the can under a gutter in year 7, year 7 is a different instrument. Throw year 7 out of the long pile, or mark it. Climate cheats when the stick changes and you pretend it did not.

**Check.** One night: 12 millimetres. Ten-year drawer: you have not added the sheets yet. Write which of the two is weather and which is the start of climate. If you called the 12 millimetres "climate" because it felt historic, you used a feeling for a drawer.

New case: 2 years of sheets, not 10. Two years is a short stack. Do not print a climate claim from 2. Open NOAA or NASA for a long record, or keep collecting. A third case: a headline that says "Pell Creek's climate has changed" with no years and no can. Write unverified. Then open two doors from chapter 10.

## 8. A river

At the bridge a painted stick stands in Pell Creek. Yesterday the water cut the stick at 0.80 metres. This morning, after the 12 millimetres, it cuts at 1.40 metres. The creek took the rain, the ditch, the street, and the garden's runoff, and it rose 0.60 metres in a night. $1.40 - 0.80 = 0.60$.

That moving water with banks, a name, and a downhill, is a **river** in the ordinary sense, even when people here say creek. Pell Creek starts in the hills, takes Mill Street's drains, passes the plant at the bend, and reaches Hart's Ferry by afternoon of a fast day. The land that sheds water into it is the creek's own catch. You can walk a ridge and see which ditch goes this way.

I fetched USGS this sitting. The home lists Water as real-time data and points at `waterdata.usgs.gov`. That is the door for a gauge you did not paint yourself. This sitting did not open a gauge for Pell Creek, because Pell Creek is this book's town. Any living height for your own river is unverified here until you fetch that door, or read a stick you can see.

The 0.80 and the 1.40 are teaching marks on this book's stick, not a USGS reading. Use them to practice the rise. Then, for a real river, open waterdata. USGS also printed, on the same home, a Magnitude 6.4 earthquake 49 km NNE of Kainantu, Papua New Guinea, at 2026-09-20 09:17:36 UTC, with moderate shaking. That is a land fact from the same host. It is not Pell Creek's water. Earth_sciences owns the earthquake walk. This chapter takes the water door and stops.

A river is a conveyor. Waste from chapter 5, soil from chapter 3, and the living place from chapter 4 all ride it. Hart's Ferry drinks downstream. What Pell Creek puts in at the bend is not Pell Creek's secret. It is Hart's Ferry's tap tomorrow.

**Check.** Stick yesterday 0.80 metres, today 1.40 metres. Write the rise. Then write whether the garden's ponding litre from chapter 3 is part of that rise. If you said no because the garden is "on land," walk the ditch on paper from the garden to the bridge.

New case: the stick reads 2.10 metres and the bridge deck is 2.00 metres. The creek is now on the road. That is a flood on this stick. Move off the road. Open USGS waterdata and NWS for the living river near you. A third case: Hart's Ferry's tap, 1 day downstream. Write one thing Pell Creek can send them (treated plant water, dump rain, soil). If you wrote "nothing, that is their town," you cut the conveyor.

## 9. What a town puts out

Stand at the plant at the bend at 16:00. A pipe pours a steady stream into Pell Creek. The stream is clearer than the creek. It still has a faint soap smell. Upstream, Mill Street's drains after rain run brown. At the dye-works gate, the idle truck puts exhaust into the same afternoon. At the south dump, three Tuesday trucks have already left their bags. Pell Creek the town is a body that exhales.

What a town sends into air, water, and soil is its **output**. People in offices say emissions, effluent, runoff, trash. Those are four handles for four doors: stack or tailpipe, pipe to the creek, rain off streets and fields, bags to the dump. You do not need the office words to count the doors. You need the four walks.

EPA's home, fetched this sitting, is the national door for rules about those walks in the United States. It printed nearly $1 billion for states to address PFAS in drinking water, and it named National Primary Drinking Water Standards for PFOA and PFOS. Drinking water is what a town takes in, treated. Output is the other direction. The same agency owns both directions on that home: contaminated sites, drinking water, air, land, and water. This sitting did not open a permit for Pell Creek's plant. What the plant is allowed to pour, in numbers, is unverified here. Open EPA. Ask the clerk which permit the bend holds. Civic_life taught you the clerk.

NASA's Earth door, named on the NASA home this sitting, is how you see a town's output from above: heat, smoke, a river's color, a coast. This sitting did not open an image of Pell Creek. Any color you remember from a documentary is unverified as this town.

A town also puts out treated water, a library, and a paved street. Output is not only harm. It is what leaves. Count it honestly. The plant's clear stream may be the reason Hart's Ferry's tap is still a tap. The brown drain after rain may be the reason the mill pond lost its fish. Both can be true. Do not pick the flattering door and skip the brown one.

**Check.** Four doors: tailpipe, plant pipe, street rain, dump. Write one sentence for what leaves through each door on a wet Tuesday in Pell Creek. If two doors share the same sentence, split them until a stranger could stand at four different spots.

New case: the idle truck is gone, the plant pipe is shut for repairs, it is not raining, the dump is closed Sundays. Write which door is still open (the air over the dump, a leaky drain, a wood stove). A town does not stop putting things out because the gates are locked. A third case: 6 tanker trucks of treated drinking water enter town in a week, in this book's teaching count. That is input. Do not file it under output. The plant pipe is output. The tanker is supply.

## 10. A check: two doors on one place

Pell Creek is this book's town. Your town has a real name. Pick one place you can stand: a tap, a bridge, a dump gate, a school can. Then open two official doors on that same place, not two articles about the planet.

A third pair, if the question is weather tonight versus climate in the drawer: NWS for the next hours, NOAA climate or NASA's Climate Change door for the long record. NASA's home this sitting named Earth, Climate Change, Earth Data, and a heat-dome story it did not open here. `climate.gov` sent this sitting to NOAA climate and printed no degree you can quote.

Do the walk on paper for 8 Willow, then do it for a real corner.

Place: the tap at 8 Willow. EPA door: who treats public water, what a standard is, the PFAS fight named on the home (PFOA, PFOS, nearly $1 billion to states). USGS door: not the tap's legal limit. USGS is the creek's height, the land's map, the 50-state geologic map, the waterdata gauges. If you used USGS to bless the tap, you opened the wrong door for that job.

Place: the bridge stick. USGS waterdata is the living door. EPA is the door if the rise is sewage or a dump leak. NWS is the door if the rise is tonight's rain still falling. Three jobs, three doors. Place: ten years of school rain. The drawer is weather stacked until it might be climate. NOAA climate and NASA Climate Change are the long doors. One 12 millimetre night is not a climate door. NASA's 1,000 museum visitors on World Ocean Day are not a sea-level door.
