# A living place and who lives there

You can already read. This book is a living place and who lives there.

This pack owns the walks for a population, a food web, and a habitat. Lived air, water, soil, and climate belong to the environment pack. Name that door and stop. Fuel and a grid belong to the energy pack. Name that door and stop.

Load-bearing public numbers in these chapters come from four official pages: the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency at epa.gov, the U.S. Geological Survey at usgs.gov, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration at noaa.gov, and OpenStax Biology 2e at openstax.org/details/books/biology-2e. A number that did not appear on those pages is marked unverified, or it is a worked case invented for the arithmetic.

Public general education.

## 1. A living place

A ditch runs behind the mill at Reed Ford. Willow roots hold the bank. Green film sits on the slow water. Minnows tick the surface. A heron stands in the shallows at dusk. Mud, light, dead leaves, and those bodies are all in the same few metres.

That whole working patch, the wet and the bank and the lives that use them, is a **living place**. School books often write **habitat** for the patch a given life can actually use, and **ecosystem** for the patch plus the flows through it. The names can wait until the ditch is in your head. The object is the place that is doing the living, not a list of animal names on a poster.

Walk the ditch from the mill wheel to the stone culvert. Wren counts 40 metres of open water, then 12 metres of culvert, then 18 metres of open water again. $40 + 12 + 18 = 70$ metres of ditch in that walk. The culvert is dark. Minnows still pass through it. The living place did not stop at the shadow. It changed what light was available.

A second walk, Cedar Slope, is dry grass, aspen, and a seep. Same county. Different water, different light, different bodies. Do not average Reed Pond and Cedar Slope into one fake place. Two places. Two counts.

How the air on that bank feels to a person, how the ditch water tastes at a tap, how the mud sits as soil, and how the year's weather sits as climate, live in the environment pack. Name that door and stop. This chapter keeps who can live here, and what the patch is offering them: cover, food, a wet edge, a dry nest.

USGS's homepage lists an Ecosystems mission among the survey's research work, and it lists water data at waterdata.usgs.gov. Those are doors onto maps and gauges. They are not a substitute for standing at the millrace. Open them when a national map or a gauge reading must travel. The 70 metres are Wren's tape.

**Check.** Jo walks a second ditch: 22 metres of open water, 8 metres of pipe, 15 metres of open water. $22 + 8 + 15 = 45$ metres. A new case: the heron hunts only the 18 metres below the culvert. That 18 is the hunting patch for that bird this evening, not the whole 70. Another: a poster says "habitat means forest." Reed Pond is not a forest. The word names a usable patch, not one biome. If you added Reed Pond's 70 metres to Cedar Slope's grass and wrote one length, you glued two places.

## 2. Who eats whom

Wren stands at the millrace at dusk and writes who is eating. Green film on the stones. Mayfly nymphs graze the film. Minnows take the nymphs. A dragonfly nymph takes a minnow. The heron takes a minnow and, later, a frog. A mussel on the floor is filtering the same film the nymphs graze.

That map of who eats whom is a **food web**. A single line through it, film to nymph to minnow to heron, is a **food chain**. The chain is a path you can tell in one breath. The web is the real ditch, where most eaters have more than one food and most foods have more than one eater.

Reed Pond, invented for the paper, one July evening:

| eater | food written that evening |
|---|---|
| mayfly nymphs | green film |
| minnows | nymphs, green film |
| dragonfly nymphs | minnows, mayfly nymphs |
| mussels | green film |
| heron | minnows, frogs |
| frog | nymphs, minnows |
| raccoon | mussels, frogs |
| vole on the bank | willow bark |
| owl | voles |

The heron is not at the top of a ladder that makes it more real than a mussel. The heron is one eater with a long path behind it. Cut the green film and the nymphs, minnows, mussels, and heron all feel it, on different clocks.

Eighty minnows in the open water. A heron takes 11 this week. $80 - 11 = 69$ minnows if nothing else changes. Dragonfly nymphs take 7 more: $69 - 7 = 62$. The 80 was a count, not a promise. Births in chapter 4 will put fish back. This chapter only writes the eating.

A vole does not eat minnows. An owl does not eat willow. The bank and the water share one place and still split some meals. If you drew one chain and stopped, you missed the mussel, the raccoon, and the owl. Draw the extra arrows you can see. Leave a hole when you cannot see.

OpenStax Biology 2e is the undergrad door for named webs in a textbook. The details page that opened did not print a Reed Pond web, and it did not print a rule for how many arrows a web must have. Unverified as a school template. Wren's table is a worked case.

**Check.** Cross out minnows. Who loses a written food? The heron, the frog, and the dragonfly nymphs. Who still has another food on the table? The heron still has frogs. The frog still has nymphs. A new case: 16 mussels, raccoon takes 4. $16 - 4 = 12$ mussels. Another: someone draws film → nymph → minnow → heron and says that is the pond. It is one path. The mussel and the vole are missing. If you added the 11 minnows the heron took to the 7 the dragonflies took and wrote 80 still in the water, you put the eaten fish back without a birth.

## 3. Energy through a place

Morning light hits the green film on Reed Pond. By noon the film has grown. Minnows that ate film are heavier than they were at dawn. The heron that ate minnows is warmer after the digesting. Nothing in that sequence created a new pile from nowhere. Light arrived. Some of it was held in green bodies. Some of that held pile moved when an animal ate. The rest left as heat you cannot eat again.

That one-way walk of useful light through bodies is **energy through a place**. The environment pack owns how the air and the water feel. The energy pack owns fuel in a can and a town's grid. Name those doors and stop. This chapter keeps sunlight becoming body, then becoming another body, then becoming leftover warmth.

Toy units for the arithmetic, not a measured pond and not a textbook law. 100 light-units hit the water this morning. The green film and the pondweed hold 4. Minnows that eat that green hold 1. The heron that eats minnows holds 0.2. $4 + 1 + 0.2$ is not 100. The missing 94.8 did not vanish into a secret store. Most of the 100 never entered a body. Of the 4 that did, most left as heat and as waste before a minnow or a heron took a share.

A remembered school percent for how much passes from one eater to the next is unverified here. OpenStax Biology 2e is the door for that percent. The details page that opened did not print it. Do not finish 10 of 100, or any other official fraction, from memory.

Five equal mats of pondweed in the sun, each holding 0.8 of those toy units this morning: $5 \times 0.8 = 4.0$, the whole plant pile in the toy. Shade two mats with a fallen willow: those two hold 0.2 each instead of 0.8. Loss on those two: $2 \times (0.8 - 0.2) = 1.2$. The plant pile is now $4.0 - 1.2 = 2.8$. Less green held means less for the nymphs later, unless some other green makes up the gap.

The heron cannot eat the morning. It eats a body that already held a share. That is why a long chain is a thin chain. Each eating is a leak of heat. Stuff can loop in the next chapter. This useful light does not loop the same way. Tomorrow's sun is a new arrival.

**Check.** 80 light-units on a smaller pool. Plants hold 3. Minnows hold 0.6. Heron holds 0.1. The three held piles are $3 + 0.6 + 0.1 = 3.7$. A new case: six mats at 0.5 units: $6 \times 0.5 = 3.0$. Shade three of them down to 0.1: loss $3 \times (0.5 - 0.1) = 1.2$, remaining $3.0 - 1.2 = 1.8$. Another: a claim that each eater keeps 10 of 100 from the one below. Open OpenStax. Unverified here. If you added the heron's 0.2 to the plant 4 and called 4.2 the sunlight, you counted bodies as extra sun.

## 4. A population

Fourteen frogs sit on the north bank of Reed Pond in April. Wren counts again in May: 25. June: 33. July, after a dry spell and a busy heron: 27. Those four numbers are one kind of life in one place, counted at four times.

That count of one kind in a named place is a **population**. Fourteen frogs in April is not "frogs" as an idea. It is Reed Pond's frogs that month. Cedar Slope can have its own frogs. Do not add the two ponds and call the sum Reed Pond.

May's 25 is $14 + 11$. The 11 are births minus deaths minus frogs that left plus frogs that arrived, lumped because Wren only has the two counts. If 16 tadpoles made frog, 3 frogs died, and 2 left down the culvert, the net is $16 - 3 - 2 = 11$. Same 25. The inner split is a second measurement. Without it, write net change, not a birth story.

A place cannot feed an endless pile. Reed Pond, invented for the paper, can carry about 30 frogs through a dry August without the bank going quiet. June's 33 is already past that 30. July's 27 is back under it. School books often write **carrying capacity** for that limit. The limit is not a law of 30 for every pond. It is this pond, this food, this year. A wetter year moves the limit. A heron that stays all summer moves it too.

Twenty-two deer on Cedar Slope in November. Winter browse on that slope, invented, can feed 18 through March. $22 - 18 = 4$ deer that the slope cannot feed if nothing else changes. They leave, or they starve, or a hard winter does both. The 22 is not a moral. It is a count against a store of twigs.

OpenStax Biology 2e is the door for named growth curves. The details page that opened did not print a formula or a national wildlife total. Unverified here. USGS's homepage lists Ecosystems among its research missions. Open that mission when a surveyed population must travel. Wren's 14, 25, 33, and 27 are a tape and a notebook.

**Check.** August count 21 frogs. September 29. Net $29 - 21 = 8$. A new case: 12 voles in March, 19 in April, 17 in May. March to April net $+7$. April to May net $-2$. Another: 22 deer, winter food for 18. Someone says the slope can take 40 because a poster said deer bounce back. The poster is not Cedar Slope's twigs. If you added Reed Pond's 27 frogs to Cedar Slope's 22 deer and wrote 49 of one population, you mixed two kinds and two places.

## 5. A cycle of stuff

Last year's willow leaves lie on the pond floor. Worms and other small lives in the mud take them apart. The pieces do not leave the county. They sit in the water, in new film, in a nymph, in a minnow. A frog that dies on the bank goes the same way. Carbon, nitrogen, and the rest of the leaf's stuff are still in the place, in a new body or in the mud.

That looping of stuff through bodies, waste, mud, water, and air is a **cycle of stuff**. School books split it into a carbon cycle, a nitrogen cycle, a water cycle. The splits are maps of the same object: atoms that were in a leaf can be in a frog without any new carbon being minted on the bank.

Energy in chapter 3 arrived as light and left as heat. Stuff does not have to leave. A carbon in a willow leaf can still be in the county next October. That is the difference to hold. Do not treat the two walks as one walk.

Forty kilograms of dry leaves fall on the 70 metres of ditch, invented for the paper. Worms and the rest take 28 kilograms into the mud food by December. $40 - 28 = 12$ kilograms still recognizable as leaf. By April another 9 kilograms have gone to mud food: $12 - 9 = 3$ kilograms of tough bits still sitting. The 40 did not vanish. They changed form.

Lived water in a glass, lived soil in a garden bed, lived air on a street, and climate as long weather belong to the environment pack. Name that door and stop. NOAA's homepage opened with a storm over a green field. That photograph is weather arriving at a place. It is not a carbon total. No gigaton, no parts-per-million, and no global cycle number is printed here. Those figures are unverified. NASA Earth is a door in the index when an Earth-system number must travel.

A mill that takes ditch water and returns ditch water is still inside the cycle. Soap, oil, or a factory chemical riding that return is stuff too. Chapter 9 takes the human share. This chapter only insists the atoms did not disappear when the leaf disappeared.

**Check.** 25 kilograms of leaves. 17 into mud food by January. $25 - 17 = 8$ kilograms still leaf-shaped. A new case: 8 kilograms in January, 5 more into mud food by March. $8 - 5 = 3$ kilograms left. Another: a claim that the world's carbon in air is a remembered parts-per-million. Open NASA Earth or NOAA. Unverified here. If you treated the missing 17 kilograms as gone from Reed Ford, you lost the cycle. They are in the mud and in the film.

## 6. Competition

Two kinds of pondweed share the same three metres of sunlit water. One kind makes a mat at the surface. The other stands in columns from the mud. In May both are thin. In July the mat closes. Light at the columns drops. The columns yellow. Wren counts stems in six equal squares.

That use of one limited thing by two lives that both need it is **competition**. The limited thing can be light, nest holes, willow bark, or the same minnows. The two lives do not have to fight in a way you can film. The mat does not bite the columns. It takes the sun first.

Invented stem counts in those six squares, July:

| square | mat stems | column stems |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 11 | 2 |
| 2 | 9 | 3 |
| 3 | 12 | 1 |
| 4 | 8 | 4 |
| 5 | 10 | 2 |
| 6 | 9 | 3 |

Mat total: $11 + 9 + 12 + 8 + 10 + 9 = 59$. Column total: $2 + 3 + 1 + 4 + 2 + 3 = 15$. $59 + 15 = 74$ stems in the six squares. The mat has $59 \div 74$ of the stems, which is a large share. The columns still exist in every square. Competition did not have to mean zero of the second kind.

Five nest holes in the mill gable. Eight pairs of swallows try them. $8 - 5 = 3$ pairs without a hole in that gable. Those three nest in the barn, or they fail this round. The holes were the limited thing. The swallows did not become a different bird. The count of holes set the count of gable nests.

Cedar Slope in winter: 22 deer and 9 elk, both browsing willow and aspen. If the twigs can feed 18 deer-sized winters, and an elk takes about twice a deer's browse in this toy, then 9 elk take $9 \times 2 = 18$ deer-sized winters by themselves. The 22 deer have no remaining browse in that toy. Real slopes are messier. The arithmetic still shows the squeeze. Two populations can share a place and still starve one of them.

Competition is strongest when the two lives want the same scarce thing at the same time. A mussel and an owl hardly meet. A mat and a column meet all July in the same sun.

**Check.** Four nest holes, nine pairs. $9 - 4 = 5$ pairs without a hole there. A new case: mat stems 20, column stems 5, in two squares. Share of columns is $5 \div 25 = 0.2$, which is 20 of 100 stems. Another: 6 deer and 4 elk on a browse store of 10 deer-sized winters, elk at twice a deer. Elk take 8. Deer need 6. $8 + 6 = 14$, which is 4 over the store. If you wrote that the elk "won" and the deer must be zero, you skipped the leftover 2 deer-sized winters and the possibility of both going thin.

## 7. A role

At dawn a heron stands in the shallows and waits. It strikes. A minnow comes up. By noon the heron is gone to another reach. All day the mussels stay on the floor and pull water through. At dusk the heron may be back. The mussels were the ones that did not leave.

That work a life does in the place, the food it takes, the time of day, the strip of water or bank it uses, is a **role**. School books often write **niche** for that role plus the conditions the life can stand. Two lives can share Reed Pond and still not share a role. The heron is a hunter of larger moving bodies in the shallows. The mussel is a filter of small bodies in the water. The vole is a bark eater on the bank at night. The owl is a vole hunter in the air above that bank.

Seven heron strikes one morning. Three connect. $7 - 3 = 4$ misses. The role is still hunter. The 3 fish are the catch. A mussel does not strike. Counting strikes on a mussel is the wrong stick.

Sixteen mussels along the 18 metres below the culvert. Four of those metres are buried in silt from a collapsed bank. Mussels on those four metres smother. $16 \times \frac{4}{18}$ is not a clean head count. Wren finds 3 dead in the silt and 13 still filtering: $16 - 3 = 13$. The role stayed. The strip that can hold that role shrank.

A second heron on the same 18 metres at the same dawn is two bodies in one role. They may split the reach, or one may leave. That squeeze is chapter 6. A heron and a mussel on the same 18 metres is two roles. They can both stay. The heron even needs the pond to keep making minnows, which need the film the mussels also use.

Do not treat role as a job title the animal applied for. It is what you can point at: what it eats, when, where, and what eats it. If you cannot point, you do not yet have the role. Watch another hour.

**Check.** Nine heron strikes, four connect. Misses $9 - 4 = 5$. A new case: 20 mussels, silt covers half the bed. Wren finds 8 dead, 12 filtering. $20 - 8 = 12$. Another: owl and vole, same bank. Two roles, one food path. If you wrote that the owl and the heron have the same role because both hunt, you skipped the bank versus the shallows, and the vole versus the minnow. The hunt is a family of roles. The prey and the strip split them.

## 8. A disturbance

Fire ran through four hectares of Cedar Slope last August. Aspen trunks blackened. Grass went to ash. The seep still wet the low strip. In September Wren finds no standing aspen in the burned four hectares, and 11 unburned aspen on the far side of the track. In the third spring, 40 aspen suckers stand in the burn.

That jolt that resets who is living where, faster than the ordinary year, is a **disturbance**. Fire, a flood, a storm, a volcano, a hard freeze, a plague of beetles. The place is still the place. The cast changes. School books often write **succession** for the ordered return of lives after the jolt. The word is a handle for the suckers, the grass, and the later shade. It is not a promise that Cedar Slope will match a textbook drawing.

Four hectares burned. Two hectares of seep and track did not. $4 + 2 = 6$ hectares in Wren's Cedar Slope notebook. The 40 suckers sit on the 4. The 11 old aspen sit on the unburned side. Do not average 40 and 11 into "25.5 aspen for the slope." Two patches. Two histories.

USGS's homepage on 20 September 2026 listed volcano watches: Great Sitkin at orange watch, Shishaldin at yellow advisory, Kilauea at orange watch. Those are living volcanic places under a public alert. A lava flow, an ash fall, or a closed slope is a disturbance you can name from that door. This book does not print a hectare of burned Hawaiian forest, or a count of birds, from that homepage. Those figures are unverified. Open the volcano notice when the place and the date must travel.

NOAA's homepage opened with a storm over a green field. A storm is a disturbance. How that storm sits in a week's weather, and how climate sits as long weather, belong to the environment pack. Name that door and stop. USGS also listed Hurricane Readiness 2026 among its tools. A hurricane that knocks a dune or a marsh is a disturbance to a living place. Marsh plant counts from that story were not on the homepage. Unverified.

A flood at Reed Ford scours the 18 metres below the culvert. Mussels: 13 before, 4 after. $13 - 4 = 9$ gone from that strip, moved or dead. Film returns in days if light and nutrients remain. Mussels return on a slower clock, if they return. Fast lives fill first. Slow lives wait. That order is the succession the word was trying to name.

**Check.** Burned patch 4 hectares, unburned 2, a later cut of 1 hectare on the unburned side. Unburned left: $2 - 1 = 1$ hectare. A new case: 40 suckers in year three, 28 still alive in year five. $40 - 28 = 12$ lost. Another: Kilauea at orange watch on 20 September 2026. Someone says the watch means 400 hectares of forest gone. The homepage did not print 400. Unverified. Open USGS. If you averaged 40 suckers with 11 old aspen and wrote one population of 25.5, you mixed the burn with the trees the fire missed.

## 9. Humans in the place

The mill at Reed Ford takes water from the ditch and returns water to the ditch. Cal cuts 12 willow from a stand of 40 for stakes. $40 - 12 = 28$ willow remain. Voles that ate that bark now share 28 trees instead of 40. The owl hunts the same bank. The mill and the stakes are not outside the living place. They are a use of it.

That human use, take, return, and change is **humans in the place**. A town is a living place with extra pipes. A field is a living place with a crop chosen on purpose. An abandoned orchard is a living place whose chosen crop is no longer tended. The food web still runs. The roles shift.

Cal's 12 willow were a take. The mill's returned water is a return. Soap in that return is extra stuff in chapter 5's cycle. A road that shades 10 metres of ditch is a disturbance that stays. Count what changed: metres, trees, bodies, not a mood about whether people belong. People already belong. The question is what the counts do.

EPA's homepage names nearly 1 billion dollars in new funding to states to address PFAS in drinking water, and two proposed rules that uphold National Primary Drinking Water Standards for PFOA and PFOS. It also names a 251 million dollar WIFIA loan to upgrade drinking-water infrastructure and water reuse in California. Those lines are EPA's. They are about water people drink. Lived drinking water belongs to the environment pack. Name that door and stop. This chapter only needs the shape: a chemical that leaves a town can enter the water a living place is already using. Open EPA when the chemical, the rule, or the dollar figure must travel. Do not finish a parts-per-trillion from memory.

USGS began in 1879, on its own homepage, as part of the U.S. Department of the Interior, to study the nation's lands and resources. It now lists Ecosystems among the missions that monitor Earth's changing systems. That is the map-and-gauge door for places humans also use. NOAA is the ocean-and-weather door. OpenStax Biology 2e is the textbook door. NASA Earth is the planet-picture door. None of those homepages printed how many voles Cal's willow cut removed. Unverified. The 12 and the 28 are the notebook.

Jo plants 8 apple trees in the abandoned orchard and fences 0.6 hectares against deer. Inside the fence, 8 trees. Outside, Cedar Slope's 22 deer still browse. The fence split one place into two for those deer. It made a third role for the apples: a crop. Wasps, moths, and a fox will still write their own arrows. A food web does not wait for permission.

**Check.** Stand of 35 alder. Cut 9. Remain $35 - 9 = 26$. A new case: fence 0.6 hectares of 6.0 hectares of orchard edge. Unfenced $6.0 - 0.6 = 5.4$ hectares still open to deer. Another: someone says EPA's nearly 1 billion dollars is a count of dead fish. It is a funding line on EPA's homepage. Open EPA for the chemical and the rule. Count fish in the ditch with a net. If you added Cal's 12 willow to Jo's 8 apples and wrote 20 of one population, you mixed a cut with a planting.

## 10. A check

A national total, a chemical standard, a volcano alert, or a global cycle figure comes from EPA, USGS, or NOAA. A pond count you made with a tape stays on paper.

Walk a living place with paper:

1. Name the patch: Reed Pond, Cedar Slope, a named marsh, a named park. Write a length or an area you actually measured.
2. Write who eats whom. One chain is a start. Extra arrows make the web. A missing arrow is a hole, not a zero.
3. Count one population at two times. Net change is the second count take away the first. Births, deaths, and travel need their own counts.
4. Separate the two walks: useful light arrives and leaves as heat; leaf-stuff can loop.
5. Name a limited thing two lives both use. Count it.
6. Point at a role: food, time of day, strip of place.
7. If a fire, flood, storm, or volcano reset the cast, keep the patches separate. Do not average the burn with the trees the fire missed.
8. If a person cut, planted, fenced, or returned water, write that as a take or a return in the same units.
9. If the number is a standard, a national fund, an alert level, or a textbook percent, open EPA, USGS, NOAA, or OpenStax. If that host is dark, mark the figure unverified.

EPA's homepage: nearly 1 billion dollars for PFAS in drinking water; proposed rules on PFOA and PFOS standards; a 251 million dollar WIFIA loan in California. USGS's homepage: founded 1879; Ecosystems as a mission; water data; a cooperative geologic map covering 50 states and U.S. territories; Kilauea, Great Sitkin, and Shishaldin under volcano alerts on 20 September 2026. NOAA's homepage opened. It did not print a species total or a transfer percent. OpenStax Biology 2e's details page is the undergrad door. It did not print those percents either.

**Check.** Someone says Wren's 33 frogs in June is the U.S. frog population. Open USGS Ecosystems or a survey that actually counted. 33 is one invented pond. A new case: meter the ditch, 40 metres plus 18 metres of hunting water. $40 + 18 = 58$ metres of open water if you skip the culvert on purpose and write that you skipped it. Another: a remembered 10 of 100 passed from plants to minnows. Open OpenStax. Unverified here. A fourth case with no door: 29 frogs in September, 21 in August. $29 - 21 = 8$ net. That is taking away. If you fetched EPA to subtract 21 from 29, you walked into the wrong house.
