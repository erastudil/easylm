# A sentence that carries a claim

You can already keep a page. This book is the next skill: putting one thing you will stand on into a sentence another person can test.

The object of this book is that sentence. Not a pile of nice words. Not a feeling that you wrote today. One stand, in a row of words, with a next sentence that would let someone check you.

EasyLM is public general education. The app is AGPL-3.0-or-later. This pack teaches anyone who can read, and who has the study pack, how to write a sentence that carries a claim.

---

## 1. One claim

You tell a neighbor the downstairs tap is dry. The neighbor can walk downstairs and try the tap. You have put one thing in the air that can turn out true or false.

"The house is a mess" does a different job. A mess according to whom. Which room. What would count as clean. The neighbor cannot go and settle it. You spent words and left a fog.

Hold those two side by side. The tap sentence names a place and a state. The mess sentence names a mood. Only the first one is a stand you can be held to.

People name that stand a **claim**. The name arrives after you already have the tap and the fog. A claim is one thing you are willing to be wrong about.

Three cousins sit nearby and are not claims. "Close the downstairs tap" tells someone to act. "Is the downstairs tap dry?" asks. "I hate this house" reports a feeling. None of those three can be true or false in the way the tap sentence can. A command can be obeyed. A question can be answered. A feeling can be honest. A claim can be checked.

A claim can be small. "This jar holds 48 dry beans" is small and checkable. Empty the jar. Count. A claim can be dated. "The roof leaked on four nights in March" is dated and checkable if you kept a pan and a calendar. Size does not make a claim. Being willing to be wrong does.

A claim can fail. The tap runs. The jar holds 47. The roof was dry on the night you named. Failure is not a scandal. Failure is how a claim earns the next sentence. A fog cannot fail, because it never stood.

**Check.** Write two sentences about a shop on Pine that locks its door at 18:00. One names the shop and the hour. One says the street feels inconvenient. Hand both to a person who has not seen the street. Only the first sentence tells that person what to go and see.

New case: a shelf in a library. "This shelf holds 12 copies of the same title" is a claim. "This shelf is well stocked" is a fog. Count the copies. If you find 11, the first sentence is false and useful. The second sentence has nowhere to land.

## 2. A sentence

Say this out loud: the clinic on Pine is closed Tuesday. Someone or something is in the first chair. An act sits in the second chair. Tuesday is when. A listener can go to Pine on Tuesday and read the door.

Now say: closed Tuesday. The act and the when are there. The someone is missing. A listener has to guess which door. The words are doing half a job.

The row that names a someone and an act, and finishes, is a **sentence**. The someone is the **subject**. The act is the **verb**. Those two names are handles for chairs you already saw. "The clinic" sits in the subject chair. "is closed" sits in the verb chair.

A row can have both chairs and still fail to finish. "The clinic on Pine, which last winter" hangs. The listener is still waiting for the act to land. A row can finish twice in one breath. "The clinic is closed Tuesday, the bus still stops at Oak." That is two stands glued with a comma. Split them. Each stand gets its own sentence.

Length is not the test. "Stop." can be a sentence in a sign, with the someone implied by the post it sits on. "Various local service arrangements may be subject to periodic adjustment depending on evolving operational considerations." has many words and no one you can visit. Count the words if you like. Then ask who, and what act, and what a stranger would go and see.

Seven words that work: "The clinic on Pine is closed Tuesday." Seven words of fog: "Things around here feel somewhat inconvenient today." Same length. Different jobs. The first sentence has a who, an act, and a when. The second has a mood and a day.

**Check.** Take the fragment "closed Tuesday." Put a someone in the first chair and finish the act. Write the new row. Then write a second row of the same length that names no one and no act. Read both aloud. The first row should send a person to a door. The second should not.

New case: "This shelf holds 12 copies." Count the words: This, shelf, holds, 12, copies. Five words, one claim, a number a hand can test. Write a five-word fog about the same shelf. If your fog still names 12 copies, you wrote the claim again. Change the 12 to a mood word and watch the stand vanish.

## 3. A paragraph

One claim can take more than one sentence. The roof leaked on four nights in March. That is the stand. The next sentences have work to do: show the stain, name the nights, say what you did not check.

A cluster of sentences that all serve one stand is a **paragraph**. The first sentence often carries the claim. The middle sentences put evidence next to it, or bound it. The last sentence does not open a second shop.

Write the roof.

The roof leaked on four nights in March. A brown stain spread on the ceiling above the stairs. I set a pan under it and emptied the pan on the 3rd, the 11th, the 19th, and the 28th. The 12th was dry. I did not check April.

Five sentences. One stand. The 12th is not a second claim. It bounds the first: four nights, not every night. April is a bound too. A reader who wants May has to go and look. You did not pretend you had May.

Now add "The rent is high." That sentence is a second shop. It may be true. It does not serve the leak. Put it in a new paragraph or cut it. A paragraph that tries to sell two stands at once will drop one of them on the floor.

A paragraph can be two sentences if those two do the job. A paragraph of eight sentences that wander from the stain to the rent to a memory of a storm in another year is a tour. The study pack taught you to bring a question to a page. Here you bring one claim, and you do not leave it.

**Check.** Write four sentences about a jar of dry beans. All four must serve this stand: the jar held 48 beans at noon on Monday. One sentence is the stand. One is the count (tens, then leftovers). One is a bound (you did not weigh them). One is what would make the stand fail (a bean under the table). If a fifth sentence starts "Beans are a healthy food," you opened a second shop. Cut it.

New case: the same four sentences, plus "The lid is blue." The lid may be blue. Ask whether the blue lid helps a reader test 48. If it does not, it does not belong in this paragraph. Color can wait for a paragraph whose claim is about the lid.

## 4. Who it is for

The same closed clinic, two notes. On the staff fridge: "Pine closed Tuesday. Use Oak. Take keys." On the public door: "The clinic on Pine is closed Tuesday. The clinic on Oak is open 9:00 to 17:00. Bring your card."

Same facts. Different people must act. The fridge note can be short because the staff already know the buildings. The door note names the buildings, the hours, and the card, because a stranger is standing on the street with one chance to read.

The person who must act is the **reader**. Writers also say **audience**. The picture is the fridge and the door. The name is a handle for "who has to do something after they read this."

A note to yourself can use a private word. A note to a landlord cannot. A note to a child cannot use a word the child does not have. You do not make the claim softer for the child. You pick words the child can act on. "The downstairs tap is dry" works for a child. "Domestic potable supply interruption at fixture 2" does not, unless that child is already a plumber.

Federal writers in the United States have a statute for this move. The Plain Writing Act of 2010 requires that content for the public be written for its specific audience. I fetched the living plain-language guide on Digital.gov, which is where `plainlanguage.gov` now sends a browser. That page says plain language is content that is clear and easy to understand, that it is also the law, and that the 2010 Act is the requirement. The four guide titles on that page are principles, writing for understanding, design for understanding, and test for understanding. You may not be a federal writer. The move is still: name who must act, then write so that person can.

A page that tries to speak to everyone at once often speaks to no one. Pick the person at the door, or the person at the fridge, and stay with that person for the paragraph. If you need both, write two paragraphs.

**Check.** Rewrite "The downstairs tap is dry" for a roommate, then for a plumber. The roommate note should say when you noticed and what you already tried (the upstairs tap still runs). The plumber note should say which fixture, which floor, and that the upstairs tap still runs. Both notes keep the same claim. If the plumber note drops the dry tap and only says "please come," you wrote a request and lost the stand.

New case: library hours. A child is at the door. A cataloger is at a desk. Write one sentence for each. "The reading room closes at 17:00" can serve the child. The cataloger may also need the calendar exception ("closed Tuesday"). If you wrote "Hours may vary" for both, you wrote a fog twice.

## 5. Evidence next to the claim

A neighbor can believe you or not. The next sentence is how you stop asking for belief and start offering a check.

"The roof leaked on four nights in March." That is the claim. "I emptied a pan on the 3rd, the 11th, the 19th, and the 28th." That is the next sentence. The dates sit beside the stand. A skeptic can ask to see the calendar and the stain. You have not proved the weather of the whole city. You have put something next to your four.

The next sentence that would let someone test you is **evidence**. Evidence is not a mood that agrees with you. "Everyone knows the roof is bad" is a chorus. It is not a check. A count, a date, a named door, or a thing a person can point at: those are checks.

Empty the jar. Count by tens. Ten, twenty, thirty, forty, and eight leftover. The jar held 48. The evidence is the count, done in a way another person could repeat. If you skip the leftover eight and write 40, you changed the claim. If you write 48 and hide the count, you asked for belief again.

A number that came from the world needs a home. I fetched Merriam-Webster's front door for this book. The banner on that page said 1,400 new words in the dictionary. I am not building a linguistics paper on that banner. I am using it as a worked case: a page you hold says "the dictionary added 5,000 words this year." Open the Merriam-Webster door in `LINK_INDEX.md`. The banner I saw said 1,400. Mark the page. If the door will not open, write unverified beside 5,000 and do not build on it.

The Library of Congress front door, fetched in the same sitting, showed a free-to-use gallery for the Centennial Exposition of 1876 in Philadelphia, named there as the first official world's fair held in the United States. A sentence that says "the first US world's fair was in 1976" is a claim with a wrong year. The door has 1876. Believe the door. I did not count the pictures in that gallery, so a sentence that says how many pictures sit there is unverified here.

If the sentence is not yours, do not stand on it as if it were. Purdue OWL's front door, fetched for this book, lists a guide on avoiding plagiarism among its popular resources, next to its MLA guide, its APA guide, and a resume guide. I did not fetch the inner plagiarism page, so I am not printing their examples. The move in this chapter is smaller: name whose sentence it was, or do not use it as your stand.

**Check.** Write the 48-bean claim. Write the count next to it, in tens and leftovers. Then change the leftover from 8 to 7 and watch the claim become 47. If your evidence still says 48, the evidence and the claim have come apart. They must move together.

New case: "The dictionary added thousands of words." Open Merriam-Webster. The banner I fetched said 1,400, which is thousands, and is also more precise. Replace "thousands" with 1,400 and name the door. If you cannot open the door, write unverified and stop. Do not upgrade "thousands" to 1,400 from this paragraph's memory on a later day. Fetch again.

## 6. A word that does work

Change one word and the whole stand can fall.

"The bus stops at Oak." A rider can wait at Oak. "The bus might stop at Oak." The rider no longer knows whether to wait. "Might" is a small word doing a large job. It turned a stand into a hedge.

"Dogs on leashes only." The word *only* is doing work. Without it, "Dogs on leashes" can be read as a description of some dogs, not a rule about all of them. With it, the sign is a rule: if the dog is here, a leash is here. Swap *only* for *welcome* and the rule vanishes.

A word that, if swapped, changes what you are standing on is a **working word**. The study pack called a cousin of this a load-bearing word on a page you read. Here you are the one placing it. Put it on purpose.

Some words look busy and do no work. "Very" in "the tap is very dry" does not change the test. The tap gives water or it does not. "Really," "quite," and "needless to say" are the same family. They raise the volume. They do not change the stand. Chapter 7 will cut them. This chapter is about the ones you must not cut.

"All" and "never" and "always" are working words. "The roof never leaked in March" is a different stand from "The roof leaked on four nights in March." One failed dry night kills the first. Four named nights, with a dry 12th beside them, can survive. If you do not mean all, do not write all.

For ordinary English, Merriam-Webster is the dictionary door in `LINK_INDEX.md`. Open it when a working word is one you cannot point at. I did not fetch the entry for *claim* or for *literally* this sitting, even though the grammar index on that front door links a page on *literally*. I am not printing a remembered definition. Look the word up. Copy one sense you can point at. Then come back to your sentence.

A subject word may need a subject door. "Work" in a physics pack is not "work" in a job listing. Unicode, in the link index, is the door for how a letter, a space, or a mark is stored as a code. I did not fetch the living Unicode version number this sitting. If a character must be named as law, open that door. Do not invent a version.

**Check.** Take "The bus stops at Oak." Swap *stops* for *passed*. Write what a rider should now do. Then swap *stops* for *might stop*. Write what a rider should now do. The two swaps must not produce the same instruction. If they do, you are not hearing the working word.

New case: the clinic sentence. Swap *closed* for *busy*. A closed door and a busy waiting room ask a reader to do different things. Write both sentences. Name the working word in each. If you cannot name it, you do not yet have it.

## 7. Cut

Some words can leave and the stand still holds.

"It is important to note that the clinic on Pine is closed Tuesday." The stand is the closed clinic. "It is important to note that" is a throat being cleared. Cut it. "The clinic on Pine is closed Tuesday." Same who, same act, same when. Thirteen words became seven.

A longer specimen. "At this point in time it is necessary that all visitors should proceed to the front desk in order to sign the book." Count them: twenty-three. The stand is that visitors sign the book at the front desk. Cut to: "Visitors, sign the book at the front desk." Eight words. The request is still a request. The place is still the front desk. The book is still the book.

The act of taking out words that do no work is a **cut**. You are not making the claim smaller. You are taking the padding off the claim so a reader can see it. If a cut changes who, or the act, or a number, you cut a working word. Put it back.

"In order to," "at this point in time," "it is necessary that," "please be advised that," and "the fact of the matter is" are padding in the specimens above. They can be useful in a letter that must slow a reader down on purpose. They are not useful when the reader is at a door and has one chance.

The living plain-language guide on Digital.gov, the same door as in chapter 4, says plain language is more efficient and effective, and that it is the law for federal public content. I did not fetch the inner "writing for understanding" page this sitting, so I am not listing their checklist from memory. The cut you can do on paper is the lesson: count the words before, count the words after, and keep the stand.

A paragraph can be cut the same way. Six sentences that serve the 48-bean claim, plus two that praise beans as food, become four if you drop the praise. The claim is not lonelier. It is easier to test.

**Check.** Copy this row: "At this point in time it is necessary that all visitors should proceed to the front desk in order to sign the book." Write 23 above it. Write your cut. Count the cut. The who, the act, and the desk must remain. If your cut is "Sign." you lost the desk and the book. If your cut is still 23 words, you copied.

New case: the fridge note from chapter 4. "Pine closed Tuesday. Use Oak. Take keys." That is eight words and three acts. Add padding until you have twenty words. Then cut back to eight or fewer without losing Pine, Tuesday, Oak, or the keys. If Tuesday disappears, the staff will come to a locked door on the wrong day.

## 8. A title that is true

A folder named Stuff hides the file you need. A folder named "Clinic closed Tuesday" puts the stand on the tab. You do not have to open every file to find the closed day.

The short line that names the claim, not a mood, is a **title**. A title is not a decoration. It is the claim in a form a stranger can use to decide whether to read the next sentence.

"Notes" is a mood for a folder. "Thoughts on today" is a mood. "Downstairs tap dry 7-9 April" is a title that is true if the paragraph underneath stands on those dates. "The tap is always broken" is a title that overclaims if you only watched three days.

The Library of Congress, on the front door I fetched, titles works so a stranger can find them. One lithograph in the 1876 gallery is titled "View of the Ground and Buildings, International Exhibition, Fairmount Park, Philadelphia." That line names a view, a place, and an exhibition. A mood title for the same picture would be "Old Fair Stuff." The long title is doing the job of a claim: you know what you will see. I did not fetch the Library's cataloging rulebook this sitting, so I am not quoting a cataloger's handbook. The picture on the homepage is enough for this check: the title that names the thing beats the title that names your feeling about the thing.

A title can be too small. "Beans" above a paragraph that claims 48 at noon on Monday hides the number and the time. A title can be too large. "All jars in the kitchen hold 48 beans" above a paragraph that counted one jar on one Monday is a new claim the paragraph did not earn.

School and press houses have styles for titles of other people's work, and for how to mark a source. The MLA Style Center, the Chicago Manual of Style, and Purdue OWL's MLA and APA guides are the doors in `LINK_INDEX.md`. I fetched OWL's front door, not those inner pages, so I am not printing a sample reference list. When a teacher names a house style, open that door. This chapter's job is smaller: your own title must be true of your own paragraph.

**Check.** Write a four-sentence paragraph whose claim is that the jar held 48 beans at noon on Monday. Title it. A failing title is "Notes" or "Kitchen." A working title names 48, the jar, and Monday. Then write a second title that says the jar always holds 48. That second title is false if the paragraph only counted once. Mark it.

New case: the 1876 gallery. Title a two-sentence note about one picture you can actually name from the Library door. If you cannot open the door, write unverified and title a note about the downstairs tap instead. Do not title the tap note "World's Fair." That title belongs to a different stand.

## 9. Two voices

Text a roommate: "Tap's dry again. Since Monday." Write the landlord: "The downstairs tap gave no water from Monday 7 April through Wednesday 9 April. The upstairs tap still runs. I shut the valve under the sink on Wednesday evening."

Same stand. Same three dates. Two ways of speaking. The roommate already knows which tap and which Monday. The landlord does not live in the house, and may have to send a person with a wrench. The landlord letter keeps the who, the act, the dates, and what you already did. The roommate text keeps the stand and trusts shared memory for the rest.

The way the same claim sounds for two readers is **voice**. People also say **register**. The picture is the two notes. You are not putting on a costume. You are matching the person who must act, which is chapter 4 done with the sound of the sentences.

A fake-fancy voice is a different failure. "Be advised that the aforementioned fixture has ceased to yield potable supply" is not more true than "The downstairs tap is dry." It is padding with a hat on. Cut it. A too-small voice is a failure the other way. "It's broken lol" to a landlord drops the dates and the upstairs tap. The landlord cannot send a wrench on that.

Both voices must keep the working words. If the roommate text says "might be dry" and the landlord letter says "gave no water," you wrote two claims. Pick the one you will stand on, then dress it for the reader.

Public writing for a specific audience is the job the Plain Writing Act of 2010 named for federal pages. Project Gutenberg is a door to whole books whose copyright term has ended, so you can hear how a sentence sounded in public for a long time. I did not fetch Gutenberg this sitting, so I am not naming a title or a sentence count from that shelf. Open it when you want to hear a voice that is not yours and not this book's. Copying a sentence from that shelf into your letter as if you made it is the plagiarism move from chapter 5. Hearing it is legal.

**Check.** Take the leak from chapter 3. Write three sentences to a roommate and three to a landlord. Both sets must keep four nights in March and the pan. The roommate set may use "again" if you share the history. The landlord set must name the stain and the four dates. If the landlord set drops the 28th, you changed the claim.

New case: a public notice and a note to yourself about the clinic on Pine. The public notice names Tuesday and the Oak clinic and the hours 9:00 to 17:00. The note to yourself can say "Pine shut. Oak. Card." Read them both. If the public notice is as short as the note to yourself, a stranger at the door will stall.

## 10. A check: read it aloud

Read the sentence with your mouth. Not with your eyes sliding. Out loud, to a wall or a person or a phone that will play it back.

Where you stumble, a reader will stall. Where you run out of breath, you probably glued two stands. Where you hear yourself add a word that is not on the page, the page is missing a working word and you have been supplying it in your head.

The ear is the last instrument. People name this check **reading aloud**. The name is almost the act. Do the act.

A sixteen-word row that carries two stands: "The downstairs tap gave no water from Monday 7 April through Wednesday 9 April and the upstairs tap hissed air Thursday morning." You will want a breath at *and*. That breath is the split. Two sentences: "The downstairs tap gave no water from Monday 7 April through Wednesday 9 April." Then: "The upstairs tap hissed air Thursday morning." Two claims. Two checks. The first is dryness for three dates. The second is a hiss on a fourth morning. They can live in one paragraph. They cannot live in one breath without a hitch.

Read a cut. The twenty-three-word visitor sentence from chapter 7 should be harder to say than the eight-word cut. If the long one is easy and the short one is hard, you cut a working word and the tongue is trying to put it back.

Read a title. "Stuff" out loud tells a listener nothing to go and see. "Clinic closed Tuesday" does. If you are shy to say your title to a person standing next to you, the title may be a mood you do not want to be held to. Change the title until you can say it.

Purdue OWL, on the front door I fetched, offers help to students, teachers, professionals, and organizations, in person on that campus and in public online. I did not fetch a tutoring-hour table, so a sentence about how many tutors sit there is unverified. The door is a place to walk when a school asks for a citation style this book did not print. The check in this chapter does not need that door. It needs your mouth and a sentence you wrote.

**Check.** Write one sixteen-word sentence about the downstairs tap. Read it aloud. If you need a breath in the middle, split it at the breath. Count the words in each new sentence. Each new sentence must still have a who and an act. If a half has no act, you split in the wrong chair.

New case: open chapter 3. Read the five-sentence roof paragraph aloud. Mark one sentence you would cut. Cut it. Read the rest. The stand must still be four nights in March, with dates a person could check. If the 28th disappeared with your cut, put that sentence back. The mouth found a working piece. That is the whole of the check.
