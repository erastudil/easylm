# Poetics & Prosody — Metric Systems, Scansion, Stanzaic Architecture & Closed Verse Forms

A comprehensive undergraduate textbook exploring the theory and craft of verse: acoustic prosody and syllabic stress, metric scansion (accentual-syllabic, quantitative, and moraic systems), rhyme mechanics and phonetic sound-binding, lineation and enjambment, figurative imagery, classical closed forms (the Petrarchan and Shakespearean sonnet, villanelle, sestina, haiku), the formal architecture of free verse, and comparative world poetics.

---

## 0. Syllabus & Structural Map

Poetry is human language organized under conscious metric, acoustic, and spatial measure. In prose, the line break is an arbitrary typographic accident determined by page margins; in poetry, the line is a load-bearing architectural unit. The word *verse* derives from the Latin *versus*—the turning of an ox and plow at the edge of a furrow. By interrupting the forward rush of syntax with metric turning points, caesuras, and acoustic rhymes, poetry compresses multidimensional cognitive, emotional, and sensory meaning into rhythmic time.

## 1. The First Principles of Poetic Form and Measure

**Poetry** is language arranged so that **line, sound, and image** do work a paragraph does not. A song lyric is a cousin (`../music/`). A novel in verse still answers to the line.

Four questions, every time:

1. **line or sentence** — which is the working unit?
2. Is there **meter**, **rhyme**, **free**, or another grammar?
3. What **form** (sonnet, haiku, lyric, epic, occasional)?
4. The line you would quote — did you **fetch** it?

**Poem** vs **text** vs **performance** vs **translation**. They are four objects. A remembered stanza is not a source.

**Verse** is the lined stuff. **prose poetry** keeps prose’s sentence and steals poetry’s pressure — name which contract you are in.

**Check:** if you cannot point to the line break, you are doing theme-talk, not poetry.

## 2. Syllabic Stress, Morae, and Phonetic Articulation

Before meter, name the **sound-unit** of *this* language.

| Unit | Is |
|---|---|
| **phoneme** | Contrast that makes a different word |
| **syllable** | A beat of vowel-centered sound (English school default) |
| **stress** | Relative prominence. English meter usually counts this |
| **mora (on)** | Timing unit in Japanese verse. Not the same as an English syllable |
| **quantity** | Long vs short syllable. Greek and Latin school meters |

**IPA** is the map of phones. Door: internationalphoneticassociation.org. Do not invent a transcription.

**Prosody** (linguistics) is stress, timing, intonation in speech. **prosody** (poetics) is how verse organizes those. Same word, two jobs — say which.

Music pack owns Hz. Here the ear counts **relative** prominence and grouping.

**Check:** which language. Which unit you are counting.

## 3. Metric Scansion: Mapping the Architectural Foot

**Meter** is a repeating count. **scansion** is marking it. A **foot** is a small repeating cell in the European school (iamb, trochee, anapest, dactyl, spondee as *names*). **iambic pentameter** is five iambs as the English school default — **fetch the line** before you declare a poem regular. Substitution, feminine ending, and headless lines are normal, not “errors.”

**Accentual** verse counts stresses, not every syllable (Old English alliterative as a named ancestor). **syllabic** verse counts syllables. **accentual-syllabic** counts both (the Shakespearean school mix). **quantitative** counts length (Latin hexameter as a named drill). **moraic** counts mora (ch 9).

**Caesura** is a cut inside the line. **catalexis** is a missing slot at the end. Names, not crimes.

Do not export English iambs into a language that does not stress that way. Do not count Japanese haiku in English syllables and call it the Japanese rule.

**Check:** which counting system. Scansion of *this* line, fetched.

---

## 4. Sound-Binding: Rhyme, Assonance, and Acoustic Resonance

**Rhyme** is identity (or near-identity) of the end-sound. **perfect / slant / eye-rhyme** as school cuts. **rhyme scheme** is a map (ABAB as a pattern-name). Fetch the stanza before you tattoo a scheme on a title.

**Alliteration** repeats initial sounds. **assonance** repeats vowels. **consonance** repeats consonants. **onomatopoeia** imitates a non-speech sound — a small tool, not a personality.

**Refrain** is a returning line. **rime riche**, **internal rhyme**, **leonine** as extra names — fetch a glossary if the job is a label.

Sound-bind is not decoration when it groups words the grammar would not. If it only jingles, say so.

**Check:** end-rhyme, head-rhyme, or inside the line. Scheme fetched from the page.

---

## 5. The Line as Primary Unit: Stanzas, Enjambment, and Caesuras

The **line** is poetry’s cut. A **sentence** may run across many lines (**enjambment**) or stop with the line (**end-stopped**). The tension between syntax and line is half the craft.

**Stanza** is a group of lines with a relation (couplet, tercet, quatrain as count-names). **white space** on the page is a time-instruction for the eye and often the voice.

**Indent, drop, concrete shape.** a picture made of words is a form; it still has to be read.

**Line length** in the English school is often named by feet (tetrameter, pentameter). In free verse it is a rhythm choice, not a vacant default.

**Check:** where the line breaks. Where the sentence breaks. They are not always the same place.

## 6. Poetic Imagery, Metaphor, and Sensory Compression

**Image** is sensory language doing a job (show a world, fuse two things, stall the eye). If it does no job it is slack.

School figures (fetch a glossary before you stack six on one clause):

| Figure | Job |
|---|---|
| **metaphor** | Speak of A in terms of B |
| **simile** | A like / as B (a flagged comparison) |
| **metonymy** | Related stand-in (the crown for the office) |
| **synecdoche** | Part for whole or whole for part |
| **personification** | Non-person given person-moves |
| **irony** | Said against meant, in a readable way |
| **allegory** | A sustained mapping. Easy to over-claim |

**Tenor / vehicle** (I. A. Richards’s school names) split what is meant and what it is dressed in. **conceit** is an extended, often strained, metaphor as a period habit (Metaphysical as a later label).

**Cliché** is a figure worn smooth. Name it; do not decorate with it.

Philosophy pack owns metaphor as a theory of meaning. This chapter owns the line.

**Check:** what two domains. What the figure *does* in the poem.

## 7. The Lyric Voice, Persona, and Dramatic Monologue

**Lyric** is the school name for a poem that stages a speaker’s now (song-rooted). **speaker ≠ poet** unless the page or a documented occasion says so. Same cut as narrator ≠ author in `../literature/`.

**Persona** is a worn voice. **apostrophe** addresses what cannot answer. **dramatic monologue** is a character speaking in a situation (Browning as a named English drill).

**Address:** to a beloved, a god, a public, the self, nobody. Who is *you*?

**Tense and aspect** place the now. A lyric can still tell a little story; epic (ch 11) is built to tell a long one.

**Check:** who speaks. To whom. From when.

## 8. The Sonnet and Closed English Forms

**Form is a contract.** count it, then see whether the poem keeps or breaks it on purpose.

**Sonnet:** fourteen lines as the school English/Italian count. **Petrarchan** (octave + sestet, volta) and **Shakespearean** (three quatrains + couplet) as the two named English-class maps. Rhyme schemes and the volta’s job: **fetch the poem**. Iambic pentameter is the English default, not a law of Italian or of every modern sonnet.

Other named contracts (counts are school definitions — verify on a fetched text before you police a draft):

| Form | Usual contract (name only) |
|---|---|
| **couplet** | Two-line unit, often rhymed |
| **villanelle** | Nineteen lines, two refrains, two rhyme sounds |
| **sestina** | Sixes + envoi, end-words permute |
| **ghazal** | Couplets, radif/qafia as the Persian/Urdu grammar — fetch; do not fake the refrain |
| **ode** | Public lyric, Pindaric / Horatian / irregular as later English cuts |
| **elegy** | Lament as a job, not always a stanza recipe |
| **ballad stanza** | Ch 11 |
| **blank verse** | Unrhymed iambic pentameter as the English drama/epic default |

**Check:** named form. Fetched count. Keep or break, and where.

## 9. East Asian Poetics: Haiku, Tanka, and Moraic Architecture

**Haiku** is a Japanese form. The school count is **5–7–5 mora (on)**, plus **kigo** (season) and **kireji** (cut) as traditional jobs. **English “5-7-5 syllables” is a different, looser drill.** do not call the English drill the Japanese rule. Fetch a Japanese text (Aozora, a facing-page edition) if the mora are load-bearing.

**Hokku** is the opening verse of a longer chain; **haiku** as a stand-alone is a later name. **renga / renku** is linked verse. **tanka** is a longer waka count — fetch the count; do not invent it.

**Chinese 詩:** regulated verse (律詩) and old-style (古詩) have tone and parallelism jobs that English stress meter does not copy. Door: ctext.org. **tonal** meter is not iambs.

**Check:** which language’s count. Mora or syllable or tone.

## 10. The Mechanics of Free Verse: Organic Rhythm and Spatial Layout

**Free verse** is verse without a preset metrical contract. It is not “prose with returns.” it still has **line**, **cadence**, **repetition**, **breath**.

Imagism as a named early-20th-century English-language program: treat the thing, spare the ornament — fetch Pound’s notes if you will recite the program. It is not the only free-verse grammar.

**Cadence** can be biblical, conversational, or jazz-lined. **anaphora** (starting-repeat) often does the binding rhyme would have done.

If you cannot say why the line breaks *there*, the free verse is not yet made.

**Check:** what replaces meter as the organizing pressure.

## 11. The Epic Tradition, Ballad Metres, and Dramatic Verse

**Epic** is long narrative verse with a public past (Homer, Virgil, later national epics as *types*). **in medias res**, invocation, epithet as school Homeric names — fetch the opening; do not fake a catalogue of ships.

**Ballad** is story in stanzas, often refrain, often 4-3 stresses as an English folk map — fetch the text. **oral** ballads vary by singing.

**Dramatic verse:** plays in meter (Greek chorus, blank-verse tragedy). Speaker is a character on a stage. Folger for Shakespeare texts.

**Occasional** verse marks a wedding, death, coronation, protest. The occasion is part of the contract.

**Check:** lyric now, or story over time, or a voice on a stage.

## 12. Verse Translation: Rhythmic Transposition and Semantic Fidelity

A translated poem is a **new** poem bound to a source. **literal**, **metrical**, **free** as three jobs that fight. You cannot keep sound, sense, and line-count equally — say which you kept.

**Facing-page** is the honest classroom object. Quote the **language you actually read**. If you need the original’s meter, fetch the original.

**Untranslatable** is usually “this device has no cheap equivalent.” name the device.

**Check:** source language. What the translator sacrificed. Page you opened.

## 13. Vocal Performance: Scansion in Real-Time Oral Delivery

Poetry is **speech**. A silent reading still hears.

**Stress the sense**, then the meter — they argue. **pause** at syntax and at some line ends; enjambment often withholds the pause. **do not thump every iamb** like a metronome unless the poem asks for comic march.

**Recordings:** PennSound, Poetry Archive, LOC. A poet’s own reading is evidence, not a law. Later readers may be better.

**Check:** can you speak it so a listener hears the line *and* the sentence.

## 14. Global Comparative Metrics: Quantitative, Alliterative, and Parallel Systems

English school meter is **one** grammar.

| Practice (name) | What to fetch |
|---|---|
| **Greek / Latin** | Quantitative feet, hexameter, Perseus texts |
| **Arabic ʿarūḍ** | Khalīl’s meters as a named system — a treatise, not a vibe |
| **Sanskrit / Tamil** | Mora and set meters; a named śāstra |
| **Hebrew biblical** | Parallelism more than foot-count |
| **West African / oral praise** | Performance, drum, formula — a recording plus a scholar’s scansion |
| **signed poetry** | Space and hand as line |

**Ethopoetics / oral formulaic** (Parry–Lord as named research) is how some long verse is made in performance. Geography pack for where a language sits.

**Do not invent a meter from a travel video.**

**Check:** which language. Which treatise or recording.

## 16. Systematic Explication of Poetic Ambiguities and Meter Anomalies

1. **fetch** the text. No remembered “quote.”
2. **line** vs **sentence**. Mark breaks.
3. **sound** — meter system, rhyme, other bind.
4. **speaker** and **address**.
5. **figures** that do jobs.
6. **form** contract — kept, broken, free.
7. Then meaning. Theme last, not first.
8. Primary record missing → state unverified.

| Symptom | Try |
