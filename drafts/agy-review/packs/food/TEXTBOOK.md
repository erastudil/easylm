# Heat, salt, spoilage, and a recipe

You can already read, and you can count. This book is heat, salt, spoilage, and a recipe.

The object is a kitchen walk. A pan. A pinch. A leftover that has sat. A page of steps. A box with a panel of numbers. How a crop is grown lives in the agronomy pack. How an organ uses a nutrient lives in the health pack. Name those packs and stop.

This pack owns kitchen and label walks. A temperature that says a roast is done, a hold time on a counter, a milligram the government treats as a Daily Value: those come from a USDA or FDA page this sitting fetched, or the line says unverified. A remembered safe temperature stays off this page.

Load-bearing public numbers in these chapters come from four official pages fetched on 20 September 2026: the Food Safety and Inspection Service home at fsis.usda.gov, the FDA food page at fda.gov/food, Dietary Guidelines for Americans at dietaryguidelines.gov, and usda.gov/foodsafety. That last URL returned a page-not-found after a site redesign. A number that did not appear on those pages is marked unverified, or it is a worked kitchen case invented for the arithmetic.

EasyLM is public general education.

## 1. Heat

A pan sits on a burner. Oil shines. You drop in a cube of potato. The cube hisses. The face that touches the pan turns gold. The centre of the cube is still cool if you cut it now. Heat is moving from the flame, through the pan, into the potato. The outside got there first. The inside has not.

That moving of heat into the food, on purpose, until the inside matches a living card, is the kitchen act people name **cooking**. The number you write from a probe in the thickest part is an **internal temperature**. The FSIS home page fetched for this sitting tells you to cook raw meat and poultry to a safe minimum internal temperature. The same page points at a safe-temperature chart. This sitting did not open that chart. Every degree that would say "done" is unverified here. Open the chart. A remembered safe temperature stays off this page.

A thermometer is a stick that reports that number. Put the tip in the thickest part, not in a bone, not in the pan, not in a pool of fat. Write what the dial shows. Then open the chart and compare. If the chart will not open, write unverified next to the dial and do not finish the sentence from a poster on the fridge.

Colour is a poor substitute. Brown on the outside of a burger can sit over a cool centre. Pink in a slice of something else can sit next to a centre that the chart would already pass. The probe is the check. The eye is a hint.

Four chicken thighs, two pans. The first pan holds two thighs piled on top of each other. The second pan holds two thighs in one layer. Heat has a shorter walk in the second pan. The probe still goes into the thickest piece in each pan. Two readings. Two comparisons to the same chart. Do not average the two readings. The cool one is the one that is not done. Fetch the chart that names the food in the pan.

**Check.** A balcony grill. One pork chop, 2.4 centimetres thick at the bone, 1.1 centimetres at the edge. Probe the 2.4 centimetre side. Write the dial. Open the FSIS chart. If you probed the thin edge, you measured the part that finished first.

New case: a 1.8 kilogram roast in an oven. After 80 minutes the surface is dark. The probe in the centre reads a number you write on a scrap. That scrap is not a pass until the chart says so. A third case: 3 fish fillets in one pan, 2 already flaking, 1 still translucent in the middle. Probe the translucent one. The two that flake are not a vote that forgives the third.

## 2. Salt

A pot of water. You add 5 grams of salt. You stir. The grains vanish. The water now tastes of salt. The salt did not leave. It mixed. Weigh the water if you want the pile: 5 grams is still in there, spread out.

That mixed salt is the kitchen object. A pinch is a poor unit because two hands pinch different piles. A gram is a unit you can repeat. 5 grams in 1 litre is not the same as 15 grams in 1 litre. Three times the salt, same water, a stronger mix.

People who print labels name the sodium in a serving. Sodium is the part of kitchen salt the panel counts. How many milligrams a body should take in a day lives on Dietary Guidelines and in the health pack. The Dietary Guidelines home page fetched for this sitting did not print a milligram target. Unverified here. Open that door, or the DRI calculator it points at, when the question is a body. This chapter owns the pinch, the gram, and the pot.

Salt also pulls water. A bowl of sliced cucumber, 12 grams of salt scattered over 400 grams of slices. After an hour, liquid sits in the bowl. The cucumber lost water. The salt did not cook the slices. It changed where the water sat. That pull is why salt shows up in curing and in a brine. A brine is salt mixed into water on purpose, with a ratio you can write.

20 grams of salt in 500 millilitres of water. $20 \div 500 = 0.04$. That is 4 grams of salt in each 100 millilitres, or 4 per hundred. Double the salt to 40 grams in the same 500 millilitres and the mix is 8 per hundred. The arithmetic is the recipe. A "good brine" percent remembered from a blog is unverified here.

Fine salt packs a spoon more tightly than coarse crystals. Weigh when the number matters.

**Check.** 8 grams of salt in 2 litres of soup. Per litre that is $8 \div 2 = 4$ grams. A new case: you scale the same soup to 3 litres and want the same strength. $3 \times 4 = 12$ grams of salt. Another: 9 grams in 1.5 litres. $9 \div 1.5 = 6$ grams per litre. If a second pot has 6 grams in 1 litre, the two pots match.

## 3. Water in food

A ripe tomato on the board. Cut it. Liquid runs. A cracker next to it. Break the cracker. Almost no liquid. Both are food. One is carrying a lot of water. The other has had most of its water driven off already.

That water inside the food is the object. You can weigh it leaving. 400 grams of strawberries in a colander. An hour later the berries weigh 320 grams, and 80 grams of juice sits in the bowl. $400 - 320 = 80$. The missing 80 grams is water, sugar, and a little of the fruit that left with the juice. Most of that 80 is water.

Heat drives water off as steam. 500 grams of dough on a tray. After baking, the loaf weighs 430 grams. $500 - 430 = 70$ grams left as steam in the kitchen air. The loaf is drier. Drier food is a worse home for the growth in chapter 4, which is why a cracker keeps longer than a tomato on the counter. Drier is not automatically safe. Oil can go off. A dry sausage can still carry a problem the eye misses. The living card still owns "safe."

Water that is free to move is more useful to microbes than water that is locked to sugar or salt. Cooks who measure that availability give it a name, **water activity**. This sitting did not fetch a water-activity table from USDA or FDA. Any threshold number for "safe dryness" is unverified here.

A stew is mostly water you put in. 800 millilitres of water on 200 grams of dry lentils. The lentils swell and take some of it up. The water did not vanish. It moved into the seeds. Stale bread is often water moving, not mould. Mould is chapter 4.

**Check.** 250 grams of mushrooms, dry-sautéed until they weigh 90 grams. Water gone: $250 - 90 = 160$ grams. A new case: 1.00 kilogram of potatoes, peeled, boiled, drained, now 0.92 kilograms. $1.00 - 0.92 = 0.08$ kilograms, which is 80 grams of water left in the pot water and as steam. Another: two apples, 180 grams and 180 grams. One baked until 140 grams, one left raw. The baked apple lost 40 grams. The raw one did not. Same fruit, different water.

## 4. Spoilage

A pot of chili in the fridge from Sunday. Thursday you lift the lid. The top is fuzzy. The smell is wrong. You do not taste to check. The food has become a home for growth you did not invite.

That unwanted growth, and the chemical change that makes food unsafe or unfit, is **spoilage**. Heat, water, time, and a food the growth can eat are the four things the kitchen can change. Chapter 1 moves heat. Chapter 3 moves water. Chapter 6 moves cold. Chapter 7 moves time. This chapter names the result when those four sit together too long, or in the wrong combination.

The FSIS home page fetched for this sitting says mishandling food can lead to foodborne illness, and it tells you to wash hands and surfaces often. It says, when handling raw meat, poultry and seafood, keep them separate to prevent cross-contamination. It says cold temperatures keep harmful bacteria from growing, so refrigerate promptly. It does not print a count of hours on the counter, and it does not print a fridge degree. Those figures are unverified here.

The same page prints 67 total recalls in 2025, and 24 total public health alerts in 2025. Of the 2025 recall reasons it shows, 48 of 100 were product contamination, 24 of 100 were unreported allergens, 18 of 100 were misbranding. $67 \times 0.48 = 32.16$, about 32 of the 67. $67 \times 0.24 = 16.08$, about 16. $67 \times 0.18 = 12.06$, about 12. Those three piles are $32 + 16 + 12 = 60$, so about 7 of the 67 sit in reasons the page did not print on that panel. A story that says "almost all recalls are dirt" loses to that split. Contamination was the large pile. Allergens and misbranding were not zero.

A blue board. Raw chicken. Juice runs. You put lettuce on the same board. The juice is now on the lettuce. Cooking the chicken later does not cook the lettuce. That move is the cross-contamination FSIS named. Two boards, or a wash with soap and water between jobs, is the kitchen fix this page can see. A spray of lemon juice as a "clean" is not a number this sitting fetched. Unverified.

Sour milk, slimy ham, a dented can that hisses when you open it, a jar whose lid has domed: those are stop signs you can see. Clear broth that smells fine can still be a problem you cannot see. The living card owns which leftovers to keep. This sitting did not fetch that list. Unverified here. When the look or the smell is already wrong, you do not need the list. The pot is done.

**Check.** Raw shrimp on a board, then sliced peaches for a salad on the same board with no wash. Write what moved. The shrimp juice moved. The peaches are now the second food. A new case: 67 recalls, 24 of 100 unreported allergens. About 16 of 67. If a poster says "recalls are almost never allergens," the poster loses. A third case: chili with a fuzzy lid. Do not stir the fuzz in to "see if it is only on top." The top is enough.

## 5. A recipe as a procedure

A card on the fridge. It says: 200 grams of dry lentils, 800 millilitres of water, 12 grams of salt, 1 onion, 2 cloves of garlic. It says: dice the onion, warm a pot, cook the onion until soft, add garlic for 1 minute, add lentils and water, simmer until the lentils mash against a spoon, then add the salt. It says the pot serves 4.

That card is a **recipe**. A recipe is a procedure: named amounts, an order of acts, and a finish test you can see. The finish test on this card is the mash against a spoon, not a clock alone. 20 minutes on a fierce boil and 40 minutes on a low simmer are not the same walk for the same 200 grams.

Order is part of the object. Salt at the start in a dry pan of onions is a different act from salt at the end in the liquid. Garlic for 1 minute at the end of the onion step is a different act from garlic dumped in with cold water. If you change the order, you have written a second recipe. Write the change.

Scaling is arithmetic. The card serves 4. You need 6. $6 \div 4 = 1.5$. Every amount multiplies by 1.5. Lentils: $200 \times 1.5 = 300$ grams. Water: $800 \times 1.5 = 1200$ millilitres. Salt: $12 \times 1.5 = 18$ grams. Onion: 1 becomes 1.5, so one and a half onions. Garlic: $2 \times 1.5 = 3$ cloves. The finish test does not multiply. You still mash a spoonful. The pot is larger. The test is the same test.

Halving is the same move. For 2 people, $2 \div 4 = 0.5$. Lentils 100 grams, water 400 millilitres, salt 6 grams, half an onion, 1 clove. A pan that is too wide will drive off water faster than the card expected. Watch the level. Add water if the lentils go dry before they mash. That extra water is a new line you write on the card, with an amount.

A recipe that says "a handful" or "cook until done" has left a hole. Fill the hole with a gram, a millilitre, a minute you measured, or a finish test you can point at. "Done" for a roast is chapter 1 and the FSIS chart, not a feeling in the wrist.

**Check.** The lentil card for 8 people. $8 \div 4 = 2$. Lentils 400 grams, water 1600 millilitres, salt 24 grams, 2 onions, 4 cloves. A new case: you have only 150 grams of lentils. $150 \div 200 = 0.75$ of the card. Water $800 \times 0.75 = 600$ millilitres. Salt $12 \times 0.75 = 9$ grams. Another: a cake card wants 180 grams of flour and 2 eggs for one tin. Two tins: 360 grams of flour, 4 eggs. If you write 3 eggs for two tins, you have left the 2-times scale.

## 6. Cold

A jug of milk on the table after breakfast. The room is warm. You put the jug back. Cold slows the growth from chapter 4. That is the whole job of the fridge: take heat out of the food and keep it out.

People name that kept-out heat **refrigeration**. The FSIS home page says cold temperatures keep harmful bacteria from growing, so refrigerate promptly. It points at a refrigeration page. This sitting did not open that inner page. The degree a fridge should hold, the degree a freezer should hold, and how long a given leftover may sit at each, are unverified here. Open the refrigeration door. A remembered fridge number stays off this page.

A freezer is a colder cold. Ice forms. Frozen peas at 500 grams, left on the counter, become a wet bag as the ice turns back to water. How to thaw lives on the FSIS door this sitting did not open. Unverified here.

Prompt, on the FSIS home page, is the word. Chili off the burner at 12:10 and still on the stove at 16:10 has sat 4 hours in the warm. Split it into two shallow pans and heat has a shorter walk out. Shallow pans are a geometry, not a fetched minute count.

The FDA food page fetched for this sitting points at a storm page: protect food and water during hurricanes and other storms. This sitting did not open that inner page. What to keep after a power cut is unverified here. Open that door when the fridge has gone quiet.

**Check.** Eight yogurt cups. Seven go in the fridge at 08:15. One sits on the counter until 11:45. That one sat $11:45 - 08:15 = 3$ hours 30 minutes. Write the 3 hours 30 minutes. Open the refrigeration door for whether it stays. A new case: a bag of frozen peas, 500 grams, thawed in the fridge overnight, still cold, still ice-free. That is a different walk from the balcony thaw. Another: two pans of chili, each 3 centimetres deep, versus one pot 12 centimetres deep. Heat leaving the 3 centimetre pans has less distance to walk. The living card still owns the minute count. Unverified here.

## 7. Time

A roast chicken, carved at 18:20. The platter sits on the table. At 21:05 someone wants a second plate. The clock is the object. $21:05 - 18:20 = 2$ hours 45 minutes on the platter.

People name a limit of that kind a **hold time**. The FSIS home page did not print one. The FDA food page did not print one. Unverified here. Open the FSIS handling doors, or the FDA Food Code 2022 door, when a minute count must be right. A remembered two-hour rule stays off this page.

Write times on leftovers. A strip of tape on a tub: chili, 12 May, 13:40, into the fridge. Three days later the tape is a fact you can read. Without the tape you are guessing. Guessing is how Sunday's pot becomes Thursday's fuzz in chapter 4.

Elapsed time is subtraction you already have. Food off the heat at 11:20, into the fridge at 11:50, is 30 minutes of walk. Food off the heat at 11:20, into the fridge at 14:05, is 2 hours 45 minutes of walk. Same pot, two stories. The living card compares those walks to a hold time this sitting does not have.

A recipe minute is not a hold time. "Simmer 25 minutes" is a cook step from chapter 5. "Sat 25 minutes on the counter" is this chapter. Keep the two clocks separate. The first clock is how long heat is going in. The second clock is how long the food has been in the room after that heat stopped.

Tailgating, on the FSIS home page this sitting fetched, is named as a season that needs the same food-safety practices as picnicking outdoors. The inner tailgating page was not opened. Any hour-count for a cooler in a parking lot is unverified here. The home page did not print one.

**Check.** Soup covered on the stove from 12:10 to 15:40. $15:40 - 12:10 = 3$ hours 30 minutes. Write 3 hours 30 minutes. Open a hold-time door. A new case: three tubs of rice, taped 09:00, 09:00, and 18:00 the same day. At 20:00 the first two have sat 11 hours in the fridge, the third has sat 2 hours. Do not treat the three tubs as one pile. Another: a picnic started at 10:00, chicken out of the cooler at 10:15, still out at 13:00. Out-time $13:00 - 10:15 = 2$ hours 45 minutes. The cooler closed at 10:15 is a different clock from the chicken on the plate.

## 8. A cut

A raw side of fish on a board. You run a knife along the bone and lift two fillets. You now have more surface than you started with. The inside of the fish, which was sealed, is open to the air, to the board, and to the knife.

That opening is a **cut**. A cut is a new surface. Heat in chapter 1 has to reach every part you care about. Growth in chapter 4 lives on surfaces and in the wet. More surface means more of both jobs.

A 1.2 kilogram roast cut into 8 slices. $1.2 \div 8 = 0.15$ kilograms per slice, which is 150 grams. The whole roast had one outer skin. The slices have 16 new faces if each slice has two faces, plus the old skin. A probe in the centre of the whole roast was one number. A pile of 150 gram slices will take heat faster, and will take fridge cold faster, and will take contamination faster. The geometry changed. The food's name on the chart may still be the same word. Fetch the chart for the form you actually have, whole or ground or sliced. This sitting did not open that chart. Unverified which number applies to which form.

The FSIS home page says keep raw meat, poultry and seafood separate. The knife is part of that sentence. A knife that just portioned raw fish, then cuts peaches, is the blue board from chapter 4 in steel form. Wash the knife. Or keep a second knife. The cut of fruit and the cut of fish do not share a blade until the blade has been washed.

How the animal was raised, and how the field was farmed, live in the agronomy pack. Stop there. This chapter owns the board, the blade, the new surface, and the separate pile.

Ground meat is a cut taken to the limit. Almost all of it is surface. Probe the thickest part of the patty, not the grill bars.

**Check.** One chicken, 1.6 kilograms, cut into 8 pieces. Mean piece $1.6 \div 8 = 0.2$ kilograms, 200 grams. Probe the largest piece, not the mean. A new case: 12 peach halves on the board that just held the fish, no wash. Write "shared board" as the fact. Another: 150 gram slices from the 1.2 kilogram roast, stacked while hot in a deep tub. The centre of the stack is a new thick object. Heat leaving that centre has a long walk. Spread the slices, or wait to stack until the living refrigeration card, still unverified here, is the next door you open.

## 9. What the label says

A box of crackers. A panel on the side. Serving size 30 grams. Servings in the box: 8. Calories 130. Sodium 180 milligrams. Added sugars 2 grams. You eat 90 grams. $90 \div 30 = 3$ servings. Calories $3 \times 130 = 390$. Sodium $3 \times 180 = 540$ milligrams. Added sugars $3 \times 2 = 6$ grams. The panel's numbers are per serving, not per box, not per sitting, unless the sitting was one serving.

That panel is the **Nutrition Facts** label. The FDA food page fetched for this sitting points at educational materials on the Nutrition Facts label, and at a proposed front-of-package "Nutrition Info box" that would show saturated fat, sodium, and added sugars as Low, Med, or High. This sitting did not open those inner pages. The milligram Daily Value for sodium, the gram Daily Value for added sugars, and the rules that set Low, Med, and High, are unverified here. Open those doors. A remembered "% Daily Value" stays off this page as a government threshold. The 180 milligrams printed on this box is a fact about this box.

The same FDA page names an updated "healthy" claim, Food Code 2022 as a model for safe handling in a retail setting, and a Human Foods Program. Food Code 2022 is a retail model, not your fridge card. Inner Food Code temperatures and hold times were not fetched. Unverified here.

Allergens on a label are names of foods that must be declared. FSIS printed that 24 of 100 of its 2025 recall reasons were unreported allergens. A label that hides a milk, egg, fish, shellfish, tree nut, peanut, wheat, or soybean line is a mis-label, not a kitchen preference. The living list of major allergens and any update to that list live on FDA. This sitting did not open the allergen inner page. Unverified as a complete list here. If a box in your hand names an allergen, believe the box and the person who has to avoid it.

Servings in the box let you check a price and a pile. 8 servings of 30 grams are $8 \times 30 = 240$ grams of crackers if the servings fill the box. If the net weight on the front is 240 grams, the arithmetic matches. If the net weight is 200 grams, the serving count and the serving size do not fill the box as printed. Believe the net weight, and recount. $200 \div 30$ is not a whole 8.

**Check.** Yogurt, serving 170 grams, 120 calories, sodium 75 milligrams. You eat 340 grams. Two servings. Calories 240. Sodium 150 milligrams. A new case: a can, serving 125 grams, 3.5 servings in the can. Whole can $3.5 \times 125 = 437.5$ grams. If you eat the can, multiply every panel number by 3.5. Sodium 220 milligrams per serving becomes $220 \times 3.5 = 770$ milligrams. Another: a front-of-package box that says only "healthy." Open the FDA "healthy" claim door. The word on the front is a claim with a rule. This sitting did not fetch the rule. Unverified here.

## 10. A check against USDA or FDA

The method is the same as the study pack's habit for a number, applied to a kitchen. Open the host that owns the step. If the host and a memory disagree, keep the host. If the host will not open, write unverified and do not fill the blank from a fridge magnet.

Do this on paper with a case this book has not used yet.
