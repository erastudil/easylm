# An animal as a system

The reader can read. This book is an animal as a system.

One living body that moves, eats, and leaves young. Kell's hen on the millpond at Harrow Bend. Oren's crayfish under a Silt Creek stone. Sela's swallow in the barn eave. Mira's turtle on the pond shelf. Not a list of every kind.

This pack owns a body plan, movement, and a life cycle. Stay here when the task is one animal.

USGS and FWS homes were fetched. Smithsonian and OpenStax book-page bodies did not arrive as usable text. Specimen counts from those two hosts stay unverified. Worked cases at Harrow Bend are invented.

## 1. An animal as a system

At 06:15 a mallard hen sits on the millpond at Harrow Bend. She paddles two metres, dabbles, lifts her head, and the water runs off her back. A stuffed duck in the mill office does none of those jobs. Same shape in the glass case. No paddle. No dabble. No wet back.

The living hen is many jobs in one body at once. She keeps a warm inside. She moves. She takes food. She watches the kiln road. Later she may sit a nest in the reeds. Take any one job away on paper and you no longer have the hen at 06:15. People name a living body that does those jobs together an **animal**. A stuffed skin is a record of a shape. It is not the system running.

The millpond tapes 40 metres along the dam by 18 metres from dam to reeds. $40 \times 18 = 720$ square metres of water the hen can use this morning. She is not 720 square metres. She is one body on that water. The pond is a place. Chapter 7 owns place. This chapter owns the body.

Oren's crayfish under the riffle stone is a second body. It does not paddle like the hen. It still keeps an inside, takes food, and backs into the dark when the stone lifts. Sela's swallow in the eave is a third. Mira's painted turtle on the shelf is a fourth. Four bodies, four systems. A pile of four names is not one animal.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service home states a job: working with others to conserve, protect, and enhance fish, wildlife, plants, and their habitats for the continuing benefit of the American people. That is an office talking about many animals. This book walks one body. The USGS home names Ecosystems as a mission area, and says the bureau began in 1879 as part of the U.S. Department of the Interior. It did not print a definition of an animal, or a count of kinds. Those figures are unverified here.

**Check.** Write three jobs the hen did at 06:15: paddle, dabble, keep water off her back. Cross out the stuffed duck in the office for each job it cannot do. If both the hen and the stuffed duck got the same three jobs, you treated a skin as a system.

New case: the crayfish, stone lifted, backs 20 centimetres in one snap of the tail fan. Write that snap as a job of that body. The stone does not snap. Another: four animals on a list, one pond. If you wrote "the millpond" as the animal, you mixed the place with the body.

## 2. A body plan

Oren turns a stone in Silt Creek. A crayfish backs into the dark, all plates and jointed pieces. He sets the stone back. He draws what he saw: a head-end with two long feelers, a plated middle, a tail fan, and matching rows of jointed pieces on the left and the right. He did not invent a new animal. He copied a layout that was already there.

That shared layout you could draw after one look, and that other crayfish under other stones would still fit, is a **body plan**. This pack owns that walk. A list of colours is not a plan. A plan says where the parts sit, what matches what, and what is hard.

On this crayfish Oren counts five jointed walking pieces on the left and five on the right. $5 + 5 = 10$ walking pieces on this animal today. The two sides match. Front is the feeler end. Back is the fan. Hard stuff is on the outside. Soft work sits inside the plates. A remembered count for all crayfish is unverified here. This count is this animal, this morning.

The hen on the pond is a different plan. Two sides still match. Front is the bill. Back is the tail. The hard rod you can feel in a cooked fish from the same pond sits inside. Two legs, two wings. The turtle on the shelf matches left and right, has a hard box on the outside, and still has the inside rod of a fish when Mira once saw a dead one opened at the mill. Three plans. Do not mash them.

A mayfly adult Oren holds later has six thin legs, three on each side, and four wings, two and two. $3 + 3 = 6$ legs on this adult. $2 + 2 = 4$ wings. The young in the creek, from chapter 5, will not have those four wings yet. Same line of animals, two layouts at two times. Chapter 5 owns the change. This chapter owns the layout you draw today.

OpenStax Biology 2e is the survey door for how a course writes plans. That book page did not return a usable body. Chapter numbers and plan tables from that door are unverified here. Draw the stone and the pond first.

**Check.** Draw the crayfish as left matching right, feelers front, fan back, hard outside. Draw the hen as left matching right, bill front, hard rod inside. If both drawings have the hard stuff in the same place, you copied one plan onto the other.

New case: the turtle, 14 centimetres of shell along the back of this animal, four legs, a head that pulls in. ## 3. Movement

Sela counts eight beats of a barn swallow over the millpond, then the bird is gone behind the kiln. Two seconds on her watch. The swallow did not wait for the pond to move it. Wings beat. Air is pushed. The body goes.

That going, by a part of the body pushing on water, air, or ground, is **movement** as this book uses the word. This pack owns that walk. A log drifting down Silt Creek also goes. The log has no part that beats. Write the part that does the work.

The hen paddles. Two feet push water back. The body slides forward. On the mill road she walks: two feet push ground. A short hop: two wings, then two feet. Same hen, three ways, three surfaces. Write the surface under the push.

The crayfish's tail fan folds, then snaps. The body shoots backward 20 centimetres under the stone. The walking pieces can also pick the body over gravel, slowly, the other way. Two tools, two directions. If you wrote "the crayfish moves" with no tool and no direction, you hid the plan from chapter 2.

The turtle walks the mud with four legs and swims with the same four, feet webbed on this animal. Sela times one walk across a 2.0 metre shelf: 14 seconds. That 14 is a watch on this turtle, this morning. A species speed from a book is unverified here.

How a push becomes a change of motion lives in the mechanics pack. Name that door and stop. Stay here for which part pushes, on what, and where the body goes.

The FWS home shows bald eagles at a nest, pelicans landing, an alligator in water. Pictures of movement. They do not print a wingbeat count or a swim time. Unverified as numbers.

**Check.** Swallow: eight beats, then gone behind the kiln. Write wings and air. Hen: feet and water, then feet and road. If both animals got "they go" as the whole answer, you skipped the part that pushes.

New case: crayfish tail fan, 20 centimetres back, under the stone. Walking pieces the other way, over gravel. Two tools. Another: the stuffed duck in the mill office. Zero beats. If you wrote that it could paddle because it has foot shapes, you mixed a shape with a job.

## 5. A life cycle

Oren lifts a tray from Silt Creek. Twelve flattened young cling to the mesh, six legs, no full wings, gill-like fringes on the sides. He has seen the winged adults over the same riffle at dusk, four wings, no water fringes. Seven of the twelve, kept in a wash-basin with creek water and a stone, push out of their old skins over three nights and stand as winged adults on the basin rim. Five remain as the water young. $12 - 7 = 5$ still in the water shape. Same line of animals. Two shapes. One stretch of days.

That stretch, from one shape of a kind to the next, and often to eggs or young that start the stretch again, is a **life cycle**. This pack owns that walk. The name is a handle for the tray and the rim. A single photograph of an adult is one frame. It is not the stretch.

Kell's hen in April sits a nest in the reeds. Nine eggs. Six open. Three do not. $9 - 6 = 3$ unhatched on this nest, this year. The six are downy and follow her onto the 720 square metres. They are not yet the mottled hen of 06:15. Same kind. Three stages on this pond: egg, downy young, sitting hen. A remembered day-count to hatch for all mallards is unverified here. Write 9, 6, and 3 for this nest.

Rainbow trout at Ennis, on the FWS home, are raised as brood stock, seven strains. Eggs and young in a hatchery are a life cycle under staff. The home does not print days to hatch. Unverified. The USGS home has a story titled Landsat Illustrates the Life Cycle of a Mine. That stretch is a mine, not an animal. The words match. The object does not. Animal life cycle stays with the tray, the nest, and the raceway.

**Check.** Twelve young on the mesh, seven winged on the rim, five still water-shaped. $12 - 7 = 5$. If you wrote 12 adults, you skipped the stretch.
