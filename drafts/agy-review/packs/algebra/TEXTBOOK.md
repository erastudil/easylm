# A letter for an unknown

You can already add, take away, multiply, share, name a percent, and keep a unit on a number. This book adds one object: a letter that stands for a number you have not filled in yet. Write the letter. Wrap it with the arithmetic you already know. Unwrap until the letter sits alone. Put a number back in and see whether the sentence still holds.

The longer courses live at official doors. OpenStax *Elementary Algebra 2e* and *Intermediate Algebra 2e* are the full school walks. OpenStax *Algebra and Trigonometry 2e* (Jay Abramson, Arizona State University; digital PDF ISBN-13 978-1-951693-40-4; published 21 December 2021; web version last updated 12 June 2026) is licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 on that details page. This pack links that door. It does not copy the book. NIST's Digital Library of Mathematical Functions, Version 1.2.8, released 15 September 2026, keeps algebraic methods in its chapter 1 and elementary functions in its chapter 4. Use those pages when a later pack needs a named identity. The arithmetic in the checks below is ordinary counting. You can redo every sum by hand.

## 1. A box for a number

A bag holds some apples. You have not counted them yet. You still need a way to talk about that bag in a sentence with other numbers.

Pick a letter. People often pick $x$. The letter is a name for the unknown count, the same way "the bag" is a name for the bag. You may pick $n$ for a count, $t$ for a time, $p$ for a price. The choice is a label. It does not change the pile.

$x + 3$ means the bag, and three more apples beside it.

$2x$ means two bags of the same size, which is $x + x$.

$x - 1$ means the bag with one apple taken out.

The letter is a blank you are allowed to write in a line of arithmetic. Until you fill it, $x + 3$ is a pile you can still name. After you fill it, the line becomes ordinary counting.

Fill $x$ with 5. Then $x + 3$ is $5 + 3 = 8$. Then $2x$ is $2 \times 5 = 10$. Then $x - 1$ is $4$. Same letter, same fill, three different wrappings.

Fill $x$ with 0. Then $x + 3$ is 3. Then $2x$ is 0. Zero is a legal fill. An empty bag is a bag.

Fill $x$ with $\frac{1}{2}$. Then $2x$ is 1. The letter does not have to be a whole count. Numeracy already let a number be a share.

**Check.** A new fill: $x = 9$. Then $x + 4 = 13$. Then $2x = 18$. Then $3x - 1 = 26$. Do those three by hand. If any one fails, the fill was copied wrong, not "almost right."

A later sentence may use two different letters. That is two blanks, not one blank written twice. Chapter 4 takes that case. Here, one letter, one fill, every time you see that letter in the same problem.

## 2. Same both sides

A kitchen scale. Left pan: the bag and 3 loose apples. Right pan: 11 apples. The pans match.

Whatever you do to one pan, do to the other, or the match breaks. Take 3 apples off both pans. Left: the bag. Right: 8 apples. So the bag was 8.

We write that story as:

$$x + 3 = 11$$

Take 3 from both sides:

$$x = 8$$

The written sentence with an equals sign is an **equation**. Both sides name the same amount. The work is keeping that sameness while you unwrap.

Put 8 back in. $8 + 3 = 11$. True. That is the close for this sentence.

A sentence can already be true with no letter. $7 + 4 = 11$. A sentence can already be false. $7 + 4 = 10$. The equals sign is a claim, not a decoration. When a letter is in the claim, your job is to find the fill that makes the claim true, or to show that no fill does.

Same move on a multiply. Two bags match 14 apples: $2x = 14$. Share both sides into two equal groups: $x = 7$. Put 7 back: $2 \times 7 = 14$.

Same move on a take-away. $x - 6 = 9$. Add 6 to both sides: $x = 15$. Put 15 back: $15 - 6 = 9$.

**Check.** A new sentence: $x + 9 = 24$. Take 9 from both sides: $x = 15$. Put 15 back: $15 + 9 = 24$. True. A second new sentence: $5x = 35$. Share both sides into five: $x = 7$. Put 7 back: $5 \times 7 = 35$. True.

If you take 9 from the left of $x + 9 = 24$ and forget the right, you have broken the match on purpose. The number you then write for $x$ will fail the put-back. The put-back is how you catch that.

## 3. Undo

Each wrap has a reverse.

| you wrapped with | undo by |
|---|---|
| add 4 | subtract 4 |
| subtract 4 | add 4 |
| multiply by 3 | divide by 3 |
| divide by 3 | multiply by 3 |

Unwrap from the outside, the last wrap first.

$3x + 2 = 14$. First the $+2$ was added to $3x$. Subtract 2 from both sides: $3x = 12$. Then the $3$ was a multiply. Divide both sides by 3: $x = 4$.

Put 4 back: $3 \times 4 + 2 = 12 + 2 = 14$. True.

The order matters. If you divide by 3 first in that sentence, you must divide the whole left and the whole right: $\frac{3x + 2}{3} = \frac{14}{3}$. That is legal and messier. The outside-first habit keeps the arithmetic small. Same match either way, if you really do the same thing to both sides.

A take-away on the outside. $2x - 5 = 11$. Add 5 to both sides: $2x = 16$. Divide by 2: $x = 8$. Put 8 back: $16 - 5 = 11$. True.

A share on the outside. $\frac{x}{4} + 1 = 6$. Subtract 1: $\frac{x}{4} = 5$. Multiply by 4: $x = 20$. Put 20 back: $20 \div 4 + 1 = 5 + 1 = 6$. True.

A wrap you cannot undo by dividing: a divide by zero. $x \times 0 = 0$ is true for every $x$, so dividing both sides by 0 does not name a single fill. If an unwrap asks you to divide by $x - x$, you have left the legal moves. Stop and look at the original sentence.

**Check.** A new sentence: $5x - 4 = 21$. Add 4: $5x = 25$. Divide by 5: $x = 5$. Put 5 back: $5 \times 5 - 4 = 25 - 4 = 21$. True. A second new sentence: $\frac{x}{5} + 3 = 8$. Subtract 3: $\frac{x}{5} = 5$. Multiply by 5: $x = 25$. Put 25 back: $25 \div 5 + 3 = 5 + 3 = 8$. True.

## 4. Two boxes

Now there are two unknown piles. Call them $x$ and $y$. One sentence that uses both is not enough to pin both.

$x + y = 10$. Many pairs work. $x = 1$, $y = 9$. $x = 4$, $y = 6$. $x = 0$, $y = 10$. $x = 7$, $y = 3$. Each pair is a different story. The sentence only says the two piles together make ten.

A second sentence can pin the pair. Keep $x + y = 10$, and add $x = 4$. Then $y$ must be 6. Or add $2x + y = 14$. Now the two sentences together name one pair.

Work the second pair of sentences. From $x + y = 10$, $y$ is $10 - x$. Put that into $2x + y = 14$:

$$2x + (10 - x) = 14$$

$$x + 10 = 14$$

$$x = 4$$

Then $y = 6$. Put both back into both sentences: $4 + 6 = 10$. $8 + 6 = 14$. True in both.

You may also subtract the two sentences so one letter vanishes. $2x + y = 14$ and $x + y = 10$. Subtract the second from the first: $(2x + y) - (x + y) = 14 - 10$, so $x = 4$. Then $y = 6$. Same pair. Legal because both sentences name true amounts, and the difference of equals is equal.

If the two sentences are the same fact twice, $x + y = 10$ and $2x + 2y = 20$, every pair that fits one fits the other. If they fight, $x + y = 10$ and $x + y = 7$, no pair fits both.

**Check.** A new pair of sentences: $x + y = 11$ and $2x + y = 20$. Subtract: $x = 9$. Then $y = 2$. Put back: $9 + 2 = 11$. $18 + 2 = 20$. True. A second new pair: $x + 2y = 14$ and $x = 6$. Then $6 + 2y = 14$, so $2y = 8$, $y = 4$. Put back: $6 + 8 = 14$. True.

One box was chapter 1. Two boxes are two blanks. You need two independent facts to fill both. A picture of those facts is the next chapter.

## 5. A picture of pairs

Write two numbers as a pair: the incoming one, then the outgoing one. For $x + y = 10$, one pair is $(1, 9)$. Another is $(4, 6)$. Another is $(0, 10)$.

Draw a grid. Incoming across. Outgoing up. Put a dot at each pair. For $x + y = 10$ the dots sit on a straight line that slants down: as $x$ grows by 1, $y$ falls by 1.

A different sentence, $y = 2x + 1$, makes a different line of pairs.

| $x$ | $y = 2x + 1$ |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 5 |
| 3 | 7 |
| 4 | 9 |

Each row is a pair: $(0, 1)$, $(1, 3)$, $(2, 5)$, $(3, 7)$, $(4, 9)$. The dots sit on a straight line that slants up.

A pair on the grid is a claim. $(2, 5)$ is on $y = 2x + 1$ because $2 \times 2 + 1 = 5$. $(2, 6)$ is not, because $2 \times 2 + 1$ is 5, not 6. The picture does not argue with the arithmetic. The picture shows the arithmetic.

Negative fills are legal pairs. $x = -1$ in $y = 2x + 1$ gives $y = -1$. The dot sits left of the outgoing axis. Numeracy already put negative marks to the left of zero. The grid is two of those number lines, crossed.

**Check.** A new pair for $y = 2x + 1$: $x = 6$. Then $y = 13$. The pair is $(6, 13)$. A pair that fails: $(3, 8)$. $2 \times 3 + 1 = 7$, not 8, so $(3, 8)$ is off the line. A pair on $x + y = 10$: $(8, 2)$. Off that line: $(8, 3)$, because $8 + 3 = 11$.

If you cannot say which number is incoming and which is outgoing, the pair is unordered in your head. Write them in a fixed order and keep it. This book writes $(x, y)$: first the incoming, then the outgoing.

## 6. Rise over run

Walk from one pair to another on the grid. From $(1, 3)$ to $(4, 9)$ the outgoing number rises by 6 and the incoming number runs forward by 3. The rise over the run is $\frac{6}{3} = 2$.

That rise-per-run is the **slope** of the line through those pairs. For $y = 2x + 1$, every step of 1 in $x$ raises $y$ by 2. From $(0, 1)$ to $(1, 3)$, rise 2, run 1, slope 2. From $(2, 5)$ to $(3, 7)$, rise 2, run 1, slope 2. The slope stays.

A line that goes down has a negative slope. On $x + y = 10$, from $(1, 9)$ to $(4, 6)$ the rise is $6 - 9 = -3$ and the run is $4 - 1 = 3$. Slope $\frac{-3}{3} = -1$. Each step of 1 in $x$ drops $y$ by 1.

A line that does not rise has slope 0. From $(1, 4)$ to $(5, 4)$ the rise is 0 and the run is 4. Slope 0. The outgoing number stays 4. The sentence is $y = 4$.

A line that does not run has no slope you can write as a share. From $(3, 1)$ to $(3, 8)$ the run is 0. You cannot divide by 0. The sentence is $x = 3$. Every pair on that line has incoming 3. The picture is a vertical line. Name it as a fixed $x$, not as a rise over run.

Units ride along. If $x$ is hours and $y$ is kilometers, slope is kilometers per hour. If $x$ is kilograms and $y$ is euros, slope is euros per kilogram. Mixing the units, or dropping them, makes a number that no longer names the walk.

**Check.** A new walk on $y = 2x + 1$: from $(2, 5)$ to $(5, 11)$. Rise $11 - 5 = 6$. Run $5 - 2 = 3$. Slope $\frac{6}{3} = 2$. Same slope as before. A new walk on $x + y = 10$: from $(0, 10)$ to $(5, 5)$. Rise $5 - 10 = -5$. Run $5 - 0 = 5$. Slope $-1$. A flat walk: from $(2, 7)$ to $(9, 7)$. Rise 0, run 7, slope 0.

Two pairs give a slope. A third pair either shares that slope with the first, and sits on the same line, or it does not. $(6, 13)$ with $(0, 1)$: rise 12, run 6, slope 2. On the line. $(6, 12)$ with $(0, 1)$: rise 11, run 6, slope $\frac{11}{6}$. Off the line.

## 7. A rule that stays

Take a number. Multiply by 2. Add 1. Do that to 0, you get 1. Do that to 1, you get 3. Do that to 2, you get 5. The machine does not change its job when the incoming number changes.

Write the machine as $y = 2x + 1$. $x$ is the incoming number. $y$ is the outgoing number. Every pair in chapter 5 came from this one job. The slope in chapter 6 is the multiply-by-2. The value when $x = 0$ is the $+1$. That hit, where the line meets the outgoing axis, is the **intercept**.

A different machine, $y = -x + 10$, is the line $x + y = 10$ solved for $y$. Multiply by $-1$, then add 10. Slope $-1$. Intercept 10. Same picture as the falling line of pairs. Two writings, one rule.

The rule is the object that stays. Pairs are what the rule emits. Slope and intercept are two numbers that name a straight rule: how much it rises per step, and where it stands when the incoming number is zero. $y = mx + b$ is that naming. $m$ is the slope. $b$ is the intercept. For $y = 2x + 1$, $m = 2$ and $b = 1$. For $y = -x + 10$, $m = -1$ and $b = 10$.

A rule may stay and still not be a straight line. $y = x^2$ sends 0 to 0, 1 to 1, 2 to 4, 3 to 9. The outgoing number is the incoming number used as a factor twice. Rise over run between 1 and 2 is $\frac{4-1}{2-1} = 3$. Between 2 and 3 it is $\frac{9-4}{3-2} = 5$. The slope of a pair-walk changes. The rule still stays: always square. Straight rules are the ones whose slope does not change. This book spends its grid time on those. NIST DLMF chapter 4 is the official door when a later pack needs named elementary functions beyond a straight line.

**Check.** A new incoming number for $y = 2x + 1$: $x = 10$. Outgoing $y = 21$. Put back: $2 \times 10 + 1 = 21$. A second new fill: $x = -3$. Outgoing $y = -5$. Put back: $2 \times (-3) + 1 = -6 + 1 = -5$. A new straight rule: $y = 3x - 4$. Intercept $-4$. Slope 3. At $x = 5$, $y = 11$. Put back: $3 \times 5 - 4 = 15 - 4 = 11$.

If two writings emit the same pair for every incoming number, they are the same rule. $y = 2x + 1$ and $y = 2(x + \frac{1}{2})$ are the same machine. If they disagree at even one incoming number, they are two machines. Test a number you did not use to write them.

## 8. When the letter is in two places

The same unknown can sit on the left and on the right.

$$2x + 1 = x + 7$$

Gather the bags on one side and the loose numbers on the other, still doing the same thing to both sides.

Subtract $x$ from both sides: $x + 1 = 7$. Subtract 1: $x = 6$.

Put 6 back into both original sides. Left: $2 \times 6 + 1 = 13$. Right: $6 + 7 = 13$. Same amount. The fill works.

The letter was in two places. It was still one box. One fill has to serve both appearances. That is why you gather: so the two appearances become one.

Sometimes the bags cancel and a true number sentence remains. $2x + 3 = 2(x + 1) + 1$ unwraps to $2x + 3 = 2x + 3$. Subtract $2x$: $3 = 3$. True, with no letter left. Every fill works. The two sides were the same rule written twice.

Sometimes the bags cancel and a false number sentence remains. $x + 4 = x + 1$. Subtract $x$: $4 = 1$. False. No fill works. The two sides cannot match.

Those two outcomes are legal. They are the whole story for that sentence. You have not failed the method. The sentence was an identity, or it was a fight.

**Check.** A new sentence: $5x - 4 = 2x + 11$. Subtract $2x$: $3x - 4 = 11$. Add 4: $3x = 15$. Divide by 3: $x = 5$. Left: $5 \times 5 - 4 = 21$. Right: $2 \times 5 + 11 = 21$. True. A new identity: $3(x + 2) = 3x + 6$. Unwrap the left: $3x + 6 = 3x + 6$. Every $x$. A new fight: $2x + 5 = 2x - 1$. Subtract $2x$: $5 = -1$. No $x$.

If you subtract the wrong letter-pile and then divide, you can manufacture a fill that does not survive the put-back. Always put the number back into the original sentence, both sides, before you trust it.

## 9. What the graph is saying

Two rules drawn on one grid may cross.

$y = 2x + 1$ and $y = 11 - x$. At a crossing, both rules are true at the same time, so the two $y$ names are the same amount:

$$2x + 1 = 11 - x$$

That is chapter 8. Add $x$ to both sides: $3x + 1 = 11$. Subtract 1: $3x = 10$. Divide by 3: $x = \frac{10}{3}$. Then $y = 11 - \frac{10}{3} = \frac{33}{3} - \frac{10}{3} = \frac{23}{3}$.

Put both numbers back into both rules. First rule: $2 \times \frac{10}{3} + 1 = \frac{20}{3} + \frac{3}{3} = \frac{23}{3}$. Second rule: $11 - \frac{10}{3} = \frac{23}{3}$. Same pair. The crossing is $(\frac{10}{3}, \frac{23}{3})$.

No crossing: the lines are parallel. Same slope, different intercept. $y = 2x + 1$ and $y = 2x - 4$ both rise by 2 per step. One hits the outgoing axis at 1, the other at $-4$. They never share a pair. Set $2x + 1 = 2x - 4$ and you get $1 = -4$, chapter 8's fight.

Every crossing: the lines are the same line written twice. $y = 2x + 1$ and $2y = 4x + 2$ emit the same pairs. Set them equal after matching forms and you get an identity.

A graph also speaks in pieces. Where the line meets the outgoing axis, $x = 0$, you read the intercept. Where it meets the incoming axis, $y = 0$, you read the fill that makes the outgoing number vanish. For $y = 2x + 1$, that fill is $x = -\frac{1}{2}$, because $2 \times (-\frac{1}{2}) + 1 = 0$. For $y = 11 - x$, that fill is $x = 11$.

Read a story, not a decoration. A line that rises through money against time is a balance growing. A line that falls through remaining fuel against distance is a tank emptying. The slope is the rate. The intercept is the starting pile. The crossing of two such lines is the moment both stories name the same pair.

**Check.** A new pair of rules: $y = x + 2$ and $y = 2x - 1$. Set $x + 2 = 2x - 1$. Add 1: $x + 3 = 2x$. Subtract $x$: $3 = x$. Then $y = 3 + 2 = 5$. Put back: $3 + 2 = 5$, and $2 \times 3 - 1 = 5$. Crossing $(3, 5)$. A parallel pair: $y = x + 2$ and $y = x - 4$. Same slope 1, intercepts 2 and $-4$. No crossing. A same-line pair: $y = x + 2$ and $2y = 2x + 4$. Every pair on one is on the other.

## 10. A check that closes

Write the sentence with a letter. Name what the letter counts, with units if there are units. Unwrap, same move on both sides. Put the number back into the original sentence. Ask whether that number can be a count of the thing you named.

Three shirts plus 8 dollars of shipping cost 47 dollars. The letter $s$ is the dollar price of one shirt.

$$3s + 8 = 47$$

Subtract 8: $3s = 39$. Divide by 3: $s = 13$. Put 13 back: $3 \times 13 + 8 = 39 + 8 = 47$. True. Thirteen dollars is a possible price. The check closes.

Four tickets plus a 6 dollar fee cost 54 dollars. The letter $t$ is the dollar price of one ticket.

$$4t + 6 = 54$$

Subtract 6: $4t = 48$. Divide by 4: $t = 12$. Put 12 back: $4 \times 12 + 6 = 48 + 6 = 54$. True. A possible price. The check closes.

A fill can satisfy the arithmetic and still fail the named thing. $2a + 10 = 4$ unwraps to $a = -3$. Put $-3$ back: $2 \times (-3) + 10 = 4$. The algebra holds. If $a$ was a count of apples, $-3$ apples is not a possible count. If $a$ was a temperature change in degrees, $-3$ is possible. The last question is not extra manners. It is part of naming the box.

A broken check is useful. Suppose someone claims $x = 8$ for $3x + 2 = 14$. Put 8 back: $3 \times 8 + 2 = 26$, which is not 14. The fill is wrong. Do not average 8 toward 4. Unwrap again from the original sentence.

When a later pack writes $F = ma$, or a percent off a price, or a line of best fit through a table, it is this habit. The letters are boxes. The equals is a scale. Your job is to keep the scale true, then ask whether the fill can be the thing you named.
