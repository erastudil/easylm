# Letters for a union

You can already read. Civic life owns a town, a vote, and an office. This book is the next object: a stack of newspaper letters that argued separate towns should bind as one union under a written plan.

The object is those letters as a walk. Not a mood about founders. Not a tour of the written plan itself. One numbered letter, then the job it tries to do.

The living civics pack is the constitution survey. Name that door and stop. Stay here when the task is the letters: a paper as a letter, union, faction, split offices, a large republic, courts, tax, who wrote, how we know, and a check against a host that keeps the pages.

Public general education.

A number, a date, a quote, or an identity that must travel comes from a host that owns the page, or the line says unverified.

## 1. A paper as a letter

Kelpwick has a printer on Market Street. On a Tuesday a sheet goes up in the shop window. A number sits at the top. Sentences under it try to move a vote. At the bottom sits a name that is not a baker's everyday name. Neighbors will soon say yes or no to a written plan that would bind Kelpwick to Ashferry, Redmarsh, Pineford, and the rest of the towns on the same water.

Hold the sheet. It is not a dock rule. It is not a tax list. It is not the written plan. It is an argument, printed, aimed at people who can vote. A stranger can copy the number, copy the sentences, and walk them against the plan if the plan is later adopted. A shout in the tavern cannot be copied that way.

People later named the whole stack the **Federalist Papers**. The name arrives after the sheet. One sheet in that stack is a **paper**: a numbered public letter arguing for the plan. The Yale Avalon Project keeps a menu titled The Federalist Papers and lists them as No. 1 through No. 85. That count is from the menu at avalon.law.yale.edu. Each number is its own letter. The menu sits in Avalon's 18th Century shelf, marked 1700 to 1799.

The letter is trying to win a vote. Civic life already taught you a written rule, a vote, and an office. This letter does not yet bind. If the vote fails, the letter remains a letter. If the vote passes, the plan binds, and the letter is still not the plan. Mixing those two objects is how a school talk turns Publius into statute.

The name on the sheet is **Publius**. Chapter 8 treats who later stood behind that name. This chapter only asks you to see one numbered letter as a public argument with a number you can fetch.

Open Avalon No. 1 when you want the first letter in that host's list. A sentence from No. 1 quoted from memory is unverified. Copy from the page.

**Check.** Write three lines for the Market Street sheet: the number on it, who it is trying to move, and whether it is a rule or an argument. If you wrote "it is the plan," you merged the letter with the written plan. The letter argues. The plan, if adopted, binds. The voter in Kelpwick is the person the sheet is trying to move.

New case: a handbill with no number, signed "A Neighbor," that says the mill smells. That is a complaint. It is not a numbered letter in the stack. Do not put it on the Avalon menu. If your three lines for that handbill still say No. 1 through No. 85, you counted the wrong pile.

## 2. Union

Eleven towns sit on the same water. Each keeps its own militia list, its own tax list, and its own dock rules. A barge from Ashferry pays a fee at Kelpwick, another at Redmarsh, another at Pineford. A raid on the coast hits Quaybend. Kelpwick's meeting argues until dusk about whether to send 14 men. The minutes of the league say they will help. The 14 men stay home.

Before any school name, hold the two pictures. Picture one: the eleven towns act as one when a barge, a raid, or a debt crosses a town line. Picture two: each town is a neighbor with a veto, and a promise in the minutes is as strong as Tuesday's mood. The letters argue that picture two is already the fact, and that it will make neighbors into rivals.

People name the first picture a **union**: one bound set of towns under one written plan, with offices that can act on the people, not only on the town meetings. The name is a handle for the bind. It is not a mood that people get along.

The letters spend early numbers on this bind. Open Avalon No. 2 through No. 8 for war, peace, and rival neighbors, and No. 15 for a league of promises that cannot keep itself. Wording on those pages is unverified until you copy it. Do not invent a sentence about "one connected country" from a talk and print it as Publius.

The living civics pack is the constitution survey. If you need the plan's own text for how towns bind, name that door and stop. Here the object is the letters' claim that eleven dock rules and a raid with no 14 men are reasons to bind.

**Check.** Three dock fees on one barge: Kelpwick, Redmarsh, Pineford. Write the three fees as three acts, not as "trade is hard." Then write what one union dock rule would replace: one fee, or none, according to a plan you have not yet opened. Unverified: the plan's actual fee rule. If you filled that rule from a proverb, empty it.

New case: Quaybend is raided. Kelpwick votes 41 to 18 not to send the 14 men. The league minutes said they would send help. Write which object failed in public: the minutes, or the men. The minutes still exist. The men did not walk. Open Avalon No. 15 and copy one sentence about a league that cannot execute its own promises. If the page is dark, write unverified. Do not supply the sentence from school.

## 3. Faction

Kelpwick's meeting has 47 votes. Mill owners want a river gate that keeps water for their wheels through June. Upriver farms want the gate open so the fields drink. The mill side has 28 votes. The farm side has 19. On Tuesday the mill side passes the gate. June water leaves the fields. No one broke a lock. They used the meeting.

A group, majority or minority, bound by a passion or a stake, that uses the public chair to take what others need to keep, is the object. People name that group a **faction**. The name arrives after the gate. A faction can be a minority that would take if it could. A faction can be a majority that already did.

You cannot make mill and farm stop having different stakes. You cannot make 47 people love the same June. The letters treat those causes as given. The walk they offer is to control the effects: build a room where one stake cannot own the whole vote as easily as 28 owns 47.

Avalon No. 10 is the letter this chapter sends you to. A definition copied from memory, including any sentence that starts by saying what a faction is, is unverified until it sits next to that page. Open the door. Copy the sentence that names the group. If you cannot find it, write unverified.

Size does not settle the word. Twelve fishers who want a night curfew on mill noise are a small group. They still meet. They still petition. If they would shut the mills' night to keep their sleep, they are a faction in the same sense as the 28. The water and the noise are different stakes. The move is the same: a public chair used for the group's own pile.

**Check.** Write 28 mill votes and 19 farm votes in a meeting of 47. The gate passes. Write who lost the June water. If you wrote "the town won," you hid the 19. A faction can be a majority. Keep the 19 on the page.

New case: 12 fishers, not 28 mills. They lose the curfew vote 12 to 35. Write whether they are still a faction if their aim is to take the mills' night. If you said no because they lost, you made the word mean "winner." The letters' word tracks the stake and the public chair, not the trophy. Open Avalon No. 10 and put their 12 next to the sentence you copy. If the sentence will not take a minority, write what it will take, from the page.

## 4. Separation of offices

One person in Kelpwick writes the dock rule, collects the fee, and sits as judge when a barge disputes the fee. On Tuesday the same person is the rule, the purse, and the bench. The barge has nowhere to stand that is not already that person's chair.

Before the school name, count the acts. Writing a rule is one act. Collecting a 7-cent sack fee is a second act. Hearing the dispute is a third. Three acts in one body means the body can write a rule that makes itself rich, then judge that it was fair.

The letters argue that those jobs should not sit in one body. People name the split **separation of offices**. School talk also says separation of powers. The name is a handle for three chairs that can refuse each other, not for a mood that power is sinful.

Split is not enough if the writer can still empty the other two chairs at will. The letters also argue that each office needs a will of its own and a tool to push back. A collector who serves at the meeting's pleasure, for one case, is still the meeting. Chapter 6 treats the bench's tools. This chapter only asks you to see the three chairs.

Open Avalon No. 47 for combined departments, No. 48 for a meeting that overruns the others, and No. 51 for offices that check offices. Famous lines about angels, or about ambition, quoted from a talk, are unverified. Copy from those pages or write that you have not.

The living civics pack is the constitution survey. If you need how the written plan names Congress, the President, and the courts, name that door and stop. Here the object is the letters' argument that one body should not write, collect, and judge.

**Check.** Draw three chairs for Kelpwick: write the dock rule, collect the 7-cent sack fee, hear the barge dispute. Put one name on all three. Then split the names. If after the split the collector still writes the rule, you did not split. The 7 cents is still the fee. The chairs are the object.

New case: the meeting writes a rule that the collector may also judge "small" disputes under 4 sacks. Four sacks is still a bench. Count 4. The letters' warning is about combining jobs, not about the size of the pile. Open Avalon No. 47. Copy a sentence about combined departments only if you see it. Otherwise unverified.

## 5. A large republic

Kelpwick's meeting is 47 voters and two stakes, mill and farm. The mill side owns 28 of 47. Now add Ashferry's 61, Redmarsh's 33, Pineford's 54, and Quaybend's 40. The five-town pile is 235 voters. Trades multiply: mills, farms, fish, timber, salt, carts. A mill faction that owns Kelpwick does not yet own the 235.

A government by chosen officers, not a room where every person speaks every line, is the first object. People name that form a **republic** in these letters. **Large** means more people, more towns, more stakes. The claim: in a large republic, one faction has a harder time being a majority everywhere at once.

Harder is not never. Count. Mills have 28 in Kelpwick, 11 in Ashferry, 8 in Redmarsh, 6 in Pineford, 9 in Quaybend. That is 62 of 235. They still own Kelpwick. They do not own the five-town vote. If a salt faction holds 90 of 235, the large pile has a majority stake of its own. Size did not save the others. The letters do not promise a charm. They claim a thicker field of stakes.

Open Avalon No. 10 again for this claim, and No. 14 for a large republic as a workable map. A sentence about extending a sphere, quoted from memory, is unverified. Copy it from No. 10 or leave it off the page.

A small room can still be a republic if officers are chosen. A large room can still be captured. The working difference is the count of independent stakes, not the poetry of size.

**Check.** Write two shares and do not blend them. Share one: 28 of 47 in Kelpwick. Share two: 62 of 235 in the five towns. If you averaged those into "about half," you hid that the mill side owns the town and loses the union vote. Keep both fractions.

New case: a salt faction with 90 of 235. Write 90. Write that 90 is a majority of 235. If you erased 90 with a proverb about large republics, you spent the chapter's claim as a lullaby. The letters' claim is a tendency, not a lock. Open Avalon No. 10 and read whether the page admits a majority faction. Copy that admission if it is there. If you cannot find it, unverified.

## 6. Courts

A barge is seized at Kelpwick under a new town rule. The rule says the collector may keep any cargo labeled salt without a hearing. The written plan, if it has been adopted, says a person may be heard before cargo is kept. Who reads both pages, and can say the town rule yields?

A bench that reads the written plan and the statute, and keeps the plan when they clash, is the object. People name that bench a **court**. The letters argue for judges who hold office during good behavior, paid in a way the meeting cannot cut for a single case, so the bench is not the meeting's clerk for Tuesday.

Least-dangerous, good-behavior, and similar school phrases quoted from a talk are unverified. Open Avalon No. 78 and copy the sentences that do the work. If the page is dark, write unverified. Do not supply a famous line to look finished.

Term of years, salary figures, and the later case-law name for this reading are not in this chapter as numbers. Unverified. The living civics pack is the constitution survey: name it for the plan's own judicial text, and stop. This chapter owns the letter's argument that a bench must be able to refuse a rush.

Pay as a lever is part of the argument. If the meeting cuts a judge's pay by half in the week of the 7-cent fee case, the bench is being priced. The letters treat pay and term as tools to keep the reading independent of that week. Whether a given cut is lawful under the plan is a different walk. Do not settle it from this paragraph.

**Check.** Monday's rule: keep salt cargo with no hearing. The plan, if in force, says a person may be heard first. Write which page the bench is asked to read first. If you said "the mood of Monday," you replaced the plan with the meeting. Open Avalon No. 78. Copy one sentence about the bench and the written plan, or write unverified.

New case: the meeting cuts a judge's pay by half in the week of the 7-cent fee case. Write whether the cut sits in the same week as the case. If yes, the tool is being used as a lever. Exact years of term remain unverified here. Do not invent a number of years to fill the chair.

## 7. Tax

The union, if it is to keep a dock, a court, and a coast watch, must pay for them. A promise from each town to send money later is the league pattern. Tuesday's raid does not wait for Redmarsh's next harvest vote.

Coast watch costs 410 coin a month. Redmarsh promises 90, then sends 40. Kelpwick sends 110 as promised. Ashferry sends 0. Needed: 410. Arrived: 150. Short: 260. The watch is not a mood. It is 260 coin that did not appear.

A power in the union to raise money from people and trade, not only to request money from town meetings, is the object. People name that power a **tax** in these letters. The name is a handle for a purse the union holds, not for a proverb that government spends too much.

Open Avalon No. 12 for trade and revenue as a picture, No. 21 for a league that cannot tax well, and No. 30 for a government that must have its own revenue. Copy sentences from those pages. A remembered line about the power of the purse is unverified until it sits on the host.

A dock fee and a union tax are not automatically the same chair. Kelpwick can charge 7 cents on each of 41 sacks: 287 cents. That 287 is one town's dock. It does not fill the 410. If you treat 287 as the union's whole purse, you have mixed the chairs. The letters argue the union needs a purse that does not wait on Redmarsh's 40.

Rates, lists of what may be taxed, and later amendments live in the constitution survey. Name that door and stop. Here the object is the letters' claim that a union without its own revenue cannot keep the watch.

**Check.** Write 410 needed, 40 from Redmarsh, 110 from Kelpwick, 0 from Ashferry, 150 arrived, 260 short. If you wrote "towns should try harder," you spent a mood. The shortfall is 260. The letters argue a union that cannot tax cannot keep the watch.

New case: 7 cents on each of 41 sacks at Kelpwick dock. Write 287 cents. Label it a town dock fee. Then write the 410 watch as a different chair. Open Avalon No. 30 and copy one sentence about a government's own revenue. If you cannot copy it, unverified. Do not turn 287 into 410 because the numbers are both about money.

## 8. Who wrote it

## 9. How we know

You know a letter by a host, a number, and words you can copy. Memory is not a host. A school booklet with no host is not a host.

Door one: the Avalon Project menu, The Federalist Papers, No. 1 through No. 85. The host names Lillian Goldman Law Library, 127 Wall Street, New Haven, CT 06511, and the menu page carried a 2008 mark for that library. That is a reprint host, not the Market Street printer. A reprint can modernize spelling. A reprint can drop a paragraph. Two reprints can disagree. If they disagree, do not average the sentences.

A list of which founding documents sit on the page, beyond the title, is unverified until you copy it there. The Archives shelf is not the Avalon numbered list.
