# A roof, a pile, and a room you can see in

You can already read. This book is shelter, load, light.

Reed House sits at 14 Clay Street. Rain hits the roof. People walk the floor. Morning sun comes through one south glass. Those three facts are the object.

This pack owns the walk of a plan, a wall, a span, and light through an opening. What a force is lives in the mechanics pack. Name that door and stop. How a unit is fixed lives in the measurement pack. Name that door and stop.

This book is public general education.

Load-bearing public numbers come from four official pages: the Historic American Buildings Survey collection at loc.gov/pictures/collection/hh/, the U.S. Access Board home at access-board.gov, NIST at nist.gov, and GSA at gsa.gov. The HABS collection door did not return a usable page body. Survey counts are unverified here. A number that did not appear on those four pages is marked unverified, or it is a worked case invented for this house.

## 1. Shelter

Rain starts at 06:40 on Clay Street. You stand in the street and get wet. You step under Reed House's roof. The rain still falls. You do not. Wind that was in your face is now a sound on the other side of a wall. That difference is the first job of a building.

People name a roof-and-wall pile that keeps weather off a body **shelter**. The name is a handle for what you already did with your feet. You need a dry patch a body can occupy.

Reed House is one storey. The roof is a rectangle 8.0 metres along Clay by 5.5 metres back from the street. $8.0 \times 5.5 = 44.0$ square metres of roof over the house. A porch roof 2.2 metres deep by 5.5 metres wide sits on the street side. $2.2 \times 5.5 = 12.1$ square metres more. The porch keeps rain off the front door. It does not keep rain off the kitchen at the back. Shelter has a shape.

A shed at 9 Ash has three walls and a roof, open on the alley. Rain from the west still reaches the floor. That shed is shelter from the east and from the sky, not from the west. Write the open side.

Heat in July is weather too. Reed House with the south glass open at 14:00 is a different indoor weather from the same house with the glass shut and a blind down. The same roof that stops rain also traps heat unless an opening lets air move.

Holt Hall, two streets north, is a public building. Same first job: a dry floor, a roof, walls.

The Access Board home names accessibility standards for facilities covered by the Americans with Disabilities Act and the Architectural Barriers Act. It does not print a roof slope or a rain number. Those figures are unverified here. Chapter 6 owns the opening.

**Check.** Clay Street at 06:40, then the front room of Reed House. Write one fact for each place a body feels without a lab: wet, wind, dry floor. If both places got the same fact, you have not split street from shelter yet.

New case: the 9 Ash shed in a west rain. The floor along the open side is wet for 1.4 metres in. The back 2.0 metres are dry. Write the dry patch as 2.0 metres, not as "the shed." A third case: Holt Hall with a missing pane on the west. Rain reaches a strip of floor 0.6 metres wide. The hall still has a roof. The strip is not sheltered.

## 2. Load

Snow fell in the night. At 08:10 you go out to Reed House. The roof that was 44.0 square metres of dry felt is now 44.0 square metres of snow. You scoop 11 buckets from a 1.0 metre by 1.0 metre patch near the porch. Each bucket of snow, once melted in a pan, weighs 18 kilograms on a kitchen scale. $11 \times 18 = 198$ kilograms on that one square metre of patch. Do not multiply 198 across 44.0 and call the product a snow load for the house. The 198 kilograms is a measured pile on one patch. The pile on the rest is unverified until you scoop it, or until a door that owns snow load speaks.

That pile the shelter must carry is a **load**. People, furniture, the roof's own boards, wind against a wall, snow: each is a load. The name is a handle for what is pressing or pulling on this piece. What a force is lives in the mechanics pack. Name that door and stop. This chapter needs the pile and which piece takes it.

Three kinds you can point at. The roof boards, the tiles, the beams: those sit there empty. People name that the **dead load** when they want a handle for the building's own weight. Four people in the front room, a table, a stove: those come and go. People name that the **live load**. Wind on the west wall, snow on the 44.0 square metres: those arrive from weather. Write which kind you are looking at.

The front room is 5.5 metres along Clay by 4.0 metres deep. $5.5 \times 4.0 = 22.0$ square metres of floor. Eight guests at a table, then two. The floor did not change size. The live load did. A sag you can see with a string from wall to wall is a fact about this floor under this pile. A remembered number of kilograms per square metre for houses is unverified here. NIST's home names earthquake-resistant skyscrapers among the work it claims. It did not print a floor load, a snow load, or a wind number. Those figures are unverified here. Open NIST when a kilogram-per-square-metre claim must hold.

A post in the middle of the 22.0 square metres takes some of the roof's pile down to the ground. Remove the post and the same pile must walk farther through the beam. Chapter 7 owns that walk. This chapter owns the pile.

**Check.** 11 buckets, 18 kilograms each, one square metre of roof patch: 198 kilograms. A new case: 7 buckets of 16 kilograms from a different 1.0 metre patch on the shed at 9 Ash. $7 \times 16 = 112$ kilograms on that patch. Do not average 198 and 112 and call the mean the town's snow. The two roofs are two piles. Another: the front room with 8 chairs, then with 2. The dead load of the floor boards stayed. The live load changed. If you wrote one number for both days, you mixed the chairs with the boards.

## 3. Light

At 09:20 the south glass of Reed House throws a bright rectangle on the floor. You can read a page in that rectangle. In the north corner of the same room, 4.0 metres from the glass, the page is grey. Same hour, same room, two seeing-places. You did not change the sun. You changed where you stood.

That seeing, from the sun through glass or from a lamp, is **light** as this book uses the word. The pack owns this walk. Here the object is the room you can use with your eyes.

The south opening is 1.50 metres wide by 1.10 metres high. $1.50 \times 1.10 = 1.65$ square metres of glass. A west opening in the kitchen is 0.80 metres by 0.90 metres. $0.80 \times 0.90 = 0.72$ square metres. The front room's floor is 22.0 square metres. $1.65 \div 22.0 = 0.075$, about 8 parts in 100 of that floor if you compare glass area to floor area. That ratio is arithmetic on this house. It is not a daylight rule. Any official ratio that would say enough is unverified here. The Access Board, NIST, and GSA homes did not print one.

A lamp in the north corner at 21:00 makes a third seeing-place. The south glass is dark. Move the table into the lamp, or move the lamp to the table. Light has a location. Write which patch, which hour, which source.

A porch roof 2.2 metres deep shades the south glass in June at noon and lets a lower sun in at 09:20 in March. The same porch that was shelter in chapter 1 is a light tool here. Write both jobs.

Holt Hall's west wall is glass from a tape of 12.0 metres by 2.4 metres. $12.0 \times 2.4 = 28.8$ square metres. Afternoon glare on desks is a light fact, not a decoration fact. A blind is a moving wall for light. Chapter 5 owns the standing wall. This chapter owns the seeing.

**Check.** 09:20, south rectangle, page readable. North corner, page grey. Write the two patches. A new case: 21:00, lamp 1.2 metres from the table, page readable; lamp 4.0 metres away in the kitchen doorway, page grey. Same lamp, two distances. Another: kitchen west glass 0.72 square metres, at 16:10, a bright strip on the sink, the pantry dark. If you wrote that the kitchen is well lit, you hid the pantry.

## 4. A plan

A sheet on the kitchen table. You look down on Reed House as if the roof had been lifted off. Three rooms and a hall drawn as boxes. A thick line is a wall. A break in the thick line is a door. A thin rectangle in a thick line is a window. The street is a line along the bottom of the sheet. North is a mark at the top.

That looking-down drawing is a **plan**. The name is a handle for a view a bird would have, not for a hope about the future. People also say "floor plan." Same sheet. This pack owns that walk. A cut as if you sliced the house from side to side is a **section**. A picture of the street face is an **elevation**. Stay on the plan while the question is which room is next to which.

Reed House on the sheet, as taped:

- Front room: 5.5 metres along Clay, 4.0 metres deep. 22.0 square metres.
- Kitchen: 3.2 metres by 3.0 metres. $3.2 \times 3.0 = 9.6$ square metres, behind the west half of the front room.
- Bed: 3.5 metres by 3.0 metres. $3.5 \times 3.0 = 10.5$ square metres, behind the east half.
- Hall: the leftover of the 8.0 by 5.5 rectangle. House floor $8.0 \times 5.5 = 44.0$ square metres. $22.0 + 9.6 + 10.5 = 42.1$. Hall $44.0 - 42.1 = 1.9$ square metres, a passage 0.95 metres wide by 2.0 metres long.

Walk the sheet with a finger. Street, porch, front door in the middle of the Clay wall, hall, then a choice: west into the kitchen, east into the bed, or stay in the front room. Put the stove on the kitchen box. If the stove landed in the hall, the sheet is lying or the house is.

How a metre is fixed lives in the measurement pack. Name that door and stop. The numbers on this sheet are tape readings on Clay Street. A plan in feet is the same walk after you convert. Do not mix metres and feet in one sum.

A second sheet: Holt Hall. A lobby, a stair, a room of desks. The Access Board home points at Building & Sites for facilities covered by the ADA and the ABA. Inner clear-width numbers are unverified here. The plan still has to show an opening a body can use. Chapter 6 will stand in that opening.

**Check.** From memory, sketch Reed House as three boxes and a hall. Write 22.0, 9.6, 10.5, and 1.9 on the boxes. Add them. You should see 44.0. If you see 42.1, you dropped the hall. A new case: move the kitchen door from the hall to the north wall, onto the alley. Finger-walk from the front door to the stove. If the walk now goes through the bed, the sheet changed the life of the house. Another: a plan with no north mark. The south glass from chapter 3 cannot be placed. Mark north.

## 5. A wall

Stand in the front room and face Clay Street. A plane of plaster, 5.5 metres long, 2.55 metres to the ceiling, with a door break and the south glass. Knock. It is hard. It holds the roof's edge. It keeps the street's wind off the table. It is also the thing the south glass is cut from.

That standing plane is a **wall**. The name is a handle for a piece that stands, divides, and often carries. A wall that carries roof or floor down to the ground is a **bearing wall**. A wall that only divides, with the roof sitting on something else, is a **partition**. Reed House's Clay wall takes porch roof and front-room roof. It is bearing in this book because you can see the roof edge sitting on it. A thin cupboard-back in the kitchen, hollow to a knock, with the roof beams running the other way, is a partition. Write which.

Clay wall area before openings: $5.5 \times 2.55 = 14.025$ square metres. South glass 1.65 square metres. Door opening 0.90 metres wide by 2.05 metres high. $0.90 \times 2.05 = 1.845$ square metres. Solid wall $14.025 - 1.65 - 1.845 = 10.53$ square metres. The openings are holes in the wall. Chapter 6 owns the holes. This chapter owns what is left, and that the leftover still has to stand.

The Clay wall tapes 0.22 metres thick. The kitchen partition tapes 0.10 metres. Chapter 8 owns the stuff. A thick wall is slow to heat in the morning and slow to cool at night. Unverified as a number of hours. Plaster still warm on the south face at 21:00 is enough for this chapter.

A wall that leans is a load fact from chapter 2 showing up as a shape. A string with a small weight, hung from the ceiling next to the wall, is a plumb line. Gap at the floor larger than gap at the ceiling means the wall has moved. How far is too far is unverified here. NIST did not print a lean limit on the home page. Write the two gaps. Then open a door that owns a limit.

**Check.** Clay wall 5.5 by 2.55 is 14.025 square metres. Take 1.65 and 1.845. Remain 10.53 square metres of solid. A new case: the bed's east wall, 3.0 metres long, 2.55 metres high, one window 0.80 by 0.90. Wall $3.0 \times 2.55 = 7.65$. Window 0.72. Solid $7.65 - 0.72 = 6.93$ square metres. Another: kitchen partition 0.10 metres thick, Clay wall 0.22 metres. If you called both the walls with one thickness, you hid the bearing wall.

## 6. An opening

The front door of Reed House is shut. You cannot enter. You turn the latch. The leaf swings. You walk through a hole 0.90 metres wide and 2.05 metres high. The hole is the building. The swinging leaf is a tool for closing the hole. Light from chapter 3 used a different hole, filled with glass that does not swing for a body.

That hole in a wall, made on purpose for a body, for air, or for seeing, is an **opening**. A door is an opening you can pass, with a leaf you can shut. A window is an opening you see through, with glass, sometimes with a sash that moves. This pack owns that walk.

Reed House front opening: 0.90 metres wide. A chair 0.55 metres wide passes with 0.35 metres to spare. $0.90 - 0.55 = 0.35$. A second chair 0.95 metres wide does not pass. The number 0.90 is a tape on Clay Street. Whether 0.90 metres is enough for a public building, a wheelchair, or a rule with a name, is unverified here.

The Access Board home names the federal agency that advances accessible design standards and guidelines in alignment with the ADA, the ABA, and other federal laws. It points at Building & Sites for facilities covered by those two acts, and at animations including Maneuvering at Doors, Wheelchair Maneuvering, Protruding Objects, and Signs. Inner millimetre and inch figures on those animations were not opened. Every clear-width, slope, and reach number is unverified here. Open those doors when the opening must pass a public rule. A remembered door width stays off this page as a rule.

Holt Hall is the public case. Reed House is a dwelling on Clay Street. Which rule covers which building lives on the Access Board door. The Access Board home also tells you how to file an Architectural Barriers Act complaint. A public opening that a body cannot use is a complaint the Board will hear.

A window that opens is an air opening and a light opening at once. Kitchen west sash, 0.80 by 0.90, cracked 0.10 metres: air opening about $0.80 \times 0.10 = 0.08$ square metres. Same sash shut: air opening 0. Light opening still 0.72 square metres if the glass is clear. Write which job you are measuring.

**Check.** Chair 0.55 metres through a 0.90 metre leaf: 0.35 metres spare. Chair 0.95 metres: stop. A new case: Holt Hall lobby door. Do not copy Reed House's 0.90 metres onto Holt Hall and call it a pass. Open the Access Board Building & Sites door, and the maneuvering-at-doors animation. Unverified until those pages speak. Another: a letter slot 0.04 metres by 0.28 metres in the Clay door. $0.04 \times 0.28 = 0.0112$ square metres. That is an opening for paper, not for a body.

## 7. A span

The kitchen has no post in the middle. A beam runs 3.2 metres from the hall wall to the west wall. The ceiling sits on that beam. You walk under the 3.2 metres with a pan in both hands. Nothing stands in the walking line. That empty distance is the point of the beam.

How far a beam, a floor, or a roof goes between two things that hold it up is a **span**. The name is a handle for the empty run. This pack owns that walk. The push inside the beam lives next door in mechanics. Name that pack and stop.

Reed House kitchen span: 3.2 metres. Front room: the 5.5 metre Clay direction is cut by a beam that sits on the hall wall, so the longest empty run of ceiling is 4.0 metres toward the street, not 5.5. Write the empty run you can walk, not the longer wall. A porch beam 5.5 metres along Clay sits on a post at 2.75 metres from each end. Two spans of 2.75 metres, not one span of 5.5. Take the post away and you have claimed a 5.5 metre span. The beam is the same stick. The span changed. A sag you can see with a string is a fact. A number that says how much sag is allowed is unverified here.

Holt Hall's lobby is 11.0 metres between columns. Write 11.0 if no extra post was added. A later timber under the middle: two spans of 5.5 metres. The ceiling did not get shorter. The empty run did.

A deep beam, 0.30 metres from top to bottom, is a different stick from a 0.10 metre board trying to do 3.2 metres. You can tape both. You cannot finish a safe-span sentence from memory. NIST's home did not print span tables. Unverified here. Chapter 8 owns the stuff.

**Check.** Kitchen 3.2 metres empty, no post. A new case: porch 5.5 metres with a post at mid: two runs of 2.75 metres. Remove the post on paper. One run of 5.5 metres. Write which beam you would go look at for sag. Another: a shelf 0.90 metres between two brackets, books piled at the centre. The span is 0.90 metres. The pile is a live load from chapter 2. If the shelf bows, you have a span-and-load fact.

## 8. Material

The Clay wall is brick, painted. The kitchen partition is timber and plaster. The south opening is glass in a timber frame. The kitchen beam is timber. The porch posts are timber. The front-room floor is timber boards on timber joists. Knock them. They do not sound the same. They will not take water, fire, or a span the same way.

That stuff a piece is made of is **material**. The name is a handle for brick, timber, glass, steel, stone, as they sit in a piece you can point at. This chapter owns the choice as it shows up in shelter, load, and light.

Brick in the Clay wall: many small units in mortar. A crack follows the mortar more often than it cuts a brick. You can replace one brick. The 0.22 metre thickness is several bricks plus mortar, not one slab. Timber in the kitchen beam: one stick, grain along the 3.2 metre span. A knot in the middle of the underside is a fact about that stick. Glass: you see through it, it breaks rather than bends in ordinary use. Write those three behaviours. A sentence that says "good materials" without a piece has no object.

Water is a test. Brick on Clay Street, after the 06:40 rain, is dark on the outside face and dry on the inside face in this house today. The 0.22 metres took the rain and did not pass it. The shed at 9 Ash, timber wall, west rain, wet on the inside face along a missing flashing at the roof edge. Same storm, two details. The missing flashing is a joint, not a brick-versus-timber slogan.

GSA's home names real estate as one of the jobs it drives for the American government, along with acquisition, technology, and shared services. A 3 September 2026 news line on that home says GSA delivered 103 prospective capital projects to Congress and urged reform of an outdated repair approval process. A 10 September 2026 line says GSA saved more than $348 million with downtown Fort Worth dispositions. Those two numbers are about a public estate: what to repair, what to sell. They are not a brick strength. Inner GSA design pages were not opened. Any GSA brick or load figure is unverified here.

**Check.** Name three pieces in Reed House and the stuff of each: Clay wall brick, kitchen beam timber, south opening glass. Write one behaviour for each: brick as small units, timber as a span stick, glass as seeing that can break. A new case: 9 Ash west wall, timber, wet inside after rain at a roof edge. Write the joint at the roof edge, not that timber fails. Another: Holt Hall lobby, a steel column, painted. Do not call it timber because Reed House taught you timber. Tape it, knock it, write steel.

## 9. A place

Walk out of Reed House to Clay Street. Morning sun is on the south glass because Clay runs east-west here and the glass faces south. West wind from the river hits the kitchen wall. The street is 7.0 metres wide to the facing porches. A lorry on Clay rattles the sash. The ground under the porch is packed gravel, dry in July, puddled at the north-east corner in January. The house is not a box in the air. It is a box on this ground, in this sun, on this street.

That sitting of a building in sun, wind, ground, and street is **place** as this book uses the word. People also say site. Same sitting. A picture of a house with no street and no north is a drawing of a box.

Sun: south glass in Reed House is the light tool from chapter 3 because of this street. Turn the same plan 90 degrees on paper so the 5.5 metre wall faces west. The 1.65 square metres of glass now take afternoon sun and miss the morning rectangle. Shelter, load, and the plan's areas did not change. Light did. Place turned the plan.

Wind: west kitchen wall as the weather wall. A second house built 2.0 metres west, as tall as Reed House, would take some of that wind and some of that afternoon light. The 2.0 metres is a place fact. Unverified as a legal setback. A setback number lives on a town door, not on the four pages this book opened.

Ground: puddle at the north-east corner. Water sits because the ground is low or sealed. A bearing wall on wet ground is a load path into mud. How deep the footing sits is unverified here. Dig, or open a drawing that shows it.

NIST's home says the institute was established in 1901, and that it is marking 125 years. It names earthquake-resistant skyscrapers as part of the work that runs from nanoscale devices to those towers. It did not print a map of shaking under Clay Street. USGS keeps earthquake doors. This book did not open a USGS page. Any claim that Marrow is a shaking place, or is not, is unverified here. Open the USGS earthquake door when the ground's motion is the load.

GSA's home is a door for a public estate. Holt Hall, if it were a federal hall, would sit in that estate. Reed House does not. Write the owner before you walk a GSA door.

HABS, the Historic American Buildings Survey, is a Library of Congress pictures collection for buildings as recorded. The collection door did not yield a body. Whether Reed House has a survey sheet there is unverified here. Open the HABS door when the question is how a building was recorded.

**Check.** Write four facts for 14 Clay that a visitor can test without a lab: south glass, west kitchen, 7.0 metre street, puddle at the north-east corner. A new case: rotate the plan so the 1.65 square metres face west. Write what happens to the 09:20 rectangle from chapter 3. It leaves the morning. Another: a new house 2.0 metres west, same height. Write one light fact and one wind fact that change at Reed House.

## 10. A check

Take a pencil, a tape, and Reed House. The object of this book, used: shelter, load, light, walked as plan, wall, opening, span, material, and place, with every public millimetre, load table, and daylight rule fetched or marked unverified.

Do this on paper with a case the earlier chapters have not already closed.

A cousin wants to turn the 10.5 square metre bed into a shop with a street door of its own, cut through the east end of the Clay wall, and to take out the hall wall so the front room and the bed run together. Write the object, not a feeling about shops.

1. Shelter. A new door in the Clay wall is a new hole in the rain wall. The porch roof is 5.5 metres wide; the bed is on the east. Does the 12.1 square metre porch cover the new hole? If the new door sits outside the porch, you have invented a wet threshold. Draw it on the plan before you cut.
2. Load. Taking out the hall wall: is that wall bearing? Chapter 5 taped the Clay wall as bearing and the kitchen partition as not. The hall wall was not taped. Unverified. Look at the joists. If the joists sit on that wall, the wall is a load path. Chapter 7 owns the new empty run. Mechanics owns the push in the stick. Stop there for the push.

Unverified as a shop rule. A new Clay door with a glass leaf would add seeing from the south. Write the added glass area once you tape the leaf.
