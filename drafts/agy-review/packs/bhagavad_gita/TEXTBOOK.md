# A chariot on a field

Two armies on a field. A prince in a chariot between them. A bow set down. A driver who speaks. This book walks that scene, then the poem that keeps it. The name of the poem arrives after the scene.

A survey of many living traditions sits in the living religion pack. This book names that door and stops. It owns this walk: field, duty, action, knowledge, path, self, devotion, one verse, two readers, then a check against a primary door.

Load-bearing letters come from three pages this book opened: the GRETIL Bhagavadgita file, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy page on Śaṅkara, and Project Gutenberg's English poem *The Song Celestial* by Edwin Arnold. A Library of Congress catalog door is listed. This book did not retrieve a class number from it. Treat that number as unverified until you open the door.

Public general education.

---

## 1. A field

Ash Ford is a gravel crossing between two farms. Harvest week. Both families have come with kin, carts, and tools. You stand in a cart in the middle of the ford. On the near bank is the cousin who slept under your roof. On the far bank is the uncle who taught you to sharpen a scythe. Eleven faces you can name. If the carts roll, someone you ate with will be hurt.

You set the scythe down.

That piece of ground is first just ground: water, stones, two banks. It is also the place where a fitting act is supposed to happen, and where it has stalled. The stall is the object. The name can wait.

The Bhagavad Gita opens on such a stall. A blind king asks what happened when two kin-groups, eager to fight, stood together on a named plain. The GRETIL file prints the first verse as:

> dharmakṣetre kurukṣetre samavetā yuyutsavaḥ
> māmakāḥ pāṇḍavāś caiva kim akurvata sañjaya 1.1

The first word is not a weapon. It is a field of dharma, then the field of Kuru. Ordinary English: a ground where the fitting act is at stake, and a ground with a family name. The king wants a report, not a mood.

Project Gutenberg's Arnold poem, chapter 1, gives the king's question in English:

> Ranged thus for battle on the sacred plain--
> On Kurukshetra--say, Sanjaya! say
> What wrought my people, and the Pandavas?

Hold the cart. One speaker cannot see the field and asks. Another speaker can see it and will tell. The prince who must act is not yet the one talking. The book begins as a report of a stall.

By the end of that first stretch the prince has done the Ash Ford move. GRETIL closes the stretch:

> evam uktvārjunaḥ saṅkhye rathopastha upāviśat
> visṛjya saśaraṃ cāpaṃ śokasaṃvignamānasaḥ 1.47

He has spoken. He sits down in the chariot. He has let go the bow with its arrow. His mind is shaken by grief. Arnold's chapter 1 ends:

> Arjuna sank upon his chariot-seat,
> And let fall bow and arrows, sick at heart.

People name that ground a **field**. The Sanskrit handle is *kṣetra*. In this book the field is the place where a fitting act and a family name sit on the same stones, and a body refuses the act.

GRETIL titles that first stretch *arjunaviṣādayogaḥ*: the joining that starts from Arjuna's distress. Arnold titles it "Arjun-Vishad," "The Book of the Distress of Arjuna." Distress is not a preface you skip. It is chapter 1 of the walk.

A date for when the poem was first spoken is unverified here. Open the living file and the English poem. Do not invent a century.

**Check.** Write three chairs for Ash Ford. Who asks: the person who cannot see the banks. Who can see: you, in the cart. What stalled: the scythe in your hand, with eleven named faces in view. If you wrote "war is sad," you named a fog. Put the scythe and the eleven faces in the sentence.

New case: same ford, zero named faces, only hired hands you met this week. The stall may still be real, but it is not the Gita's stall. The Gita's first field is kin on both banks. Write the difference in one line.

## 2. A duty

The mill at Ash Ford has a night chair. You hold the license this week. Fourteen sacks must be ground before the bakery opens at 04:00. The cousin on the far bank is named on a lawsuit over the ford. You do not want to feed a bakery that will sell bread to that cousin's house. The stones do not care. The license is in your name. The fourteen sacks are on the floor.

A fitting act that belongs to the chair you actually sit in, not to the mood you have about the people in view, is what this chapter needs. People name that fitting act a **duty**. The Gita's handle is *svadharma*: the dharma that is one's own.

After the prince is told that the one who lives in a body is not slain when the body is slain, the next turn is not a cloud. GRETIL prints:

> svadharmam api cāvekṣya na vikampitum arhasi
> dharmyād dhi yuddhāc chreyonyat kṣatriyasya na
> vidyate 2.31

Look also at your own dharma. You ought not tremble. For a person in the warrior chair, nothing else is better than a fight that is dharmic. The chair in the verse is the prince's chair, not yours. The move you can use is the look: name the chair, then name the act that chair already owed, then see whether trembling is about the act or about the faces.

Your chair at 19:00 is miller, licensed, fourteen sacks. "Be a good cousin" names a mood. "Win a lawsuit" names a second mood. If you leave the stones still because you dislike a house, you dropped the dharma you already had.

A written statute belongs to another book. Name that door and stop. An office you can vote out belongs to the civic door. Name it and stop. The Gita's duty is spoken to one body on one field, about the work that body already stood in.

Do not steal the prince's chair. You are asked to see how a text talks when a person wants a smaller job than the one they already hold.

**Check.** License, 14 sacks, bakery at 04:00, stones still at 19:00. Write the owed act: 14 sacks ground. Write the mood that is not the act: dislike of the far-bank house. If those two lines are the same, you have not split duty from mood.

New case: the license is not yours. You are a hired hand for 3 hours. The owed act is 3 hours of turning, not 14 sacks. If you stay until 04:00 unpaid, you invented a chair. Write the 3.

## 3. Action as the text uses it

The millstones turn. Flour falls. At 04:00 the bakery may thank you, or it may not. It may pay on time, or it may not. The turning and the thanks are not the same chair.

The Gita's hard line on that split sits in the second stretch. GRETIL prints:

> karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadācana
> mā karmaphalahetur bhūr mā te saṅgostv akarmaṇi
> 2.47

Four refusals, in order. Your claim is in action. Never in the fruits. Do not make the fruit the cause of what you do. Do not cling to not-acting.

People name the act **action** as this text uses it. The Sanskrit handle is *karma*. In street talk karma often means payback that arrives later. That is not the chair of 2.47. Here karma is the turning of the stones, the work of the hands, the act that can be done tonight.

Arnold's English for those four refusals is on the Gutenberg door. This book's fetch of that poem did not return the 2.47 lines. Do not paste a private English. Teach the move from the Sanskrit, then open Arnold and copy what you find.

A later verse keeps the mill running. GRETIL:

> tasmād asaktaḥ satataṃ kāryaṃ karma samācara
> asakto hy ācaran karma param āpnoti pūruṣaḥ 3.19

Therefore, unattached, always do the action that is to be done. Doing the action without clinging, a person reaches the further thing the text names. The next line, 3.20, says even people who already stand complete still act, looking also to the holding-together of the world. The handle there is *lokasaṃgraha*.

Action in this book is not busyness. Sitting still because you want a cleaner score is still a choice about the stones. 2.47 refuses that cling as well.

**Check.** 14 sacks on the floor at 19:00. By 02:00 you have ground 11. The bakery's thanks are not in the room. Write the act-chair: 11 done, 3 left. Write the fruit-chair: thanks, pay, the cousin's bread. If you mark the night a failure because no one has thanked you, you sat in the fruit-chair and called it the act.

New case: you grind all 14 and then hide 2 sacks so the far-bank house gets less bread. The turning was done. A second act began: taking 2 from a floor that was not yours to cut. Write both acts. 2.47 did not license the hiding.

## 4. Knowledge

At 01:00 the stones seize. You sit. A hired hand walks in and says you did nothing. From the door, the mill is quiet. From the chair, you are in the middle of an act: finding the jam, clearing it, or choosing to let 3 sacks stay whole until dawn.

The Gita wants a seeing that can tell those two pictures apart. GRETIL:

> karmaṇy akarma yaḥ paśyed akarmaṇi ca karma yaḥ
> sa buddhimān manuṣyeṣu sa yuktaḥ kṛtsnakarmakṛt
> 4.18

Who would see non-action in action, and action in non-action, that one is the intelligent one among humans, joined, the doer of the whole of action.

People name that seeing **knowledge** as this text uses it. The handle is *jñāna*. A pile of facts about kings will not do the job. The seeing is a way of looking at an act so you do not mistake a quiet body for an empty chair, or a busy body for a finished duty.

Arnold titles his fourth chapter "Jnana Yog," "The Book of the Religion of Knowledge." GRETIL's fourth stretch is the knowledge-joining. The title is a handle. The work is 4.18, done on a mill floor.

A second knowledge-claim in the same stretch is about the speaker. GRETIL:

> yadā yadā hi dharmasya glānir bhavati bhārata
> abhyutthānam adharmasya tadātmānaṃ sṛjāmy aham
> 4.7

When, and whenever, there is a wasting of dharma, and a rising-up of what is not dharma, then I send forth myself. The next line on that door, still in the same turn, names a protecting of the good and a destroying of the ill-done. This book does not paste a private English for 4.8. Open GRETIL and read the next couplet.

Knowledge here is whether you can see, in one night, the difference between a seized stone and a refused sack.

The living philosophy pack surveys schools of knowing. Name it and stop.

**Check.** Stones seize at 01:00. 3 sacks still whole. Write one sentence in which sitting is an act (clearing the jam). Write one sentence in which sitting is a refusal (leaving the jam, hoping dawn will forgive the 3). If the two sentences match, you have not done 4.18.

New case: you walk the mill, oil every shaft, and grind 0 sacks. Busy, and the bakery still opens short. Write the action that was missing: turning grain. Knowledge that names every shaft and misses the 14 is a list. It is not the seeing 4.18 asked for.

## 5. A path in the text

At the gate of Ash Ford two tracks leave the same post. One climbs to a shed where a person sits still and sorts what is lasting from what is not. One stays on the mill floor and turns stones without clinging to the flour. Both tracks start because the scythe went down in chapter 1. Neither track is a brand of exercise class.

The prince asks for one answer. The speaker gives two standings that were already told. GRETIL:

> lokesmin dvividhā niṣṭhā purā proktā mayānagha
> jñānayogena sāṅkhyānāṃ karmayogena yoginām 3.3

In this world a twofold standing was declared by me of old: by the knowledge-joining of the Sāṅkhya people, by the action-joining of the yoga people.

People name that pair a **path** in the text. The handle is *niṣṭhā*, a standing, and *yoga*, a joining. Yoga in this book is how a person is joined to the work of seeing, or to the work of acting, or, later, to the speaker.

Arnold's third chapter is titled "Karma-Yog," "The Book of Virtue in Work." His second is "Sankhya-Yog," "The Book of Doctrines." The English titles are one poet's handles. They should not replace 3.3. 3.3 says the two standings were already spoken. The book does not throw one away to keep the other.

Later chapters will add devotion as a joining. That is chapter 7. Here the job is to see that the stalled prince is offered more than one way to stand, and that both ways are joinings, not moods.

If you "pick knowledge" so you can leave the 14 sacks, you did not take the Sāṅkhya standing. You took a nap and borrowed a name. If you "pick action" so you can grind and hate, you did not take the karma-joining. You took 2.47 and dropped the unattached half.

**Check.** Two tracks from one post. Track A: sit in the shed and sort lasting from not, then return to the stones. Track B: turn the stones without waiting for thanks. Write what each track does with the 14 sacks. If either track writes "0 sacks, and that is the point," you have left the Gita's 3.3, which does not offer a hole in the floor.

New case: you try both in one night. 2 hours sorting in the shed, 5 hours on the stones. Write the hours. The text's two standings are not a schedule. They are two ways of being joined. A night can hold both without you having "solved" 3.3. Do not force a winner.

## 6. The self as the text uses it

A coat hangs on a peg in the mill. In winter you put it on. In thaw you take it off. You are not the coat. You are the one who wears it and sets it down. Bodies in the Gita are talked about that way: put on, worn, set down.

GRETIL, still in the second stretch:

> na jāyate mriyate vā kadācin
> nāyaṃ bhūtvā bhavitā vā na bhūyaḥ
> ajo nityaḥ śāśvatoyaṃ purāṇo
> na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre 2.20

It is not born, it does not die, at any time. This one, having been, will not come to be again-not. Unborn, constant, everlasting, ancient. It is not slain when the body is being slain.

Arnold puts that verse into English the Gutenberg door still hosts:

> Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never;
> Never was time it was not; End and Beginning are dreams!
> Birthless and deathless and changeless remaineth the spirit for ever;
> Death hath not touched it at all, dead though the house of it seems!

The next turn on GRETIL is the coat:

> vāsāṃsi jīrṇāni yathā vihāya
> navāni gṛhṇāti naroparāṇi
> tathā śarīrāṇi vihāya jīrṇāni
> anyāni saṃyāti navāni dehī

As a person, having set down worn-out clothes, takes other new ones, so the embodied one, having set down worn-out bodies, goes to other new ones.

People name that wearer the **self** as the text uses it. The handle is *ātman*, and in 2.20 the verses also say *purāṇa*, ancient, and *dehī*, the one with a body. This book does not settle, from one night's mill, whether that self is the same as the speaker, or the same as every other wearer. Chapter 9 is where two readers disagree. Here the job is the coat: do not count the coat as the person when the text is talking about the wearer.

GRETIL's file labels that clothes verse 2.20 after it has already printed 2.21. Printed books usually count it as 2.22. Chapter 10 will make you look at that slip. For this chapter, hold the picture.

A later line turns the miller's own night back on the miller. GRETIL 6.5:

> uddhared ātmanātmānaṃ nātmānam avasādayet
> ātmaiva hy ātmano bandhur ātmaiva ripur ātmanaḥ

One should lift the self by the self. One should not cause the self to sink. The self is the friend of the self. The self is the enemy of the self.

The stall in chapter 1 was kin on both banks. 6.5 says the night can also be lost inside one body.

**Check.** Coat on a peg. Body on a chariot seat. Write what 2.20 says happens when the house looks dead: the wearer is not slain. Write what 6.5 says you can still do to yourself before any far-bank cousin arrives: lift, or sink. If you wrote only "the soul is immortal," you copied a slogan and dropped the friend-or-enemy line.

New case: a mill-hand dies on the floor at 00:40. The Gita's 2.20 is not a form you fill for a burial office. Write one sentence the text allows: the wearer is not slain with the body. Write one sentence the text has not yet given you: what the town should do with the body. If you use 2.20 to skip the town's work, you stole a verse.

## 7. Devotion

The mill key hangs on a hook. Some nights you keep it. Some nights you put it in a hand you trust with the whole dark, not with a slogan about trust. You still turn stones. You have handed the night.

The Gita names a joining that is that handing, aimed at the speaker in the chariot. GRETIL 9.22:

> ananyāś cintayanto māṃ ye janāḥ paryupāsate
> teṣāṃ nityābhiyuktānāṃ yogakṣemaṃ vahāmy aham

People who, thinking of me as not-another, sit around me in worship: of those who are joined always, I carry the yoga and the keeping-safe.

GRETIL 12.2, when the prince asks which worshippers are more joined:

> mayy āveśya mano ye māṃ nityayuktā upāsate
> śraddhayā parayopetāḥ te me yuktatamā matāḥ 12.2

Those who, having caused the mind to enter me, joined always, worship me, endowed with the further faith: they are thought by me the most joined.

Arnold titles that twelfth stretch "Bhaktiyog," "The Book of the Religion of Faith." GRETIL's own heading for the twelfth stretch is on that Sanskrit door. The English word **devotion** is this book's handle for *bhakti* as the text uses it: mind set on the speaker, not a playlist of feelings.

Near the end the speaker gathers the handing into a few lines. GRETIL 18.65–18.66:

> manmanā bhava madbhakto madyājī māṃ namaskuru
> mām evaiṣyasi satyaṃ te pratijāne priyosi me 18.65
> sarvadharmān parityajya mām ekaṃ śaraṇaṃ vraja
> ahaṃ tvā sarvapāpebhyo mokṣyayiṣyāmi mā śucaḥ
> 18.66

Become one whose mind is on me, my devotee, my worshipper; bow to me. You will come to me; I promise you truly; you are dear to me. Having set all dharmas aside, go to me alone as refuge. I will release you from all ill-deeds. Do not grieve.

18.66 is not a permission to dump the 14 sacks. It sits after a whole book of chairs: field, duty, action, knowledge, path, self. A person who uses it to skip 2.47 has not read the line. A person who refuses to see that the book also ends in refuge has not read the line either.

The living religion pack is the survey of devotion across traditions. Name it and stop.

**Check.** Key on a hook. 14 sacks. Write what 9.22 adds that 2.47 did not say: the speaker carries the keeping of those who sit toward him as not-another. Write what it does not erase: the sacks still have to be ground by someone in a chair. If your sentence is only "have faith," you dropped both the sacks and the speaker.

New case: you say 18.66 aloud and leave the mill at 19:05 with 14 sacks still whole. The verse named refuge. It did not name 19:05 as closing time. Write the unpaid chair from chapter 2. Devotion that cancels the license is a mood wearing a line.

## 8. A verse

A wall is a pile. A brick is one brick. If you cannot hold one brick, you do not have the wall.

The brick for this chapter is 2.20, already met. Walk it again, slower. GRETIL:

> na jāyate mriyate vā kadācin
> nāyaṃ bhūtvā bhavitā vā na bhūyaḥ
> ajo nityaḥ śāśvatoyaṃ purāṇo
> na hanyate hanyamāne śarīre 2.20

Word by word, in ordinary English, without pretending this is a hidden translation you must memorize. *na jāyate*: is not born. *mriyate vā*: or dies. *kadācit*: at any time. *nāyam*: this one is not. *bhūtvā bhavitā vā na bhūyaḥ*: having come to be, will not come to be again. *ajaḥ*: unborn. *nityaḥ*: constant. *śāśvataḥ ayam purāṇaḥ*: this one everlasting, ancient. *na hanyate*: is not slain. *hanyamāne śarīre*: when the body is being slain.

Arnold's four English lines, already quoted in chapter 6, are the public-domain poem of that brick. Copy them from Gutenberg when you open the door. If your copy disagrees with this book's copy, keep the door.

A verse in this walk is a numbered stretch on a primary door, not a motto on a poster. GRETIL numbers this one 2.20. Arnold does not print "2.20" beside the English. You match them by place: after the lines that say life cannot slay, before the worn-clothes picture.

The Gita's first stretch on GRETIL runs through 1.47. Its last spoken line from the prince on that door is 18.73:

> naṣṭo mohaḥ smṛtir labdhā tvatprasādān mayācyuta
> sthitosmi gatasaṃdehaḥ kariṣye vacanaṃ tava 18.73

The bewilderment is gone. Memory is got. By your grace. I stand. Doubt is gone. I will do your word.

18.73 is a close of the stall from 1.47. The bow that was set down is picked up as an act, not as a mood. This chapter still keeps 2.20 as the brick you can carry in a pocket. 18.73 is the door of the house. Do not swap them.

**Check.** Write 2.20's four Sanskrit lines from the GRETIL door, not from this page. Then write Arnold's four English lines from Gutenberg. If you cannot open a door, write unverified and stop. Do not invent a fifth line to make it prettier.

New case: take 1.1 instead of 2.20. Same two doors. Sanskrit from GRETIL, English from Arnold's first three lines of chapter 1. If your English names a "sacred plain" and "Kurukshetra," you have Arnold's brick. If your English names a different plain, you opened a different poem. Keep the name of the poem you actually opened.

## 9. Two commentaries

Two neighbors read the same mill log. One says the night was about flour. One says the night was about who holds the key. The log did not change. The chairs did.

A commentary is a second voice on the same letters. If you cannot point at the verse the commentator is reading, you have a speech.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy page *Śaṅkara*, first published 4 October 2021, is one scholarly door this book opened. It says Śaṅkara flourished during the eighth century CE, and that he composed our earliest complete extant commentaries on the *Brahmasūtras*, the *Bhagavadgītā*, and ten principal Upaniṣads. It says the Upaniṣads, the *Bhagavadgītā*, and the *Brahmasūtras* constitute Advaita Vedānta's triple canon, the *prasthānatrayī*. It says Advaita Vedānta articulates radical nondualism: the essential core of one's self (*ātman*) is *brahman*, one without a second.

That is voice A, as a living encyclopedia states it. On that same page, Śaṅkara places great emphasis on moral virtues and acting for the good of the world, and it points at his Gita commentary on chapter 3 for that emphasis. Nondual knowledge is not, on that door, a license to sneer at the mill floor.

Voice B: another classical reader of the same Gita is Rāmānuja, whose commentary GRETIL's register lists as a *Bhagavadgītābhāṣya*. This book did not open that commentary file. Do not paste a private English of Rāmānuja. Open the GRETIL door. If the file is dark, write unverified.

The method you can still use tonight: take 2.47. Voice A, after the encyclopedia's Śaṅkara, will tend to read the unattached act as training toward knowledge that the doer is not a second thing beside *brahman*. Voice B, as a second Vedānta chair, will tend to read the same unattached act as service that keeps a Lord and a devotee from collapsing into one blank. Do not average the two chairs. Write both. Then go back to the verse.

GRETIL also lists other Gita commentaries, including a four-voice file and Yāmuna's *Gītārthasaṃgraha*. Those are doors, not this chapter's fetch. A count of all Gita commentaries is unverified here.

Two commentaries show that 2.47 can bear more than one finished night. If both voices agree, write the agreement. If they do not, keep both sentences.

**Check.** Open the Śaṅkara encyclopedia page. Copy the first-published date you see. This book saw 4 October 2021. If you see that date, you have the living page this book used. If you see a later revision line this book did not have, keep the door and mark this book's date as old.

New case: 18.66. Write one sentence voice A might stress (refuge as the last name of a knowledge that was already in chapter 4). Write one sentence voice B might stress (refuge as going to a Lord who remains). If your two sentences are the same, you have not yet made two chairs. If you cannot fetch Rāmānuja, mark voice B unverified as a quote, and still write the chair as a question the verse can hold.

## 10. A check against a primary door

The mill log is not the mill. This book is not the Gita. A primary door is a page that hosts the letters. GRETIL hosts Sanskrit. Gutenberg hosts Arnold's English. The encyclopedia hosts a scholarly account of one commentator. Library of Congress hosts catalog records of editions. Open them.

Do this, in order, on paper.

One. Field. Open GRETIL. Find 1.1. Copy *dharmakṣetre kurukṣetre*. If those two words are not on the page, you are in the wrong file. Then open Arnold chapter 1. Copy the lines that name the sacred plain and Kurukshetra. If you cannot open either door, write unverified and stop. Do not finish from memory.

Two. Distress. GRETIL 1.47: the prince sits in the chariot and lets go the bow. Arnold's chapter 1 end: he sank upon his chariot-seat and let fall bow and arrows. Match the stall, not the punctuation.

Three. Eighteen joinings. Arnold's Gutenberg page prints chapter headings I through XVIII, and an end-line for each, from "The Book of the Distress of Arjuna" through "The Book of Religion by Deliverance and Renunciation." Count the chapter-end markers. Write 18 if you count 18. If you count another number, keep the door.

Four. Numbering slip. On GRETIL, 2.20 is the unborn-verse. The next numbered line is 2.21. The clothes-verse that follows is labeled 2.20 again on that file. Printed books usually call the clothes-verse 2.22. Write both labels. Do not average 2.20 and 2.22 into 2.21. The file and the shelf can disagree. The disagreement is the check.

Five. Action line. Copy 2.47 from GRETIL, the two lines that begin *karmaṇy evādhikāras te*. Then open Arnold's second chapter and find the English that carries those four refusals. This book did not retrieve that English on its fetch. Your copy is the living one. If the host is dark, write unverified.

Six. Two standings. Copy 3.3 from GRETIL. Knowledge-joining and action-joining. If you write "many paths" and stop, you left the verse.

Seven. Self. Copy Arnold's four lines that begin "Never the spirit was born." Match them to GRETIL 2.20, not to 18.66.

Eight. Devotion. Copy 18.66 from GRETIL. Then look at 18.73. Write what returned that 1.47 had dropped: the prince will do the word. If you end the book at refuge and never see 18.73, you closed early.

Nine. Commentator. Open the Śaṅkara page. Write eighth century CE as that door states his flourishing, and 4 October 2021 as its first-published date, or write what the door now shows. Write that the Gita sits in a triple canon on that page. A Library of Congress class number for an English-Sanskrit edition is unverified in this book.

Ten. The living religion pack remains the survey. Name it. Stop. Do not paste its map into this walk.

A composition date for the Gita, a count of all its verses, and a class number from a catalog this book did not retrieve stay unverified. Empty hits stay empty.

**Check.** 14 sacks, 1.1, 1.47, 2.20, 2.47, 3.3, 18.66, 18.73, 18 chapter-end lines, one encyclopedia date. If those ten are not on your paper from doors, you described a famous poem and you did not walk it.

New case: a second English poem, not Arnold. Same Sanskrit 2.20. If the second poem's host is not public-domain or scholarly, do not paste it. Teach the move from GRETIL. The object is still the chariot on the field, not a stack of unmatched mottos.
