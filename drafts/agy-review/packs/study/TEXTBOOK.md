# Keeping a page

You can already read. This book is the next skill: keeping what a page is doing, checking it, and using it tomorrow.

The object of this book is one working page that stays. Not a pile of highlighted sentences. Not a feeling that you were present while words went by.

This pack teaches anyone who can read how to hold a page from a library of first books.

---

## 1. One object

Open any chapter in this library. The writer is trying to put one working object in your head: a falling stone, a vote, a cell, a promise in a contract.

Walk into a shop for salt. If you leave with a bag of everything and no salt, you were busy and you still missed the thing you came for. The rest of this chapter is about the salt, not the shop.

Before you read for keeps, name that object in a short sentence you could say aloud. "A fraction is a share of a whole." If you cannot say the sentence, you have not found the object yet. Skim until you can. Then read that stretch slowly.

The name the book uses may arrive late on purpose. Let the picture land first. The name is a handle for a thing you already saw.

Copying the look of study without the object is an old failure. In 1974 Richard Feynman spoke at Caltech's commencement about people who built the form of a landing strip, the wooden headphones, the fires along a runway, and then waited. The planes did not land. He called that cargo cult science: the surface of the work with the working piece missing. The talk lives on Caltech's library door in `LINK_INDEX.md`.

A chapter you highlight from end to end, and cannot name tomorrow, is that runway. The fires were lit. Nothing landed.

**Check.** Open chapter 5 of this book. Do not reread this paragraph. Write one sentence for what that chapter is trying to leave in your head. A sentence that works names a home for a number you did not invent. A sentence that fails says "be careful with numbers" and stops. That second sentence is a mood. It is not the object.

New case: a civics page about who can stop an office. Your sentence must name the office and the stop. If you wrote "government is complicated," you named a fog. Skim until the office and the stop are both in the sentence.

## 2. The question you bring

A page answers a question. If you arrive with none, the page feels like weather.

Write one question at the top of your paper. "What happens to the other side if I do this to this side?" "Who can stop this office?" "How does the body know to bleed less?"

Read to answer that question. When the page answers a different one, note it and return. Curiosity is legal. Wandering until the first question is lost is how a chapter turns into fog.

The question is a filter, not a prison. A good page will try to teach you a nearby fact. Write that fact in a margin as a second question, then go back to the first. Two questions on the paper are a map. Zero questions is a tour.

OpenStax keeps a catalog of free textbooks at the subjects door in `LINK_INDEX.md`. Each book in that catalog is built around a kind of question: how a body is put together, how a force changes a motion, how a vote is counted. The catalog does not bring your question for you. You still write one. I did not count the books on that door in this sitting. If a page tells you how many titles sit there, mark the count unverified until you open the door and see the list yourself.

**Check.** Take one paragraph from chapter 3. Read it twice, with two different questions at the top of the paper. First: "What must my hand do?" Second: "What goes wrong if I only watch?" The two notes must not be the same paragraph copied twice. If they are, you collected sentences. You did not answer.

New case: the same paragraph, a third question, "When am I done?" Your note should name a finished act (a new number done on paper, a drawing labeled, a rule with a breaking case). If the note is "when I finish the chapter," you measured time. The question asked for a done act.

## 3. Paper is part of the book

Eyes can slide. A hand cannot.

When the book shows a step, copy the step onto paper and do it with a new number. When the book describes a part, draw the part and label what you can see. When the book states a rule, write the rule in your own words, then write one case that obeys it and one case that would break it.

Those three moves are the reading. They are not extra. A page you only watched is a page you visited.

If you cannot do the step with a new number, you copied a shape. Go back one paragraph. Printed digits can sit in the right chairs and still be a copy. The hand finds out whether the places bundled.

A worked step from a numeracy page: 8 and 5. Count on from 8: 9, 10, 11, 12, 13. Ones past nine bundle into a ten. That is the move the page wanted. Watching the 8 and the 5 sit next to a printed 13 does not do the move.

**Check.** The book showed 8 + 5. You do 9 + 6 on paper. Write the ones, write the bundle if there is one, write the tens. 9 ones and 6 ones are 15 ones: 1 ten and 5 ones. The sum is 15.

New case: 27 + 18. 7 ones and 8 ones are 15 ones: write 5, carry 1 ten. 2 tens and 1 ten and the carried ten are 4 tens. The sum is 45. If 9 + 6 was easy and 27 + 18 stalls, the first sum was copying the book's 13. The second sum is the reading.

A breaking case for the same rule: do not add 3 meters to 50 centimeters as 53 of anything. Convert first, or the places are not the same kind of chair. Units belong to the measurement pack. The study move is the same: a new number, on paper, or you do not have the step.

## 4. A word you do not have

Stop. Do not guess from the rest of the sentence if the word is load-bearing: a number, a name of a part, a verb that changes the claim.

A load-bearing word is one that, if swapped, would change what the sentence is willing to stand on. "The charge of the electron" is load-bearing. "Nice" in "a nice proof" is not. Guessing "nice" from tone is harmless. Guessing "electron" from atmosphere is how a wrong object gets a fluent name.

Three places to look, in this order:

1. See whether this same book taught it earlier. The table of contents is a map, not decoration.
2. See whether another pack in this library is listed as a prior book.
3. Use the pack's link index. Official doors own facts. A random explainer does not.

For ordinary English, Merriam-Webster is the dictionary door in `LINK_INDEX.md`. For a subject word, the subject door beats a general dictionary. "Work" in physics is not "work" in a job listing. Put the new word on paper with one example you could point at. Then return to the sentence that stopped you.

If the word is a name of a unit or a constant, skip ahead to chapter 5 and open the standards door. A dictionary will tell you how people use the word. It will not tell you the living value.

**Check.** The word is *hypotenuse*. Do not finish the sentence from the sound of "long side." Draw a right triangle that is not the book's picture: one corner square, the two shorter sides 3 and 4 of any unit you like. The hypotenuse is the side that sits opposite the square corner. In this drawing that side is 5, because 3×3 + 4×4 = 9 + 16 = 25, and 5×5 is 25. If you cannot point at the side on your drawing, you still do not have the word.

New case: the word is *denominator*. Draw a loaf cut into 8 equal pieces. You hold 3. The denominator is 8, the count of equal cuts in the whole, not the 3 you hold. Write $\frac{3}{8}$. If you labeled 3 as the denominator, you named the piece you like instead of the cut.

## 5. A number on the page

A number that matters has a home: a standards body, a statute, a measurement institute, a textbook that shows the count.

If the number is a constant, a dose, a legal threshold, or a conversion, open the door in `LINK_INDEX.md` and look. If the door and the book disagree, believe the door and mark the book. If you cannot fetch, write "unverified" next to the number and do not build on it.

Hands in this app can add, convert units, and search the stacks. Use them for arithmetic. Do not trust a remembered constant. Memory is how a slightly wrong famous number stays famous.

Feynman, in that same 1974 Caltech talk, told how Robert Millikan measured the charge on an electron and got a value that later work showed was a little off, because the viscosity of air he used was wrong. Later published charges crept up toward the better number, one small step at a time. People discarded results that sat too far from Millikan, and kept results that sat close. The form of measurement was there. The honesty about what would make the number fail was thin. I am not writing Millikan's figure or the living figure here. Those figures live on a constants door, not in a remembered sentence.

Constants live on the NIST constants door. Units live on the BIPM SI Brochure door. The measurement pack walks those doors. This chapter is the habit: open the host that owns the fact.

**Check.** A page in your hand names an old SI brochure edition. Open the BIPM door. Mark the page. Keep the door.

New case: a page says to use an old CODATA set, or a blog, for a constant. Open the NIST constants door. If that host is dark, mark the constant unverified. Leave it.

A third case with no constant at all: 20% of 50. That is arithmetic, not a constant. 20 in each 100 is one fifth. One fifth of 50 is 10. Hands may check 0.20 × 50 after you have written 10. If you fetched NIST to learn 20% of 50, you walked into the wrong house.

## 6. Two kinds of not-knowing

You can fail to know a word. You can fail to know a move.

A missing word is a dictionary problem. A missing move is a practice problem. "I do not know what *hypotenuse* means" is the first. "I cannot see why this side must be the longest" is the second.

For a missing word, find the object, as in chapter 4. For a missing move, do three small cases on paper. The third case is where the move usually appears. The first case is often a copy of the book. The second is a cousin. The third is the one you could not have traced through the ink.

The two failures feel similar from the inside: a stall, a heat in the face, a wish that the next sentence would rescue you. They do not share a fix. Looking up a move in a dictionary gives you a sentence. Doing a word as if it were a drill gives you busy paper and no handle.

Feynman's cargo-cult runway is a missing move wearing the clothes of a word. The islanders had the names: runway, controller, headphones. The planes still did not land. Having the vocabulary of a chapter is not the same as being able to do the chapter's act on a new case.

**Check.** You stall on percent. Sort the stall. If the stall is "I do not know what percent means," that is a missing word: per hundred, 17 in each 100, written 17%. Open the numeracy pack, or write three percents as fractions with 100 in the cut. If the stall is "I cannot see why 20% of 50 is 10," that is a missing move. Do three cases on paper: 10% of 80, 25% of 40, 20% of 50.

10% of 80: 10 in each 100 is one tenth. One tenth of 80 is 8.

25% of 40: 25 in each 100 is one fourth. One fourth of 40 is 10.

A third: open chapter 4 of this book, pick one load-bearing word, write it, write one example, close the book, use the word in a new sentence. If you need this page to make the sentence, the word is still borrowed.

New case: a missing move in another pack. Three small cases on paper, then a fourth the book did not print. The fourth is the reading. The percent pack is numeracy. Come back here for the habit, not the arithmetic.

## 7. Teach it back

Close the book. Say the object and the rule to a wall, a person, or a blank page. Use no names you cannot point at.

If you need the book's sentence to say yours, you are still borrowing. Open the book, find the hole, and try again from there. This is the check. It is not a show.

Pointing is the test of a name. "Denominator" without a finger on the 8 in $\frac{3}{8}$ is a noise. "SI Brochure" without a door you can open is a noise. "Unverified" with a finger on the number you refused to build on is a working sentence.

Feynman, again from the 1974 Caltech door, put the first principle in one line:

> The first principle is that you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool.

Teaching it back is how you catch the easy fooling. A fluent recitation can still be a runway. The blank page does not grade your tone. It asks whether the object is there when the book's sentence is gone.

Lean over backwards, which is the rest of that talk's honesty: say what would make your telling fail. "I can say the rule, and I stall on the worked case" is a better teaching-back than a smooth paragraph that hides the stall.

**Check.** Close this book. Teach chapter 3 to a blank page: what the hand must do, and what counts as done. If the page only repeats this book's sentences, open chapter 3, find the hole, and tell it again in words you can point at.

New case: teach chapter 5 back. Your telling must name NIST for constants (CODATA 2022, SRD 121, last data update May 2024) and BIPM for the SI Brochure (9th edition, 2019, complete text updated 2026). If you said "look it up somewhere official" and cannot name those two houses, you taught a mood. Open the doors, then tell it again.

## 8. Tries are the work

The first time through a hard page, you will miss. That miss is the map of what to do next.

Studio in this app marks pass or fail. It does not mark you. Extra tries are how the object settles. A clean first pass often means you already knew the object. The lesson lives in the pass after a fail.

No due date is a feature. A stuck day is a feature. Return. The page does not expire.

A try that changes nothing is the runway again: same highlighting, same hope, same fog tomorrow. A try that changes something is small and visible. You wrote a question you did not have. You did the new number. You marked unverified. You looked up the word and drew the part. The next fail should be a different fail, or you are repeating the form.

Feynman's talk asked for a kind of honesty that reports what would make the claim fail, not only what would make it look good. A study try has the same shape. After a fail, write one line: which object was missing, which question was missing, which step on paper was missing, which word, which door. That line is the work. The second try starts there, not at the top of the chapter as if the first try never happened.

**Check.** Pick the check at the end of chapter 3. Do it once with your eyes only. Write your answer. Then do it on paper with 27 + 18. If the eyes-only answer was 45 and the paper is 45, you already had the move. If the eyes-only answer was 45 and the paper is 35, or a stall, the first 45 was a memory of someone else's 13. The paper is the try that counts.

New case: fail on purpose in a way you can name. Skip the question at the top of a page, read, then try to teach it back. Note the hole. Put a question on the paper. Read again. Teach it back again. The two tellings should not be interchangeable. If they are, the question did not change what you kept.

## 9. A map of a subject

When you enter a new pack, read the first chapter until you can name the kind of question that pack keeps asking. You do not need the whole syllabus. You need the question. The rest of the book is cases of it.

A field is a pile of objects that share a kind of question. Physics asks what will happen to a measurable thing if we wait, or push, or wait after pushing. History asks how we know a past event from what remains. Civics asks who may do what to whom under a written rule. Numeracy asks how many, in what place, and how a share relates to a whole. This pack asks how a page becomes an object you can still use tomorrow.

Two maps sit on official doors, and they do different jobs. OpenStax subjects is a catalog of free textbooks grouped by the kind of course a person might take. The Library of Congress classification outlines are a cataloger's map: a code so that two works on the same kind of question can sit near each other on a shelf. This library's own cards (Dewey 001 on this pack) are a third, smaller map for the packs in EasyLM. Do not mix the codes as if they were one language. If you need the Library of Congress outline, open that door. I did not fetch the outline in this sitting, so I am not quoting a class number from it.

Project Gutenberg is a door to books whose copyright term has ended, so the text itself is the object you can keep. MIT OpenCourseWare is a door to course materials a university has put in public view. UNESCO's literacy door is a door to how reading as a public fact is talked about by that body. None of those doors replace the question you write at the top of your paper. They tell you where a kind of page lives.

**Check.** Open the OpenStax subjects door. Pick one book title you can actually see. Write, in one sentence, the kind of question that book answers. Pick a second title in a different subject. Write a second sentence. The two sentences must not be interchangeable. If you cannot open the door, write unverified, then do the same check with two packs in this library: numeracy and this book. Numeracy's question is how many, place, share, and unit. This book's question is how a page is kept.

New case: a friend hands you a chapter and says "this is science." Ask which question it is answering. If the chapter tells you who may search a house under a written rule, it is sitting in civics, whatever the cover says. The map is the question, not the label on the spine.

## 10. When the next book is for someone else

A fluent paragraph can still be wrong. Keep the object, the step on paper, and the door for the number.

Studio marks pass or fail on the work. The record stays on this device. A late night and a slow page are legal.

When a chapter in another pack feels written for a different reader, open the prior pack named in the front of that book, or open chapter 0 if the pack has one. Then come back. This book starts at read. A later book may start at count. That is a map, not a verdict on you.

A list of chapter titles with no object sentence, no question, no new number, and no door is a stub of notes. Finish the object or mark the hole. Do not sign off a visit you did not take.

The next book is for someone else when its first chapter assumes an object this book has not yet put in your head, or assumes a move your paper cannot yet do. You find that out by trying the first check, not by judging the title. If the check needs a letter for an unknown, the algebra pack is waiting, and it names numeracy as a prior book. If the check needs a file and a folder, the computers pack is waiting, and it starts at read, as this one does.

Other people will write pages for other readers on purpose: a professional code, a graduate note, a statute. Your job is the same job this book taught. Name the object. Bring a question. Do a new case on paper. Look up the load-bearing word. Walk the number to a door or write unverified. Tell it back. Try again. See which map you are on. If the page was not for you yet, take the prior book, then return.

**Check.** Open the front of the algebra pack, or of any later pack that names a prior book. Read the first check you can find. If you cannot do it, do not push. Open the named prior pack, find that pack's object in one sentence, do one new case there, and come back. The later check should now name its pieces.

New case: a paragraph in another pack states a constant, a unit, or an edition. Do not finish the sentence from this chapter's memory of CODATA 2022 or of the 9th SI Brochure. Open the door that owns that fact. If the door agrees, keep the number. If the door disagrees, believe the door and mark the paragraph. If the door will not open, write unverified and stop building on it. That last move is the whole of keeping a page when the next book was written for someone else, including a past self who remembered a number too fast.
