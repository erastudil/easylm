# A second tongue

You can already keep a page. This book is the next skill: getting another tongue into your ear, your mouth, and tomorrow.

The object of this book is a second tongue you can use. Not a feeling that you studied. Not a stack of lists you never said aloud. One new sound, then one word that does a job, then a sentence a stranger could answer.

EasyLM is public general education. Anyone who can read, and who has the study pack, can walk how a second tongue is learned.

A living pack in this library treats how English-like systems are built. That book owns the insides of one tongue. This book owns the walk of getting a second. Name that door and stop.

Portuguese is the worked second tongue here, with Japanese as a contrast when time or script changes the job. You may pick another tongue. The walk stays the same. Foreign Service Institute week counts later in the book are for native English speakers in a full-time US school. If English is not your first tongue, those counts are a picture of distance, not your calendar.

---

## 1. A sound you can make

Stand in a bakery on Elm. The person behind the counter says a short word for bread. Your mouth does not have that sound yet. You hear a vowel with a hum in the nose, and your first tongue wants to turn it into "pow" or "pong."

That gap is the work of this chapter. A second tongue is not first a grammar. It is a sound you can make, and a sound you can tell apart from a neighbor sound.

Say the English word "pan." Now try to say the Portuguese word for bread, *pão*. The letters look near. The sound is not. Air comes through the mouth and the nose at once, and the vowel moves. If you said "pow," you made an English sound and wrote a Portuguese word on top of it.

Hold *pão* next to Portuguese *pau*, a word for a stick. Same start. Different vowel. A listener in that tongue hears two words. If your mouth makes one sound for both, you have a spelling pair and not a sound pair.

People name a smallest sound that can change a word a **phoneme**. The name arrives after you already heard the bakery. *Pão* and *pau* are two phonemes at work. You do not need the name to practice. You need your mouth and a pair.

A pair you can use today if English is new to you: "ship" and "sheep." One vowel is shorter and sits lower. One is longer and sits higher. If your first tongue does not split those two, a listener in English still hears two words. Stand the two sounds next to each other. Record five of each. Play them. If you cannot hear a difference, you do not have the pair yet. Change the mouth, not the letters. Do not add a third pair until these two split.

The Foreign Service Institute trains US government employees in job languages. I fetched the State Department's foreign-language-training page for this book. That page says the Directorate of Language Studies provides training in 60 languages, and that a typical week is 23 hours in class and 17 hours of self-study. Those hours are not a promise about your kitchen. This chapter asks for minutes, not a 23-hour week.

You will want to skip the sound and jump to a list of words. The list will sit on paper and die in the mouth. The bakery worker will not hear your paper.

**Check.** Pick one new pair that your first tongue does not split. Write both marks. Say each five times into a phone. Play the ten. Mark the two takes a stranger could tell apart. If all ten sound like one word, you copied a spelling. Go back to the first take and change the mouth, not the letters.

New case: the English sound at the start of "this," the tongue between the teeth. If that sound is already in your first tongue, pick the vowel in "book" versus "buck" instead. The check is the same: two sounds, ten takes, a playback that splits.

## 2. A word you can use tomorrow

A sound that never enters a word is a gym trick. Tomorrow you need bread, or water, or a way to say you do not understand.

Pick one job for tomorrow. "I need bread." In Portuguese that job can be *pão*. One word. Not a page of bakery nouns. The word has to leave your mouth at a counter, or into a recording you will play before you sleep, or to a person who will answer.

People name a word you can actually send a **usable word**. The test is tomorrow, not a quiz tonight.

Write the word. Say it. Park it in a tiny frame you have already heard, not in a frame you invented. "I need ___" is an English frame. If the new tongue puts the pieces in another order, steal one frame from a sentence a speaker already said. Inventing a frame is how you teach yourself a private dialect.

Twelve words on a card feel like progress. Twelve words you cannot say at a counter are a stack. Three words you will actually send — bread, water, thank-you — beat twelve that stay in a pocket.

Thank-you in Portuguese is *obrigado* if you are speaking as a man in the usual pattern, and *obrigada* if you are speaking as a woman in the usual pattern. The last vowel is doing work. If you swap it, a listener may still hand you the bread. They also heard which pattern you used. That is a usable word with a small hook. Learn the hook with the word, not later.

Please, in a bakery row, can be *por favor*. Water is *água*. Four sendable pieces if Portuguese is the new tongue: *pão*, *água*, *por favor*, and the thank-you that matches you. Four is a day's work. Forty is a week's fantasy unless you will send them.

Do not count the world's tongues in this chapter. SIL International keeps a door on language survey and documentation. I did not fetch a living total from that door this sitting. A sentence that says how many tongues exist is unverified here. You need one new tongue, not a census.

**Check.** Write three usable words for a shop you can actually enter this week, or for a recording you will make if the shop is far. Word one is the thing you will ask for. Word two is please or thank-you. Word three is a stop: "I do not understand" or the local cousin you have heard. Say each to a wall tonight. Tomorrow, send one. If you only reread the card, you did not use a word.

New case: water. Portuguese *água*. Write it. Say it. Put it next to bread as a second sendable noun. If your mouth still says the English sentence "I need água" with one foreign noun stuck in, you have a mixed row. Chapter 3 will ask for a whole sentence in the new tongue. This chapter only needs the noun to be sendable.

## 3. A sentence in the new tongue

A word buys you a thing if the counter already knows the rest. A sentence lets you move the thing: where it is, whether you want it, whether you can pay.

English: "The bread is here." Someone in the first chair: the bread. An act: is. A place: here.

Portuguese: *O pão está aqui.* The someone is still the bread. The act is *está*. The place is *aqui*. The small word *O* in front of *pão* is a marker English often skips in the same spot. If you drop it, you said a different row.

The row that names a someone and an act, and finishes, is a **sentence**. You already met that chair-picture in the writing pack. The new tongue may put the chairs in another order. Some tongues put the act at the end. Some mark the someone on the act and you hear no separate word for "I." Copy a sentence you have heard. Do not rebuild it from English chairs and hope.

A failing copy: take "The bread is here" and swap each English word for a Portuguese word in the same slots, including slots Portuguese does not use that way. You will invent a private mix. *É* and *está* are different acts in Portuguese. I am not teaching the full split in this book. The lesson is smaller: the sentence you send has to be a sentence someone already said, until you have enough of them to feel the chairs.

Write three sentences you will need this week, all in the new tongue:

1. A request for the thing.
2. A question about where it is.
3. A thank-you that can stand alone.

For a Portuguese bakery: *Quero um pão.* *Onde está o pão?* *Obrigado.* Three rows. Each one a sentence, or a sentence-word that a counter will answer. If you wrote a twelve-word essay about bread, you wrote English and dressed it.

*Quero um pão* is four pieces: I-want, a, bread. English would have put "I" in its own chair. Portuguese parked "I" on the act. That is not a missing word. Copy the map. Do not insert an English "I" in front and call it complete.

The Council of Europe keeps the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages. I fetched that door. The page presents a companion volume that replaces the 2001 set of descriptors, and it says the 2001 framework was designed to provide a transparent, coherent and comprehensive basis for syllabuses, teaching materials, and assessment of foreign language proficiency. I did not fetch the inner ladder of letter-and-number labels this sitting. I am not printing that ladder from memory. A sentence you can send at a bakery is the object. The ladder lives on the door.

**Check.** Write one four-word sentence in the new tongue that you have actually heard or looked up. Count the words. Then write an English sentence of the same length that means the same job. Do not copy word-for-word across. If the new-tongue row is just the English row with nouns swapped, tear it up and find a sentence a speaker of that tongue would say.

New case: "The water is here." Portuguese *A água está aqui.* The marker in front changed from *O* to *A*. That change is part of the sentence, not decoration. If you wrote *O água está aqui,* you kept the bread's marker and missed the water's. Fix the marker. Say the new row aloud.

## 4. Listen first

Your mouth wants to work. The ear has to go first, or the mouth will print your first tongue onto the new one.

Sit with a short stretch of the new tongue: a weather sentence, a shop greeting, a child's two-line skit. Do not pause to speak. Do not look at a translation yet. Listen once for the music: where the voice rises, where it stops. Listen again for one word you already have. Listen a third time for a word you do not have.

People name this **listening**. The name is a handle for "the ear got the message." The message can be only "this is a greeting" or "someone asked a question." That is already a catch.

Five minutes of this is work. Fifty minutes of a show you treat as furniture is not. If you can cook and "have it on," the ear is not in the chair.

A trap: you hear a sentence, you translate every word into English in your head, then you feel you understood. You understood English. Next time, point at the thing. If the speaker said the bread is here, point at the bread. If you cannot point, you do not have it yet.

The State Department page I fetched groups languages by how long a full-time English-speaking student is usually expected to take to reach an integrated score of 3, speaking and listening, on the Interagency Language Roundtable scale. Listening is in the score. A school that trains diplomats still puts the ear in the grade. Your kitchen can do the same at a smaller size: no point for a word you cannot catch from a mouth.

Portuguese, on that page, is a Category I language, 24 weeks. Spanish is Category I, 30 weeks. Italian is 24. French is 30. Those weeks are for a 23-hour class week plus 17 hours of self-study, aimed at that ILR 3. They are not a kitchen calendar. They tell you that even a tongue close to English still spends a pile of hours on the ear.

Category I on that page is named as languages close to English. Close is about the ear as much as the page. A Portuguese sentence can still be noise if you never sat still for it.

**Check.** Play one new minute of the tongue. No transcript. Write three notes: the music (question or not), one word you caught, one stretch you did not. Play it again. The third note should shrink or split. If all three notes are English translations of whole sentences, you skipped the ear and used a subtitle in your head. Turn the subtitle off and point.

New case: the same minute, now with a paper. Draw a line when the voice stops. Count the stops. That count is a sentence-guess. If you counted 4 stops, write 4. Play it once more and see whether a stop was a breath inside one sentence. Change the 4 if the breath fooled you.

## 5. Repeat

The ear caught a row. Now the mouth has to copy it, many times, before the row is yours.

Say the bakery sentence. *O pão está aqui.* Again. Again. Change nothing the first eight times. You are not composing. You are wearing a groove.

People name this **repeat**. Schools also say **drill** when the copies are the same row on purpose, and **shadow** when you speak on top of a recording a fraction behind it. Both are repeat. Repeat is the work. A new sentence every minute is a tour.

After eight clean copies, walk to another room. Say it once more. If it broke, the eight were only echo. The ninth, after the walk, is the one that counts.

The State Department page gives class hours next to weeks. Category I: 24 to 30 weeks, 552 to 690 class hours. Category II: about 36 weeks, 828 class hours, with German, Haitian Creole, Indonesian, Malay, and Swahili named there. Category III: about 44 weeks, 1,012 class hours, named there as hard languages with significant linguistic or cultural differences from English, and the list marked as not exhaustive. Category IV: 88 weeks, 2,200 class hours. Category IV on that page is Arabic, Cantonese, Mandarin, Japanese, and Korean, called there super-hard for native English speakers. Portuguese sits in Category I at 24 weeks.

Those hours are repeats in a building, with instructors who are native or near-native speakers. You do not have that building. You have a mouth and a recording. The move that transfers is the repeat, not the badge of 552.

Do not turn the Category II figure of 36 weeks into a homemade sum with some other number. The 36 weeks are a full-time student figure on a named scale. Your week is not that week.

A typical FSI week on that page is 23 class hours and 17 self-study hours. The 17 is the part a kitchen can imitate: the same sentences, again, without a new list.

Shadow a recording for one sentence, not for a whole show. Stay a fraction behind the mouth you hear. If you overtake it, you stopped listening. Eight copies of one row beat one copy of eight rows. The next day's first take is the test. If it is broken, the groove was echo. Start the eight again.

**Check.** Take the four-word sentence from chapter 3. Say it 8 times without looking. Walk. Say it a 9th time. Write whether the 9th matched the 8. If the 9th lost a marker, the marker was not in the groove. Put the marker back and do 8 more.

New case: a new number. 5 repeats of *A água está aqui,* then a 6th after you wash a cup. The 6th must keep *A* and *água* together. If the 6th says *O água,* chapter 3's marker is still on paper.

## 6. A script

A tongue can live in the air. Many also live as marks. The marks are not the sounds. They are a way to park the sounds so tomorrow's mouth can find them.

Portuguese uses a Latin script with extra marks: the tilde on *ã* in *pão*, the acute on *á* in *água*. If you write *pao* and *agua*, you wrote a cousin. A reader may still guess. A dictionary may not find your cousin.

Japanese, on the State Department page, is a Category IV tongue, 88 weeks, 2,200 class hours for that ILR 3. Part of the weight is a script system that is not one Latin line. I am not teaching those marks here. The lesson is: a script is part of the tongue when the tongue uses one. You cannot treat Japanese as if it were Portuguese with different nouns.

People name a set of marks a **script**. A **letter** is one mark in an alphabet. Some scripts write a syllable as one mark. Some write a whole word-idea as one mark. Copy the kind the tongue actually uses. Do not force every tongue into the alphabet you already have.

Write *pão* on paper. The tilde has to be there. Then type it. If your keyboard will not make *ã*, learn the key, or copy the mark from a page that already has it. A handwritten *ã* that looks like *a* will fool you later.

How a machine stores those marks is a different job. The Unicode Standard is the universal character encoding designed to support the worldwide interchange, processing, and display of the written texts of the diverse languages and technical disciplines of the modern world, and it also supports classical and historical texts of many written languages. I fetched the Unicode Consortium's standard page for that sentence. The latest version always lives at the versions/latest door in `LINK_INDEX.md`. Unicode owns encodings. This pack names that door and stops.

The Library of Congress, on the front door I fetched, calls itself the largest library in the world and invites a person to use it online or in person. I did not count the volumes. I did not fetch a language-collection catalog this sitting. The door is a place to find a page already in the script you are learning. A page in the script beats a page about the script.

Read *pão* and say it. Hear *pão* and write it. If you can only do the first, you have a reading trick. If you can only do the second, you have a spelling trick. Tomorrow's bakery may need both: a sign on the wall, and a word you hear.

**Check.** Copy five marks you did not grow up writing. They can be *ã, ç, á, õ, ê* if Portuguese is the new tongue. Write them from memory after a walk. If *ã* comes back as *a*, the tilde was decoration in your head. It is not decoration in the word *pão*.

New case: type the same five in a notes file. Save. Close. Open. If a mark became a square or a neighbor letter, the file did not keep the script. Unicode's job is to keep it. Open the Unicode door. Do not invent a code number from memory.

## 7. A mistake that teaches

You will say the wrong row. That is not a scandal. That is a light on a chair.

Say *O água está aqui* to a wall. A Portuguese ear wants *A água*. The marker was wrong. Write the wrong row. Write the right row. Say the right row eight times. The mistake named the chair you had not yet built.

People name this a **useful mistake**. A useful mistake is specific. "I am bad at Portuguese" is a fog. "I put *O* in front of *água*" is a chair.

A second useful mistake: word order. You want to ask where the bread is. English: "Where is the bread?" Portuguese: *Onde está o pão?* If you said *Onde o pão está?* you dragged English chairs into a Portuguese row. Write both. Circle the act. In the Portuguese question the act sits before the bread. That circle is the lesson.

A useless mistake is one you never see. You mumble, no recording, no listener, and you feel fine. Record. Or say it to a person. Or say it to a wall and then compare to a sentence you did not invent.

Do not collect mistakes as shame. Collect them as a list of chairs. Three chairs in a week is a map. Thirty vague feelings of failure are not.

The CEFR companion volume I fetched names plurilingual and pluricultural competence, and it includes descriptors for sign language competences. A second tongue can be a signed tongue. A mistake in a signed tongue is a handshape or a motion, not a vowel. The useful-mistake move is the same: name the part that was wrong, then repeat the part that is right.

I did not fetch an inner CEFR table of error types. A sentence that says how many errors a level allows is unverified here.

When a person corrects you, write the correction in the new tongue, not a long English note about how you felt. The next mouth needs the right row.

**Check.** Make one wrong row on purpose from a sentence you already have. Change one chair only: the marker, or the act, or the order. Write the wrong row and the right row. Say the right row 8 times. Then write a new wrong row that changes a different chair. If both wrong rows are the same, you did not isolate a chair.

New case: thank-you. Swap the last vowel of *obrigado* / *obrigada* to the one that does not match how you mean to speak. Then swap it back. The last vowel is a chair. If you cannot hear the swap on a recording, the chair is still paper.

## 8. Two languages in one head

You already have a first tongue. The second one does not erase it. Both sit in the same head and will try to use the same mouth.

Ask for bread in Portuguese, then tell a friend in English what you did. That switch is legal. It is also work. The work is keeping the chairs of one tongue from sitting down in the other.

A mixed row: "I need o pão here." English chairs, one Portuguese noun. Fine as a joke. Bad as practice. Practice wants one tongue per row, until you can switch on purpose.

People name the switch **code-switching** when a speaker moves between tongues in one stretch of talk. People name the drag of one tongue on another **interference**. The picture is the mixed bakery row. The names are handles. This pack does not own a linguistics treatise. The living language pack in this library does that job for English-like systems. Name it and stop.

Two tongues can help each other. If you already have Spanish, Portuguese words will look near. Near is a gift and a trap. *Pão* is not Spanish *pan*. The nasal vowel from chapter 1 is the trap. Use the nearness to guess, then check the guess with a mouth or a dictionary. An unchecked guess teaches Spanish with a Portuguese hat.

If your first tongue is already Portuguese, the FSI table is not written for you. That table measures English speakers walking out. The walk in this book still holds.

The CEFR companion volume, on the door I fetched, adds descriptors for mediation, online interaction, and plurilingual and pluricultural competence, and it says the descriptors are now gender-neutral. Plurilingual here is the official name for living with more than one tongue, not as a defect. You fail only when English writes the Portuguese row for you and you do not notice. A signed tongue and a spoken tongue can sit in the same head too. The companion volume names sign language competences. I did not fetch those descriptors. The move you can use: one row, one tongue, then a clean switch.

**Check.** Write the bakery request in the new tongue. Write the same job in your first tongue. Read them in the order new, first, new. The second new must match the first new. If the second new picked up a first-tongue chair, circle it and put the new-tongue chair back.

New case: a number. Ask for 2 of the thing in the new tongue, then for 4, then for 2 again. Portuguese: *dois pães*, then *quatro pães*, then *dois pães* again. The 2 must come back as the same word you started with. If 4 poisoned 2, the number words are still English in your head. Look up the 2. Repeat it 8 times. Then try 4 on a new card, not on the 2 card.

## 9. A dictionary door

A guess is not a definition. Open a door that owns the word.

For English, Merriam-Webster is the dictionary door in `LINK_INDEX.md`. Open it when the English side of a gloss is a word you cannot point at. I did not fetch a living entry count this sitting. Do not invent how many words sit there.

For the new tongue, you need a dictionary that actually lists that tongue. A bilingual page that puts Portuguese on one side and English on the other is a **bilingual dictionary**. A page that stays inside Portuguese is a **monolingual dictionary**. Early on, the bilingual page is a bridge. Later, the monolingual page stops English from writing the row.

Look up *pão*. Copy one sense you can point at: bread. Copy a sentence if the door gives one. Do not copy five senses and pretend you own them. One sense, one sentence, one repeat.

A dictionary can lie to you if you pick the wrong sense. English "roll" can be bread or a motion. The bakery wants the bread. Read the example. If there is no example, park the word and find a sentence in the wild.

Write the word, the sense, and the door. If you cannot name the door, the sense is unverified. Pretending you looked it up is not.

The Library of Congress door is a place to find a whole book in the tongue, or a grammar you did not invent. I fetched the front door, not a language catalog. If you cannot open a catalog, write unverified next to any claim about how many language collections sit there.

SIL International's door is a place for language documentation. I did not fetch a language total. Unverified here. Unicode owns how the marks are encoded. If a dictionary page shows squares instead of *ã*, the encoding failed in the display. That is not a fact about bread. Name the Unicode door and stop. Copy the headword as it is printed, including the tilde. If you copy *pão* as *pao*, tomorrow's search will miss.

**Check.** Look up one new word you do not have, in a dictionary you can name. Write the tongue, the word, one sense you can point at, and the door. Close the door. Write the sense from memory. Open the door. If the sense changed, you invented. Copy again.

New case: look up the English word you used as the sense. If you wrote "bread" for *pão*, open Merriam-Webster on *bread*. If that entry's first sense is not a food, you picked a gloss you cannot point at. Change the English or keep looking until the two doors agree on a thing you could hold.

## 10. A check: say it to a wall

Stand facing a wall. No phone in your hand. Say three sentences in the new tongue:

1. The request for the thing.
2. The where-question.
3. The thank-you.

Then say a fourth you have not practiced in this book. The fourth is the test of whether the chairs can hold a new row, or only the three souvenirs.

Where you stumble, you do not have it. Where you switch to English, the first tongue grabbed the mouth. Where you hear yourself add a word that is not in the tongue, you are translating in the air.

People name a grade of this **proficiency**. Schools use scales. The State Department page I fetched aims at an integrated ILR score of 3 in speaking and listening. That 3 is a professional bar for a diplomat's job, after hundreds of class hours. It is not this week's wall. This week's wall is three sentences and a fourth.

The Council of Europe door holds the living CEFR descriptors, including the companion volume that replaced the 2001 set. Open it when a school names a CEFR level. I did not fetch the inner labels, so I am not printing a letter-and-number as if this wall had earned it.

Read the three sentences again, slower. If a marker drops when you go slow, the marker was never in the groove. If a marker drops when you go fast, you have it only at drill speed. Both findings are useful mistakes.

A wall does not smile. A friendly classmate may nod while you say English-with-nouns. The wall does not nod. You have to hear the row.

If you cannot yet say a fourth sentence, do not fake one. Go back to chapter 2 and get one more usable word. Build one more sentence in chapter 3. Repeat it in chapter 5. Then face the wall.

Record the session once you have said the rows from memory. Play it. Compare each row to the paper from chapter 3. A diplomat's 88 weeks for Japanese, or 24 weeks for Portuguese, is a school answering a job. Your wall is answering a bakery. Do not mix the bars.

**Check.** Say the three sentences. Record the wall session. Play it. Count how many of the three match the rows you wrote in chapter 3. If 2 match and 1 lost a marker, that 1 is tonight's repeat. Do not add a fifth sentence until the 3 are clean.

New case: the fourth sentence. It must use one old word and one word that is not *pão* or *água*. If the fourth sentence is *O pão está aqui* with a new place-word you invented, you did not look the place-word up. Open the dictionary door. Then say the fourth to the wall. If you stall, that stall is the map. It is not a failure of the week. It is the next chair.
