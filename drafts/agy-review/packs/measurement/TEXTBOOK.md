# A unit, an instrument, and a shared stick

You can already add, take away, multiply, share, name a percent, and keep a unit on a number. This book is the next act: compare a thing in the world to an agreed stick, write the count with the stick's name, and check the stick against a door that owns it.

The objects are a unit, an instrument, and the living international set of units. Numeracy taught you not to add metres to seconds. Here you learn how a metre reading is made, what can go wrong in the making, and where a load-bearing number has to live.

EasyLM is public general education. License AGPL-3.0-or-later. Hands in this app can add and convert after you have tried the arithmetic on paper. They do not own a constant. BIPM and NIST do.

## 1. A comparison

Two boards sit on a bench. You want to know which is longer, and by how much. Laying them side by side, ends together, already answers which. The leftover piece of the longer board is the gap. To send that gap to someone who cannot see the bench, you need a third thing that both of you can count.

Take a tile from the floor. Lay it along the first board, end to end, without gaps and without riding up onto itself. You fit 18 tiles. Along the second board, the same tile fits 11 times. The first board is 7 tiles longer, because $18 - 11 = 7$. The number 7 is the comparison. It is how many of the third thing fit the leftover.

If you switch to a smaller tile halfway along the second board, the 11 and the 18 no longer share a stick. Subtracting them would mix two stories. Finish one board with one tile size. Then, if you must change size, convert before you subtract, the same way numeracy converted centimetres before adding them to metres.

You can compare without a number. Hold the two boards together. The leftover is still there. The number is what makes the leftover travel: onto paper, into a shop order, across a year. A comparison that never becomes a number is still a comparison. A number with no named third thing is a count of nothing.

Order of the two boards does not change the gap. 18 tiles take away 11 tiles is 7 tiles. 11 is 7 tiles shorter than 18. Same leftover, two sentences.

A third board takes 6 of the same tiles. The first is longer than the second, and the second is longer than the third, so the first is longer than the third. $18 - 6 = 12$ tiles of gap from first to third. The two-step walk $18 - 11 = 7$ and $11 - 6 = 5$, then $7 + 5 = 12$, lands on the same gap. If those two walks disagree, a tile was skipped or counted twice.

**Check.** A ribbon takes 9 hand-spans. A window takes 4 of the same spans. The ribbon is 5 spans longer. A new case: two ropes marked with the same knotted string. One rope holds 14 knots. The other holds 6. The gap is 8 knots. Put the shorter rope against the leftover of the longer: you should see those 8 knots again. A third case: three shelves, 22 tiles, 15 tiles, and 15 tiles. The first is 7 tiles longer than each of the others. The two shorter shelves match. Matching is a comparison whose gap is 0.

## 2. A unit

The third thing you laid along the boards has to stay the same when you walk to another room, another shop, another year. An agreed size of that third thing is a **unit**. The unit is the stick you and the other person both count. The number is how many of those sticks fit.

You already met this in numeracy: "3" is not a length. "3 metres" is. The word after the number names the stick. Drop the word, and the 3 could be apples, hours, or grams. Keep the word, and you may add 3 metres to 2 metres because both counts use the same stick. 3 metres plus 2 seconds is not a length and not a time. Convert before you add, or refuse the sum.

Smaller cuts of the same stick are still that stick. A metre cut into 100 equal parts gives centimetres, the same per-hundred cut you already have. 80 centimetres is 80 of those parts, which is $0.80$ metres. 4 metres and 80 centimetres is $4 + 0.80 = 4.80$ metres, not 84 of anything.

A thousand of the same stick is a bigger named pile of that stick. Kilo in front of a unit is a thousand of it. Milli is one of a thousand equal cuts. Those two prefixes are the thousand and the thousandth you can already name. The living prefix list, including names this book does not use, sits on the NIST SI prefixes door.

Write the count, then the unit. 4.80 metres. People who write SI every day also use short marks: m for metre, kg for kilogram, s for second. How to space those marks, and which marks are legal, lives in NIST Special Publication 811. This book often writes the names in words so the stick stays visible. Fetch 811 when a short mark must be right.

**Check.** 4 metres and 80 centimetres. 80 centimetres is $0.80$ metres. The length is $4.80$ metres. A new case: 8 glasses, each 250 millilitres of the same glass. $8 \times 250 = 2000$ millilitres. One more of those glasses makes 2250 millilitres. A millilitre is one of a thousand equal cuts of a litre, so 2250 millilitres is $2.250$ litres. Another: 3 metres plus 40 seconds. Stop. Convert nothing. The two counts do not share a stick.

## 3. SI

Towns used to keep their own sticks. A cloth merchant in one market and a cloth merchant in another could both say "three yards" and mean different leftover boards. Trade, science, and a shop scale that must match a shop scale in another country need one living set.

That living set is the **SI**, the International System of Units. NIST's Metric Program page, fetched for this book, states that the SI is the preferred system of weights and measures for U.S. trade and commerce, and that the SI has seven defining constants and seven base units. The same page says all countries have recognized and adopted the SI, including the United States, and that U.S. customary units are based on the SI.

BIPM publishes the set as the SI Brochure. The brochure page fetched for this book names the 9th edition (2019), complete text updated in 2026, DOI 10.59161/AUEZ1291. The citation BIPM prints is Bureau International des Poids et Mesures (2026), *Le Système international d'unités / The International System of Units*, 9th edition. The PDF was not opened in this sitting. The seven unit names, the seven defining numbers, and the sentences that fix those units are unverified here. The brochure is the door. This paragraph is the pointer.

NIST Special Publication 330 is the U.S. edition of that brochure. SP 811 is the writing guide. Both are doors, not a second memory.

The United States still uses miles, pounds, and gallons in daily talk. Under those names the SI stick is still there. On 1 January 2023, NIST and NOAA retired the U.S. survey foot. The international foot, based on the metre, superseded it. Fetch the NIST survey-foot door if a map or a deed still says "survey foot." Do not average two feet.

This book walks four of the quantities the SI names: length, mass, time, and temperature. The NIST SI units door lists all seven base units and the seven defining constants. Open that door when a later sentence needs a fifth quantity.

**Check.** A page in your hand says, "The living SI Brochure is the 8th edition (2006)." Open the BIPM brochure door. The living brochure is the 9th edition (2019), complete text updated in 2026. Mark the page. A new case: a land description written in survey feet. Open the NIST survey-foot door. That foot was deemed obsolete on 1 January 2023. Convert using the factor on that door. Do not mix the two feet in one sum. A third case: "the United States has not adopted the SI." The Metric Program page says the SI is the preferred system for U.S. trade and commerce, and that customary units are based on the SI. Daily talk and the legal stick are not the same sentence.

## 4. Length

You want to know how far it is from one end of a table to the other. That quantity is **length**. The SI unit of length is the metre. NIST writes *meter*. BIPM writes *metre*. Same stick, two spellings. This book uses metre in running prose and treats meter as the NIST spelling of the same unit.

A kilometre is a thousand metres. A millimetre is one of a thousand equal cuts of a metre. A centimetre is one of a hundred equal cuts. 1 metre 80 centimetres is 180 centimetres, which is $1.80$ metres, which is 1800 millimetres. The pile did not change. The grouping did.

How the metre is fixed in the SI (a defining constant, a sentence in the brochure) lives on the BIPM brochure door and on BIPM's metre page. Unverified here. Do not finish that sentence from memory. The count 1.80 metres you made with a tape is a reading. The definition is what makes that reading comparable to a reading in another country.

A hallway 12 metres long takes three equal rugs. $12 \div 3 = 4$, so each rug is 4 metres. Two boards, 2.4 metres and 1.7 metres, laid end to end: $2.4 + 1.7 = 4.1$ metres. A path 9 metres with a gap of 150 centimetres: 150 centimetres is $1.50$ metres, and $9 - 1.50 = 7.50$ metres of path remain.

Inches, feet, and miles are customary names. NIST keeps the U.S. legal conversions on the SI units page for length. Fetch that door when a number must travel from inches to millimetres. This book does not finish those exact factors from memory.

**Check.** 7 metres and 250 millimetres. 250 millimetres is $0.250$ metres. The length is $7.250$ metres. A new case: 3 kilometres and 400 metres. 3 kilometres is 3000 metres. The length is 3400 metres, which is $3.400$ kilometres. Another: a shelf 2 metres 6 centimetres. 6 centimetres is $0.06$ metres. The length is $2.06$ metres. Undo: $2.06$ metres is 206 centimetres. If you wrote 26 centimetres, a place was lost.

## 5. Mass

You want to know how much flour is in the bowl. That quantity is **mass**: how much stuff, not how hard the bowl presses on your hand, and not how heavy it would feel on another planet. A kitchen scale that reads in grams is the daily instrument. NIST keeps a metric kitchen as a door for cooking with that scale. Read a recipe from that door. Do not invent the gram count.

The SI unit of mass is the kilogram. A kilogram is a thousand grams. 2 kilograms and 750 grams is $2 + 0.750 = 2.750$ kilograms, because 750 grams is 750 of the thousand cuts of a kilogram. Three bags of 400 grams: $3 \times 400 = 1200$ grams, which is $1.200$ kilograms.

How the kilogram is fixed in the SI lives in the brochure. Unverified here. The old story of a metal cylinder in a vault is a history sentence, not the living definition. Fetch BIPM if you need the living sentence.

Pounds and ounces are customary names. NIST keeps the U.S. legal conversions on the SI units page for mass. Fetch that door when a number must travel from pounds to kilograms. This book does not finish those exact factors from memory.

A letter of 80 grams and a book of 920 grams: $80 + 920 = 1000$ grams, which is 1 kilogram. Five kilograms take away 1 kilogram 200 grams: 1 kilogram 200 grams is $1.200$ kilograms, and $5 - 1.200 = 3.800$ kilograms remain.

Mass is not the same quantity as volume. A litre of water and a litre of oil do not have to match on the scale. The bottle names a volume. The scale names a mass. Convert one into the other only with a measured density, and that density is a door of its own, not a memory.

**Check.** 250 grams, eight times. $250 \times 8 = 2000$ grams, which is 2 kilograms. A new case: 5 kilograms take away 1 kilogram 200 grams. Remain $3.800$ kilograms. Put $1.200$ back on $3.800$ and you should see 5. Another: 80 grams plus 920 grams is 1000 grams, 1 kilogram. If you wrote 100 grams, you added the 80 to the 20 and dropped a place.

## 6. Time

You want to know how long to wait. That quantity is **time**. The SI unit of time is the second.

Ordinary clocks group 60 seconds into a minute, and 60 minutes into an hour. Those groupings are the clock face you already use. 3 minutes 20 seconds is $3 \times 60 + 20 = 180 + 20 = 200$ seconds. 90 seconds is 1 minute 30 seconds, because 60 seconds fill a minute and 30 remain.

How the second is fixed in the SI lives in the brochure. Unverified here. Do not finish the cesium sentence from memory. Official civil time in the United States is a service, not a kitchen clock. NIST's time door, and the public clock at time.gov, are where a time of day that must match another building should be checked. Leap seconds and time scales are on those doors. Unverified here.

2 hours 15 minutes is $2 \times 60 + 15 = 135$ minutes. In seconds: $135 \times 60 = 8100$ seconds. A class of 50 minutes, a 10 minute break, and another class of 50 minutes: $50 + 10 + 50 = 110$ minutes, which is 1 hour 50 minutes.

4 minutes 5 seconds plus 2 minutes 55 seconds: the seconds are $5 + 55 = 60$, which is 1 extra minute. The minutes are $4 + 2 + 1 = 7$. The sum is 7 minutes, or 420 seconds. Undo: 7 minutes take away 2 minutes 55 seconds should return 4 minutes 5 seconds.

NIST's constants page, fetched for this book, keeps the CODATA 2022 values as Standard Reference Database 121. Last update to that data content on the fetched page: May 2024. The next regularly scheduled CODATA adjustment is 2026. Data used in that adjustment must have been discussed in a publication or a preprint before 31 December 2026. A frequency that defines a time unit, if you need its digits, is a constants-door number or a brochure number, not a remembered string.

**Check.** 3 minutes 20 seconds is 200 seconds. A new case: 4 minutes 5 seconds plus 2 minutes 55 seconds is 7 minutes. Undo by taking 2 minutes 55 seconds off 7 minutes: 4 minutes 5 seconds. Another: 2 hours 15 minutes is 135 minutes. $135 \div 60 = 2$ remainder 15, which is 2 hours 15 minutes again. If you wrote 215 minutes, you treated the 2 hours as 200 minutes and the colon as a decoration.

## 7. Temperature

You want to know how hot the room is. That quantity is **temperature**. A number on a thermometer is a mark on a chosen scale. Two common writings in daily U.S. talk are Celsius marks and Fahrenheit marks. The SI base unit for thermodynamic temperature is the kelvin. How the kelvin is fixed, and the exact link between a Celsius reading and a kelvin reading, live on the BIPM brochure door and on NIST's temperature door. Unverified here. Do not finish 273 from memory.

You can still compare two readings on the same scale without that link. A room at 21 and a room at 18, both on the same scale, differ by 3 degrees of that scale: $21 - 18 = 3$. A rise of 5 degrees from 16 lands on 21. A fall of 7 degrees from 30 lands on 23. Same stick, ordinary taking away.

Do not add a Celsius reading to a Fahrenheit reading. 20 on one scale plus 68 on the other is not 88 of anything. Convert first, using the factor on the NIST temperature door, or refuse the sum. The two numbers can describe the same room. They still do not share a stick until you convert.

A freezer dial at $-18$ on a Celsius kitchen scale and a room at 22 on that same scale: the gap is $22 - (-18) = 40$ degrees of that scale. Negative is a legal reading. It means below the zero that scale chose, not "no temperature."

Water's freeze and boil marks on a given scale are load-bearing if you use them as fixed points. Fetch NIST or BIPM for the living statements. Unverified here as exact official figures. A kitchen thermometer may print those marks. Printing is not the brochure.

**Check.** 12 degrees, then a rise of 9 on the same scale, lands on 21. A new case: 30 take away 7 is 23 on that scale. Another: a freezer at $-18$ and a room at 22, both Celsius on the same dial, differ by 40 degrees of that scale. A fight: 20 °C plus 68 °F. Stop. Two sticks. Open the temperature door if you must name one room in both writings. If the door will not open, write unverified and keep the two readings separate.

## 8. An instrument

A tape, a scale, a clock, and a thermometer do the comparison for you. Each is an **instrument**: a tool with marks, a zero, and a job. The instrument is not the quantity. It is how you read the quantity today.

Every instrument has a smallest mark. A tape marked every millimetre can honestly claim millimetres. A reading of 1.423 metres is 1423 millimetres, at that mark. A reading of 1.4234 metres claims a tenth of a millimetre the tape does not show. Write up to the smallest mark. The extra digit is a guess wearing a serious face.

Zero has to be the right end. A tape that starts at the 8 centimetre mark and meets the far end at 53 centimetres is measuring $53 - 8 = 45$ centimetres, not 53. A scale that already reads 120 grams with an empty bowl on it will add the flour to 120. Tare (zero with the bowl sitting there) if you want the flour alone. Leave the 120 if you want bowl and flour together. Name which.

NIST's Office of Weights and Measures, whose page was fetched for this book, works so that consumers get what they pay for and sellers get fair payment, by promoting a uniform system with SI use and traceability. A grocery scale, a fuel pump, and a shop ruler are instruments under that work. A number on an unchecked scale is still a reading. It is not yet a trusted mass.

A 500 gram test mass on a scale that reads 0 when empty: if the scale then reads 500 grams, it matched that mass today. If it reads 480 grams, the reading is 20 grams low. Do not "split the difference" with a previous shop. Fix the instrument, or name the 20 gram miss, or fetch a checked scale.

**Check.** A tape against a shelf reads 142 centimetres with zero at the end of the shelf. The shelf is $1.42$ metres if those marks are centimetres. A new case: the tape's 8 centimetre mark is at one end, the 53 centimetre mark at the other. The length is 45 centimetres. Another: empty scale reads 0. A 500 gram test mass reads 480. The instrument is 20 grams low on that mass. Write 480 grams as the reading, and 20 grams as the miss. Do not report 500 because "it should have been."

## 9. Error

Read the same shelf three times. The tape says 141 centimetres, then 143, then 142. The three numbers are not a fight you settle by picking the one you like. They are the instrument and your hands, repeating. The spread from smallest to largest is $143 - 141 = 2$ centimetres. Add the three and share into three: $141 + 143 + 142 = 426$, and $426 \div 3 = 142$. The shared value is 142 centimetres. The leftover 2 centimetre spread is still part of the report.

A single wild reading changes the share more than it changes the middle. 141, 143, and 190. Lined up: 141, 143, 190. The middle is 143. The share is $(141 + 143 + 190) \div 3 = 474 \div 3 = 158$. If you only quote 158, you hid the 190. Look at the three before you trust the share.

Two different misses live in those numbers. One miss is a lean: the instrument is always high, or always low, like the 20 gram-low scale. Repeating will not cancel a lean. A test mass, a second instrument, or a door has to catch it. The other miss is scatter: 141, 143, 142 dancing around 142. Repeating, then sharing, shrinks the dance. It does not shrink the lean.

Write what you know. 142 centimetres, three readings, spread 2 centimetres, smallest mark 1 centimetre. That sentence can travel. "About 142" cannot be checked. "Exactly 142.000" claims marks you did not have.

NIST OWM exists in part because a wrong scale at a shop is a money error, not a mood. Traceability, on that page's mission line, means a shop reading can be walked back through checked instruments toward the SI stick. This book does not walk that chain. It names why the chain is there: so 500 grams in one town is 500 grams in another.

**Check.** Four readings of a bag: 10.0 kilograms, 10.2, 9.8, and 10.0. Sum $10.0 + 10.2 + 9.8 + 10.0 = 40.0$. Share into four: $10.0$ kilograms. Spread from 9.8 to 10.2 is 0.4 kilograms. A new case: you zeroed the scale with a bowl already on it, then added flour, and the scale reads 180 grams. That 180 grams is the flour, not the flour plus bowl. If you needed both, you missed the bowl. Another: 141, 143, 190 centimetres. Middle 143. Share 158. Report both, and the 190, or you have dressed a wild reading as typical.

## 10. A check against a door

A number that must travel to another person, another year, or another country has to come from a host that owns it. Arithmetic you can redo by hand does not need a door. A definition, a legal conversion, a constant, an edition, or a retirement date does.

Walk:

1. Name the quantity: length, mass, time, or temperature.
2. Name the unit, in words first.
3. Write the reading, up to the smallest mark, with the spread if you repeated.
4. If the number is a definition, a conversion factor, a constant, or a date that changes a stick, open BIPM or NIST.
5. If the door and the page disagree, believe the door and mark the page. If the door will not open, write unverified and stop building on it.
