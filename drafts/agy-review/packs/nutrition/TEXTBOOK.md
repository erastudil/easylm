# Food as fuel for a body

You can already read. The food book taught heat, salt, spoilage, and a recipe. This book is the next act: what a plate does after it is eaten, and the box of numbers on a carton.

How a kidney or a liver uses a nutrient lives in the health pack. Ten minutes with a body that is hurt, stopped, or poisoned live in the first_aid pack. Name those doors and stop. Stay here when the task is a serving, a Daily Value, a day's energy count, or a walk on the guidelines page.

This pack owns those walks. A calorie, a milligram, or a Daily Value that must travel comes from Dietary Guidelines, the FDA Nutrition Facts door, or the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, or the line says unverified. A remembered recommended amount stays off this page.

Public general education.

---

## 1. Food as fuel

Tess eats a bowl of oats at 06:30 and walks the river path to the mill. An hour later the bowl is empty and she is still walking. The oats did not stay in the bowl. They became the walk, the warmth in her hands, and the work at the loom.

What a body takes from a plate and spends on a walk, a shift, a night of sleep, or the quiet work of staying alive is **fuel**. The everyday word "food" covers the oats, the bowl, the recipe, and the taste. This chapter uses the spend. The kitchen walk that cooked the oats lives in the food pack. Name that door and stop.

An empty plate at 06:30 is a different morning. Tess still walks. The walk draws on a store from yesterday's plates. The store is not endless. A long day with no plate spends the store down. The mill does not run on air. Neither does Tess.

Bram packs a tin for a night shift: bread, cheese, an apple. He eats at 22:00. At 04:00 he is still on his feet. The tin was a store he could count before he opened it. After he eats, the store is in him. The tin is light.

A second tin stays closed in the locker. Same bread, same cheese, same apple. No spend from that tin until someone eats it. Fuel for a body starts when the food is eaten, not when it is packed.

The energy pack counts work, heat, and a grid in joules and kilowatt-hours. Name that door and stop. The number on a food panel is a different writing of a spend, meant for a plate. How that writing is fixed to a joule is a door number. Unverified here. Do not finish a calories-to-joules factor from memory.

**Check.** Tess eats the oats. Bram eats the tin. Pio skips breakfast and works four hours on yesterday's store. Three mornings, three spends. A new case: two identical tins, one eaten at 22:00, one left shut. Fuel from the shut tin is none until it is eaten. Another: a loaf on the board, unsliced. The loaf is a kitchen store. It is not yet fuel for a body. If you counted the loaf as Tess's morning spend, you counted a shelf as a stomach.

## 2. A serving

A carton of oat drink sits on the boarding-house table. The side of the carton writes that the numbers in the box are for $240$ millilitres. Tess pours a mug. The mug holds two of those pours. She drinks the mug.

The pile the panel's numbers describe is a **serving**. The mug in her hand is a pour. The two piles match only when she pours what the panel named. Two pours are two servings. Each line in the box, read for that mug, is twice the written line, if the carton is honest.

A can of beans writes $3.5$ servings in the can. Tess heats the whole can for herself. She ate $3.5$ of the panel's piles, not one pile because she used one can. The can is a container. The serving is the panel's yardstick.

Bram's granola box writes that two bars are one serving, and that the box holds $6$ bars. $6 \div 2 = 3$ servings in the box. He eats four bars on the path. That is $4 \div 2 = 2$ servings, not four, and not one because it was one snack. The panel picked two bars as the pile. His mouth picked four bars.

A bowl heaped above the rim is still one bowl. It may be two servings, or more, if the panel's pile is a level cup and the heap is two of those cups. Count the panel's pile, not the crockery.

Household spoons are not the panel. A "spoon of oil" in a recipe is a kitchen step. The food pack owns that step. Here, if the panel writes $15$ millilitres as a serving of oil, two of those pours are two servings. A remembered kitchen spoon that you treat as $15$ millilitres without checking the panel is unverified as a serving.

**Check.** A jar writes $5$ servings. Inez eats $2$ servings at noon and $1$ serving at night. $2 + 1 = 3$ servings from the jar, $2$ remain. A new case: a carton writes $8$ servings, Tess uses $3$ cartons in a week. $8 \times 3 = 24$ servings. Another: a bag writes $4$ servings, Pio eats the bag and writes "$1$" because it was one bag. The panel's count is $4$. If you let the bag win, you threw away the yardstick.

## 3. Three kinds of fuel

Bram's plate holds rice, lentils, and a spoon of oil. The rice is mostly the white, starchy stuff plants store. The lentils are the stuff that builds and repairs. The oil is the greasy stuff that carries a lot of spend in a small pour. Three kinds, one plate.

People name those three **carbohydrate**, **protein**, and **fat**. The names are handles for the three piles you can already see. Fruit sugar and bread starch sit in the first kind. Beans, eggs, fish, milk, and meat sit in the second. Butter, oil, and the fat in nuts sit in the third. A plate can hold all three. A plate can lean on one.

The panel writes grams next to each kind. Grams of rice-fuel and grams of bean-fuel do not add into one honest total of "fuel," because they are different kinds. Grams on the same line do add. Bram's rice line writes $42$ grams of carbohydrate. A banana he eats after writes $27$ grams on its own carbohydrate line. $42 + 27 = 69$ grams of that kind from those two foods, if both panels are honest.

Do not turn those grams into calories from a factor you remember. How many calories hide in a gram of fat, or of carbohydrate, or of protein, is a door number. Unverified here. Add the grams the panel wrote. Add the calorie lines the panel wrote. Do not mix the two sticks with a remembered multiplier.

A fourth kind, alcohol, can also be a spend. This book stays with the three on an ordinary plate. If a label writes alcohol, treat that line as its own stick. Do not pour it into the protein grams.

Fiber sits with carbohydrate on many panels, as a part of plant carbohydrate that does not spend the same way in the body. The panel may write it on its own line. Keep that line. Do not subtract it from carbohydrate unless the panel's own arithmetic already did, and you can see the doing.

**Check.** A yogurt writes $11$ grams of protein. Cheese on the same lunch writes $8$ grams of protein. $11 + 8 = 19$ grams of that kind. A new case: bread $38$ grams of carbohydrate, jam $14$ grams of carbohydrate. $38 + 14 = 52$ grams of that kind. Another: a label writes $12$ grams of fat. Someone multiplies by a remembered number and writes calories in the margin. Stop. The gram line is grams. Open the FDA Nutrition Facts door or the USDA composition door for a factor, or write unverified. If you treated $12$ grams as $12$ calories, you mixed two sticks.

## 4. A vitamin

Nuri turns a bottle in the chemist shop and reads a small number next to the word iodine. The three fuels on a plate do not cover every piece a body uses. Some pieces are needed in small amounts. You do not eat them by the bowl. You meet them in fish, milk, a pinch of iodized salt, a fortified pour, or, if someone chose a bottle, in a tablet.

People name those small pieces **vitamins** when they are the carbon-based ones the body cannot make enough of, and **minerals** when they are the elemental ones, such as iodine. The name is a handle for a gap the three fuels do not fill. How a thyroid uses iodine is an organ walk. That walk lives in the health pack. Name that door and stop.

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements home page names iodine as important for proper thyroid function, and for bone and brain development during pregnancy and infancy. That sentence is the door's. A milligram or microgram that a body should meet in a day is not on that home page. Unverified here. Do not print a remembered recommended amount.

The same home page names salmon, tuna, and other fatty fish, along with vitamin D-fortified foods such as milk, as good sources of vitamin D. Fortified means the vitamin was added. The fish and the milk are the foods. The amount on a carton, in micrograms or in international units, is a label number. Fetch the carton or the ODS fact sheet. Unverified here as a daily target.

Nuri's bottle writes that one tablet is a serving, and that the bottle holds $90$ tablets. One tablet a day, if that is what the bottle's serving is, is $90$ days. $90 \div 3 = 30$ days if she takes three tablets a day. The arithmetic counts tablets. It does not prove she needed three.

ODS publishes fact sheets, A through Z, and databases of supplement labels. MedlinePlus, a service of the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health, is an online health information resource for patients and their families and friends, and it keeps a drugs-and-supplements door. Open those doors when a bottle claim must travel. A remembered letter-and-number vitamin dose stays off this page.

**Check.** A bottle of $60$ tablets, serving of $2$ tablets. $60 \div 2 = 30$ servings in the bottle. A new case: Nuri takes one serving every other day. $30$ servings last $60$ days. Another: a friend quotes a milligram of iodine from memory and writes it as Nuri's need. Open the ODS iodine fact sheet. If the door will not open, write unverified. If you treated the friend's milligram as a law, you skipped the host that owns the fact.

## 5. Water

Pio fills a glass from the tap and drinks it before the shift. He sweats on the mill floor. He drinks again at the noon bell. Nothing on that glass is a carbohydrate, a protein, or a fat. The glass is still doing work. Without water, the mill walk does not last.

What a body drinks, and what it loses in urine, sweat, breath, and stool, is the water account. People name the drink **water** when it is the tap, a bottle, or a kettle. Soup and fruit also carry water. The food pack owns water in a stew as a kitchen object. Name that door and stop. This chapter is the account in the body.

How much of a body is water is a door number. Unverified here. Do not print a remembered percent. How many glasses a day a guideline names is also a door number. Unverified here. Do not print a remembered "eight glasses." Pio's day is a count of what he drank, not a law.

A household glass is not a millilitre. Pio's morning glass, filled to the line he always uses, is one of his units. Two of those in the morning, one at noon, two after the shift: $2 + 1 + 2 = 5$ of Pio's glasses. If a bottle writes $500$ millilitres, two bottles are $1000$ millilitres. $500 + 500 = 1000$. That sum is millilitres. It is not yet a guideline.

Sweat is a loss. A hot loft, a long walk, a fever: more loss. The drink has to cover the loss plus the quiet work of a cooler day. You can see the drink. You cannot see every loss. Thirst is one signal. It is not a millilitre count.

Tea, coffee, and milk are drinks with water in them. They also carry other lines: caffeine is not a fuel in the three-kind sense; milk carries protein and fat. Count the water they contribute as water, and read their other lines on their own panels.

**Check.** Three bottles, each $330$ millilitres. $3 \times 330 = 990$ millilitres. A new case: Inez drinks $2$ of her glasses before work, $1$ at noon, $1$ at 16:00, $2$ at night. $2 + 1 + 1 + 2 = 6$ of her glasses. Another: a poster says everyone must drink eight glasses. That eight is unverified as a guideline. Pio's $5$ is what he drank. If you replaced his $5$ with the poster's $8$ and called it a measurement, you mixed a slogan with a glass.

## 6. A label

Sora turns a soup can and finds a boxed grid of numbers on the back. At the top, a serving size, and how many of those servings sit in the can. Under that, a calorie number for one serving. Under that, grams for the three fuels, a sodium line in milligrams, and a short list of vitamins and minerals with a percent.

That boxed grid is the **Nutrition Facts** label. The name is a handle for the panel you can already read. Whether Sora's can matches the living legal layout is an FDA door question. Unverified here as a statute. The can in her hand still has those lines, and those lines are what she can add.

The percent next to a vitamin, a mineral, or a fuel on many panels is a **Daily Value** percent. It is a share of a reference amount the label office set for a day, not a share of Sora's Tuesday, and not a share of Nuri's pregnancy. One serving that writes $8\%$ of Daily Value, eaten four times, is $8 \times 4 = 32\%$ of that Daily Value from those four servings, if the percent is per serving.

The milligram or gram that equals $100\%$ of a Daily Value is a door number. Unverified here. Do not finish that milligram from memory. You may still multiply the percent the panel wrote. You may still add milligrams the panel wrote. You may not invent the missing $100\%$ line.

Calories on the panel are for the serving. Sora's can writes $90$ calories per serving and $2$ servings in the can. The whole can, if she eats it, is $90 \times 2 = 180$ calories. A second can of the same soup is another $180$. Two cans are $360$. The number $90$ is this can's writing. It is not a law for all soup.

Sodium is the salt line on many panels, in milligrams. The pinch in the pan lives in the food pack. Here, the milligram line is the object. A serving writes $410$ milligrams of sodium. Two servings write $820$ milligrams. $410 \times 2 = 820$. Whether $820$ is high for a day is a Daily Value and guidelines question. Unverified here as a limit.

**Check.** A yogurt writes $70$ calories per serving, $4$ servings in the cup. Tess eats the cup. $70 \times 4 = 280$ calories. A new case: a cereal writes $10\%$ Daily Value of iron per serving. Bram eats $3$ servings. $10 \times 3 = 30\%$ of that Daily Value from the cereal, if the percent is per serving. Another: a friend says the Daily Value for sodium is a number remembered from a poster. Open the FDA Nutrition Facts door. If the door will not open, write unverified. If you put the poster's milligram on Sora's can, you wrote a memory onto a panel.

## 7. Energy in a day

Tess writes every panel from Tuesday on a scrap of paper. Breakfast oats: $240$ calories. A mug of oat drink: $110$ calories. Lunch beans and rice: $430$ calories. An apple: $80$ calories. Dinner soup, two servings: $180$ calories. A slice of bread: $90$ calories. She adds.

$240 + 110 = 350$. $350 + 430 = 780$. $780 + 80 = 860$. $860 + 180 = 1040$. $1040 + 90 = 1130$. Tuesday's written spend, from those panels, is $1130$ calories.

A day's energy from food is that sum. It is not a serving. It is not a can. It is the servings she actually ate, each multiplied as chapter 6 taught, then added. Miss a snack and the sum is short of the day. Count a serving twice and the sum is high.

$1130$ is Tess's Tuesday. It is not a requirement. How many calories a guideline names for a woman of her age, height, and activity is a door number on Dietary Guidelines for Americans. Unverified here. Do not print a remembered daily calorie target. Tess's $1130$ is a count of panels. A target would be a second number, from the door, to compare.

A day with no writing still had a spend if she ate. The scrap is the record, not the eating. Bram eats from a diner plate with no panel. USDA FoodData Central is a composition door for a food that has no carton. Open it when a diner plate must become a number. A remembered "plate of stew is about four hundred" stays off this page. Unverified until a door or a panel writes it.

Sleep, a walk, a loom shift, and sitting all spend. This book counts the inbound side: the plates. Outbound spend as a measured burn lives with clinics and with research doors, not with a kitchen scrap. Unverified here as a personal burn number. Do not subtract a remembered exercise calorie from Tuesday's $1130$ and call the remainder a law.

**Check.** Wednesday: breakfast $180$, lunch $350$, a bun $140$, dinner $490$, tea with milk $40$. $180 + 350 = 530$. $530 + 140 = 670$. $670 + 490 = 1160$. $1160 + 40 = 1200$ calories from those panels. A new case: Tess repeats Tuesday's $1130$ and Wednesday's $1200$. $1130 + 1200 = 2330$ for two days. Another: someone says Tess must eat two thousand calories because that number is on many labels as a footnote. The footnote is a reference, if the living label still writes it. Unverified here as Tess's need. Her scrap is $1130$. If you replaced $1130$ with $2000$ and called it Tuesday, you mixed a reference with a record.

## 8. A missing piece

Inez eats rice and oil for seven nights and nothing from the sea, and no iodized salt, and no milk. The three fuels may still add to a calorie number that looks full. A small piece can still be missing.

A diet that never meets a vitamin or a mineral the body uses is a **gap**. The calorie sum can be large and the gap can still be there. Tuesday's $1130$ does not prove iodine was in the oats. You have to look for the piece, on a panel, in a food list, or on an ODS fact sheet.

Seven nights of rice and oil: seven dinners. If each dinner writes no vitamin D on its panel, and no salmon, tuna, or other fatty fish, and no fortified milk, then those seven dinners did not bring the sources ODS named for vitamin D. One dinner of the fish ODS named is a different week. The count of dinners is $7$. The count of named vitamin D sources in those dinners is $0$. The gap is that $0$, not a disease name you apply from memory.

A gap is not a diagnosis. How a bone or a gland fails when a piece is missing for a long time lives in the health pack. Name that door and stop. First aid is ten minutes with a body in the room. A missing vitamin over months is not a ten-minute object. Name that door and stop.

A bottle can fill a gap only if the piece in the bottle is the piece that was missing, and if the serving is what the bottle wrote. ODS fact sheets and the ODS databases of supplement labels are the doors for what a bottle contains and what the science pages currently say. MedlinePlus health topics are a door for reading a condition in ordinary words. A milligram target from those doors is unverified here as a dose.

Variety is a practical hunt for missing pieces. A week of four kinds of plant, one kind of fish or bean, and a dairy or fortified pour, is a wider net than seven identical plates. Variety is not a number. It is a way of not relying on one panel to carry every small piece.

**Check.** Inez's week: $7$ rice-and-oil dinners, $0$ of the fish ODS named, $0$ fortified milk. Named vitamin D sources at dinner: $0$. A new case: she adds tuna on two nights. Named sources: $2$ of $7$ dinners. Another: a neighbour says Inez has a disease because her dinners look pale. The neighbour is not a door. Open MedlinePlus on the named condition if a word must be read, and ODS on the nutrient. If those doors will not open, write unverified. If you named a disease from the colour of rice, you skipped the host.

## 9. Who the advice is for

A page on a government site speaks to a group, not to one kitchen on Kestrel Street. Inez reads a sentence that starts with who the page is for. The next sentence may not fit a child, a person who is pregnant, or a person whose organs already need a clinic.

Advice on a guidelines page is written for an audience the page names. Open Dietary Guidelines for Americans and read the audience line. That line is unverified here. A remembered age cutoff stays off this page. The method still holds: find who the page says it is for, before you treat a number as yours.

A Daily Value is one reference person the label office chose, used so two cartons can be compared. Sora's $8\%$ is a share of that reference, as chapter 6 taught. Nuri, pregnant, is not that reference just because a carton writes a percent. ODS names iodine as important for bone and brain development during pregnancy and infancy. That is a who. It is not Nuri's milligram.

A child is not a small adult on a label. A panel percent may still be readable as arithmetic. The percent is still the reference person's percent. Whether a child should meet that reference is a guidelines and pediatric door question. Unverified here.

A body that is already ill is the health pack's object: organs, disease, a clinic's plan. This book names that door and stops. A body that is bleeding, choking, or down on the floor is the first_aid pack's object: ten minutes. This book names that door and stops. A healthy plate question stays here.

MyPlate is USDA's picture door for a plate aimed at the public the department names. FoodData Central is a composition door for a food. FDA owns the panel. ODS owns supplement fact sheets. MedlinePlus owns encyclopedia-style pages for patients and families. Pick the host that owns the kind of sentence you need. Do not borrow a child's chart for Tess, or Tess's scrap for a clinic.

**Check.** A carton writes $20\%$ Daily Value of calcium per serving. Tess eats two servings. $20 \times 2 = 40\%$ of that Daily Value. The $40\%$ is still the reference person's scale. A new case: a blog says the guidelines are for everyone on earth. Open dietaryguidelines.gov and read who it names. Unverified here. Another: Nuri uses an adult panel percent as her pregnancy milligram. Open the ODS iodine fact sheet and the guidelines audience line. If they will not open, write unverified. If you treated one carton's percent as every body's law, you skipped who the advice is for.

## 10. A check against the guidelines door

Someone tells Tess that her Tuesday total is too low. The someone may be a neighbour, a blog, a bottle, or a memory. A calorie target, a milligram Daily Value, or a who-this-is-for line comes from Dietary Guidelines, FDA, or ODS. $90 \times 2 = 180$ stays on paper.

Walk:

1. Name the object. A serving, a gram of one fuel, a vitamin, a glass of water, a panel percent, a day's calorie sum, a missing piece, an audience.
2. Separate the arithmetic from the law. $90 \times 2 = 180$ is taking a panel at its word. "$180$ is too low for a day" is a law. The law needs a door.
3. Pick the host. Dietary Guidelines for Americans at dietaryguidelines.gov for pattern and audience. FDA Nutrition Facts for the panel and Daily Value. USDA FoodData Central for a food with no carton. ODS for a supplement and for vitamin and mineral fact sheets. MedlinePlus for a condition in ordinary words.
4. Open the door. Read who it is for. Read the number. Write the date you opened it.
5. If the live page and a remembered line fight, keep the live page. If the host is dark, mark the milligram unverified.

Dietary Guidelines for Americans is the guidelines door this pack owns. A pattern, a calorie table, and an audience sentence from that door stay unverified here. Fetch the live page when Tuesday's $1130$ must be compared to a named pattern.

Milligrams that equal $100\%$ Daily Value stay unverified here until the FDA Nutrition Facts door is read.

The ODS home page names iodine's jobs, vitamin D foods, fact sheets, and databases. It does not print a recommended milligram on that home page. MedlinePlus is NLM's patient-and-family resource. A Daily Value is not that home page's object.

**Check.** Someone says Tess's $1130$ calories is half a required day. Half of an unverified target is still unverified. Open dietaryguidelines.gov. A new case: a bottle writes $50\%$ Daily Value of vitamin D per tablet. Two tablets: $50 \times 2 = 100\%$ of that Daily Value from the bottle's own arithmetic. Whether $100\%$ of that Daily Value is Nuri's need is an ODS and guidelines question. Open those doors. Another: Wednesday's panels add to $1200$ as in chapter 7. That add does not need a door. If you asked FDA to add $180$ and $350$ and $140$ and $490$ and $40$, you used the panel door as a calculator.
