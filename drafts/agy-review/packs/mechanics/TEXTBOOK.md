# Push, mass, and motion

You already unwrap a sentence and keep a unit on a count. This book is the next act: a shove you can point, a pile of stuff that fights a change of motion, and the motion itself. Write the shove. Write how much stuff. Write how the motion changes. Put the numbers back and see whether the world you named could hold them.

The measurement pack owns the SI walk: the metre, the kilogram, the second, and the sentences that fix those sticks. This book names that door and stops. The living physics pack in `stacks/physics/` is the survey of the field. This book owns the push, the mass, and the motion.

The longer course and the unit doors are in `LINK_INDEX.md`. Sound, waves, torque, and a spin's momentum stay on those doors. NIST constants and the BIPM brochure live in the measurement pack. Name that door and stop.

## 1. A push

A crate sits on a warehouse floor. Rafi leans his shoulder into one face and the crate starts to slide east. Sela leans into the opposite face and the crate slows, then stops, then starts west. The crate does not care who is tired. It answers the two leans as they are, right now, along that line.

A lean has a size and a way it points. Two leans along the same line add when they point the same way. They take away when they point opposite ways. Rafi at 20 of some push-unit east, Sela at 8 of the same unit west: leftover 12 east. Rafi at 11 east, Sela at 11 west: leftover 0, and the crate's motion along that line does not change from those two leans.

A lean that points elsewhere is a different story on a different line. Rafi east and Sela north do not cancel. Each line keeps its own leftover. This book walks one line until the circle in chapter 7, where the leftover has to keep turning.

The leftover of every lean on the crate, counted with size and direction, is the **net push**. The crate's motion along a line changes only when that leftover on that line is not zero. A lean you feel in your shoulder is still a lean if another lean cancels it. The crate then keeps whatever motion it already had along that line.

Stand and hold a bag still. Your arms are working. The bag is not speeding up and not sliding. Along the vertical, your up-lean matches the down-lean on the bag. Net push on the bag along that line: 0. The tiredness is in you. The bag's motion did not change.

**Check.** A new pair: 14 east and 5 west, same unit. Leftover 9 east. A second new pair: 6 north and 6 south. Leftover 0. A third: 9 east, then another 4 east, and 3 west. Leftover $9 + 4 - 3 = 10$ east. Put each leftover back as the two (or three) leans on a line. If a put-back fails, a sign was dropped, not "almost cancelled."

## 2. How fast it changes

A cart rolls along a painted line at 3 metres per second. Six seconds later a tape on the cart reads 15 metres per second, still along that line, still the same way. The speed number rose by $15 - 3 = 12$ metres per second. That rise took 6 seconds. The rise per second is $12 \div 6 = 2$ metres per second per second.

The speed number is how fast the place is changing. The 2 metres per second per second is how fast the speed number itself is changing. Those are two different rates. Algebra already let you write a letter for each. Call the speed $v$ and the time $t$. If $v$ goes from 3 to 15 in 6 seconds of steady rise, the change of speed over the change of time is 2. People name that rate an **acceleration** when they want one word. The word can wait until the rate is sitting on the page.

Direction rides with the speed. A cart at 8 metres per second east that later reads 8 metres per second west has not kept the same motion. The speed number is still 8. The arrow flipped. The change of that arrow, east-positive, is $8 - (-8) = 16$ metres per second the other way. If that flip took 4 seconds of steady change, the rate is $16 \div 4 = 4$ metres per second per second west. A turn is a change even when the speed number holds still. Chapter 7 is that case on a ring.

A rate can be negative on a chosen line. A cart at 10 metres per second that later reads 4 metres per second on the same line, same way, in 3 seconds: change $4 - 10 = -6$, rate $-2$ metres per second per second. The cart is slowing on that line. The minus is the way you chose the line, not a broken tape.

Units stay on the rate. Metres per second per second is a speed-unit per time-unit. Drop the units and 2 is a number that no longer names the walk. Measurement owns those sticks. Name that door and stop.

**Check.** A new tape: 1.5 metres per second to 7.5 metres per second in 4 seconds. Rise 6. Rate $6 \div 4 = 1.5$ metres per second per second. A slowing walk: 10 metres per second to 4 metres per second in 3 seconds. Rate $-2$ metres per second per second. A flip: 5 metres per second east to 5 metres per second west in 2 seconds. Change of the arrow, east-positive, is $-10$. Rate $-5$ metres per second per second. Put each rate back as change over time. If a put-back fails, the two tapes were copied wrong.

## 3. Mass

Rafi shoves two crates with the same shoulders for the same few seconds. One crate is a light empty box. The other is packed with bricks. The empty box picks up speed quickly. The brick crate barely starts. Same push, different change of speed.

The difference is how much stuff is in the crate. That pile of stuff is **mass**. A larger mass, same leftover push, gives a smaller change of speed. A smaller mass, same leftover push, gives a larger change of speed. The crate is not being stubborn. It is answering with the pile it has.

The SI unit of that pile is the kilogram. How the kilogram is fixed lives in the measurement pack and on the BIPM SI Brochure door. Unverified here. Do not finish that sentence from memory. A reading of 4 kilograms on a scale is a count of that pile. The definition is what makes that count comparable to a count in another country.

Two bags, 3 kilograms and 5 kilograms, take the same leftover push. The 3 kilogram bag's change of speed is $5/3$ of the 5 kilogram bag's change of speed, because the push is the same and the piles sit in the denominator. If the 5 kilogram bag gains 1.2 metres per second per second, the 3 kilogram bag gains $1.2 \times 5/3 = 2$ metres per second per second. Same push: $5 \times 1.2 = 6$ and $3 \times 2 = 6$. The two products match. That matching product is the next chapter.

Mass is the pile. It does not change when the crate starts moving, and it does not change when you carry the bag to another room. A later chapter names a pull on the pile. That pull can change from place to place. The pile does not.

**Check.** Same leftover push. A 2 kilogram crate gains 4 metres per second per second. An 8 kilogram crate then gains 1 metre per second per second, because $2 \times 4 = 8 \times 1$. A new pair: 6 kilograms at 1.5 metres per second per second. Product 9. A 3 kilogram crate on that same leftover push gains 3 metres per second per second. Another: 4 kilograms and 12 kilograms, same push. The 4 kilogram crate's rate is three times the 12 kilogram crate's rate. If the heavy crate gains 0.5 metres per second per second, the light one gains 1.5. If those two products disagree, a mass or a rate was copied wrong.

## 4. Force

You already have a push, a pile of stuff, and a change of speed. On one line, with one leftover push, those three sit in one sentence. The leftover push equals the mass times the rate at which the velocity changes:

$$F = m a$$

A 4 kilogram crate, speeding up at 3 metres per second per second along a smooth line: leftover push $4 \times 3 = 12$ of the unit kilogram-metre per second per second. People name that unit a **newton** when they want one word. How the newton is fixed as an SI derived unit lives in the measurement pack and on the BIPM SI Brochure, 9th edition (2019), complete text updated in 2026, DOI 10.59161/AUEZ1291. The brochure PDF was not opened here. The defining sentence is unverified in this book. The product $4 \times 3 = 12$ does not wait on that sentence.

The letter $F$ in that line is the leftover, the net push of chapter 1. Two people on opposite faces: 10 newtons east and 4 newtons west on a 2 kilogram crate. Leftover 6 newtons east. Rate $6 \div 2 = 3$ metres per second per second east. If you feed 10 into $F = m a$ and skip the 4, you have described a different crate.

A leftover of 0 means a rate of 0. The crate then keeps its velocity: still, or steady along the line. That is the same fact as chapter 1, written with mass in the sentence. A steady velocity is not a missing push. It is a matched set of pushes, or no push at all.

The sentence is a scale, the same way algebra's equals was a scale. Unwrap for the letter you need. $a = F/m$. $m = F/a$. Put the numbers back into $F = m a$ before you trust a fill. A negative $F$ on your line is a leftover the other way. A negative mass is not a pile. Refuse that fill.

**Check.** A new crate: 7 kilograms, rate 2 metres per second per second. Leftover $14$ newtons. Put back: $7 \times 2 = 14$. A new fill: leftover 20 newtons, mass 5 kilograms. Rate $4$ metres per second per second. Put back: $5 \times 4 = 20$. A two-push case: 18 newtons east, 6 newtons west, mass 4 kilograms. Leftover 12 newtons. Rate 3 metres per second per second east. If you used 18 as $F$ and got 4.5, you dropped the west lean.

## 5. Work as a push through a path

Nia leans on a crate until her shoulders burn. The crate does not move. Along the floor the leftover is 0, or the leftover never gets a path. Nothing about the crate's place has changed.

She leans again. This time the crate slides 7 metres along a painted line while a steady leftover of 18 newtons points along that same line. The leftover rode 7 metres of path. The delivered change on that crate is $18 \times 7 = 126$ of the unit newton-metre.

When a leftover push actually moves a thing through a distance along its own direction, that delivered pile is **work**. A longer path with the same leftover is more work. A harder leftover along the same path is more work. No path, no work on that crate from that lean, even if Nia's shoulders disagree.

The SI name for a newton-metre of this pile is the joule. How the joule is fixed lives in the measurement pack and on the BIPM brochure door. Unverified here. Energy as a household bill lives in the energy pack. This book names those doors and stops. The walk here is the push through the path.

A leftover of 9 newtons through 0 metres: work 0. A leftover of 5 newtons through 4 metres, then the same 5 through 3 metres more: $20 + 15 = 35$ joules. A leftover that points against the path takes work out of the motion: 8 newtons against a 3 metre slide is $-24$ joules on that crate.

From rest, the numbers close another way. A 4 kilogram crate, leftover 12 newtons, 3 seconds. Rate $12 \div 4 = 3$ metres per second per second. Speed after 3 seconds: $3 \times 3 = 9$ metres per second. Path at a steady rate of speed-up from rest: half of $3 \times 3^2$, which is $13.5$ metres. Work $12 \times 13.5 = 162$ joules. Half the mass times the speed squared: $\frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 81 = 162$ joules. Same pile. The work landed in the motion.

**Check.** A new path: 12 newtons through 8 metres. Work 96 joules. A second new path: 6 newtons through 11 metres. Work 66 joules. A hold: leftover 20 newtons, path 0, for 10 seconds. Work on the crate 0. From rest: 5 kilograms, leftover 8 newtons, 4 seconds. Rate 1.6 metres per second per second. Speed 6.4 metres per second. Path $\frac{1}{2} \times 1.6 \times 16 = 12.8$ metres. Work $8 \times 12.8 = 102.4$ joules. Half mass times speed squared: $\frac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 40.96 = 102.4$. If those two landings disagree, a 2 was dropped from the path rule or from the half.

## 6. Momentum

A 3 kilogram cart rolls at 4 metres per second and hits a 5 kilogram cart that sits still. After the hit they roll as one. What speed?

Mass times velocity, before the hit: $3 \times 4 = 12$, plus $5 \times 0 = 0$, total 12 of the unit kilogram-metre per second. After the hit the mass is 8 kilograms. If no leftover push from the floor along the roll, that 12 is still the total. Speed $12 \div 8 = 1.5$ metres per second along the same line.

The product of mass and velocity is **momentum**. A leftover push lasting a time changes that product by $F$ times the time. 6 newtons for 2 seconds: change of momentum 12 kilogram-metres per second. On a 4 kilogram cart from rest, that is a later speed of 3 metres per second, because $4 \times 3 = 12$. Same 12 you already met as $m a t$, because $a t$ is the change of velocity.

When two carts bump and the floor does not push along the line, the total of the products stays. Each cart can change. The sum does not. That staying is the conservation the MIT 8.01SC course page names for momentum. This chapter is the sum you can add by hand.

A 2 kilogram cart at 9 metres per second hits a 4 kilogram cart at rest and they stick. Total $18$. Mass 6. Speed 3 metres per second. An 8 kilogram cart at 2 metres per second has product 16, already, with no hit.

A fill can satisfy the arithmetic and fail the named thing. A later speed of $-3$ metres per second on a chosen line is a roll the other way. A mass of $-3$ kilograms is not a cart. Refuse that fill.

**Check.** New stick-together: 1 kilogram at 8 metres per second hits 3 kilograms at rest. Total 8. Mass 4. Speed 2 metres per second. A new impulse: 10 newtons for 3 seconds on 5 kilograms from rest. Change of momentum 30. Later speed 6 metres per second. Put back: $5 \times 6 = 30$, and $10 \times 3 = 30$. A third: 6 kilograms at 2.5 metres per second, product 15. If a hit with a still 4 kilogram cart, stick-together, total 15, mass 10, speed 1.5 metres per second. If the after-speed and the before-total disagree, a mass was left out of the sum.

## 7. A circle

A cart runs around a painted ring. A tape on the cart always reads 10 metres per second. The speed number holds still. The way the speed points does not. East, then southeast, then south. Every few seconds the arrow has turned.

A change of the arrow is a change of velocity. Chapter 2 already counted a flip on a line as a change. Here the change never stops, and it points toward the centre of the ring. Without a leftover push toward that centre, the cart would run off on a straight line at 10 metres per second.

In a short 0.1 second the cart at 10 metres per second runs 1 metre of arc. On a ring of radius 5 metres that arc is a turn of $1/5$ of a radian. The speed arrow turns by that same share. The leftover of the old arrow and the new arrow is about $0.2 \times 10 = 2$ metres per second, pointing inward. Rate $2 \div 0.1 = 20$ metres per second per second toward the centre. The same number from the compact rule $v^2/r$: $10^2 / 5 = 20$. The short-step leftover and the rule head toward each other as the step shrinks.

A 2 kilogram cart on that ring then needs a leftover push $2 \times 20 = 40$ newtons toward the centre. A string, a rail, a banked floor: something has to supply those 40 newtons. If the string cuts, the leftover toward the centre vanishes and the cart leaves along the tangent it had at that instant.

Steady speed on a ring is not a leftover of 0. It is a leftover that keeps turning with the cart. People name that leftover the **centripetal** push when they want one word: the push that keeps pointing at the centre. The word is a direction, not a new kind of shove.

**Check.** A new ring: speed 6 metres per second, radius 2 metres. Rate $36 / 2 = 18$ metres per second per second inward. A second: speed 8 metres per second, radius 4 metres. Rate $64 / 4 = 16$ metres per second per second. A mass on the second ring: 3 kilograms. Leftover push $3 \times 16 = 48$ newtons inward. Short-step on the first new ring: in 0.05 second the cart runs 0.3 metres of arc, share $0.3/2 = 0.15$, arrow leftover about $0.15 \times 6 = 0.9$, rate $0.9 / 0.05 = 18$. Same 18. If the short-step and $v^2/r$ disagree by more than the roughness of the step, the radius or the speed was copied wrong.

## 8. A spring

You pull the free end of a coil a little. The coil pulls back a little. You pull twice as far. The coil pulls back twice as hard, the other way. Let go, and the end runs toward the unstretched place, through it, and out the other side, then back again.

Call the stretch $x$, measured from the place the coil sits with no lean. For many coils, in a middle range of $x$, the pull back is a constant times $x$, and it points against the stretch:

$$F = -k x$$

The letter $k$ is how stiff that coil is, in newtons per metre. A coil that pulls 20 newtons when you hold it 0.08 metres past the free place has $k = 20 / 0.08 = 250$ newtons per metre. At 0.04 metres the pull is 10 newtons. At 0.12 metres the pull is 30 newtons. Double the stretch, double the pull, until the coil leaves the range where that sentence holds. Past that range the metal stays bent, or the turns bind. The sentence is a middle-range claim, not a promise at every $x$.

The minus is the way: stretch to the right, pull to the left. Compress to the left, push to the right. The coil always aims at $x = 0$.

Work to hold a stretch, from 0 to 0.08 metres, is not $20 \times 0.08$, because the pull was not 20 the whole way. It grew from 0 to 20. The average leftover along that path is 10 newtons. Path 0.08 metres. Work $10 \times 0.08 = 0.8$ joules. Half of $k x^2$: $\frac{1}{2} \times 250 \times 0.0064 = 0.8$. Same pile, now sitting in the coil. Let go, and chapter 5's landing in the motion can take it.

People name $F = -k x$ **Hooke's law** when they want the historical name. OpenStax *University Physics Volume 1* is the long walk on oscillations once this coil is fluent. This chapter is the pull that grows with the stretch.

**Check.** A new coil: $k = 400$ newtons per metre, stretch 0.05 metres. Pull size 20 newtons, against the stretch. A second new stretch on the 250 newton-per-metre coil: 0.12 metres. Pull size 30 newtons. Work from 0 to 0.05 metres on the 400 coil: average 10 newtons, path 0.05, work 0.5 joules, matching $\frac{1}{2} \times 400 \times 0.0025 = 0.5$. If the average-path pile and half $k x^2$ disagree, the 2 was dropped or the stretch was squared wrong.

## 9. Gravity as a pull

A stone and a steel bolt leave your hand together, from the same height, with no throw. In still air they hit together. The stone is not "lighter" in the sense that it falls slower. The pull on each pile is larger when the pile is larger, in step with the mass. The rate $a = F/m$ then matches for both. Air is a second leftover, against the motion. A dry leaf shows that second leftover. This chapter first walks the pull alone.

Near the ground, over a room's height, that pull on each kilogram is nearly the same number. This book uses 10 newtons per kilogram as a round teaching stand-in. A 4 kilogram bag is then pulled with 40 newtons toward the ground. A 2.5 kilogram bag with 25 newtons. The living digits for a standard gravity, and the CODATA 2022 digits for the gravitational constant, live on the NIST constants door (CODATA 2022, SRD 121, last update to data content May 2024). Those digits did not appear on the landing page opened for this book. Unverified here. Do not finish 9.8 from memory and call it NIST.

The pull on the pile is **weight**. The pile is mass. Same 4 kilogram bag on another world is still 4 kilograms. The pull number changes. Fetch NIST if a local gravity must travel. Unverified here.

Two separate piles pull on each other. Each feels the same size of leftover, opposite ways. A larger pile pulls harder. A larger distance weakens the pull. Double the distance between the same two piles and the pull is four times weaker. At one distance the pull is 36 of some unit. At twice that distance, $36 / 4 = 9$. At three times, $36 / 9 = 4$. The living factor that turns two kilograms and a metre into newtons is the gravitational constant on the NIST constants door. Digits unverified here.

The same pull that drops the stone can keep a cart on a ring if the sideways speed is large enough. Chapter 7's inward leftover is then this pull. The MIT 8.01SC course page names the motion of the planets as the problem those core objects were built to solve. The long walk is that course and OpenStax Volume 1. This chapter is the pull, the round 10 newtons per kilogram near a room's floor, and the inverse-square weakening with distance.

**Check.** A new bag, 6 kilograms, teaching pull 10 newtons per kilogram. Weight 60 newtons. A new bag, 2.5 kilograms: 25 newtons. Inverse square: 48 units at one distance. At twice the distance, 12 units. At half the distance, 192 units, because half the distance is four times the pull. If someone writes 9.8 newtons per kilogram as a NIST number without opening the constants door, mark the digits unverified. The teaching 10 is a stand-in. It is not the door.

## 10. A check

Write the push, the mass, and the motion. Put the numbers back. Ask whether the landing can be the thing you named.

A 5 kilogram crate on a smooth floor, from rest. Leftover 20 newtons along the floor for 4 seconds. Rate $20 / 5 = 4$ metres per second per second. Speed after 4 seconds: 16 metres per second. Path from rest: $\frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times 16 = 32$ metres. Work $20 \times 32 = 640$ joules. Half mass times speed squared: $\frac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 256 = 640$ joules. Momentum $5 \times 16 = 80$ kilogram-metres per second. Leftover times time: $20 \times 4 = 80$. The four landings close.

The crate then meets a coil with $k = 320$ newtons per metre, head-on, and compresses until the motion's pile has moved into the coil. Half $k x^2 = 640$. Then $160 x^2 = 640$, so $x^2 = 4$, and the compression is 2 metres if the coil stays in the middle range of chapter 8. Put back: $\frac{1}{2} \times 320 \times 4 = 640$. Same 640. The check closes.

A ring: 2 kilograms, speed 6 metres per second, radius 3 metres. Inward rate $36 / 3 = 12$ metres per second per second. Leftover $24$ newtons toward the centre. Short-step: 0.05 second runs 0.3 metres of arc, share $0.1$, arrow leftover about $0.6$, rate $12$. Same 12.

A fill can satisfy the arithmetic and fail the named thing. Unwrap $F = m a$ with $F = 12$ newtons and $a = -3$ metres per second per second and you get $m = -4$ kilograms. The algebra of the division holds. A pile of $-4$ kilograms is not a crate. The last question is whether the landing can be the thing you named.

A broken check is useful. Suppose someone claims a 5 kilogram crate, leftover 20 newtons, gains 20 metres per second per second, copying $F$ onto $a$. Put back: $5 \times 20 = 100$ newtons, which is not 20. Do not average 20 toward 4. Unwrap again from $a = F/m$.

When a later pack writes a field that pushes a charge, or a heat engine that turns, or a wave on a string, it is this habit. The leftover push, the pile, and the change of motion are one sentence. Work is that leftover through a path. Momentum is the pile times the velocity. A ring still needs an inward leftover. A coil's leftover grows with the stretch. Gravity is a pull you can count, with living digits on NIST. Your job is to write the sentence, put the number back, and ask whether the fill can be the thing you named.
