# A wage, a price, and interest on one life

You can already add, take away, multiply, share, and name a percent. This book is those moves on money that belongs to one life. A shop pays you. A ticket asks you to pay. A pile sits. Extra is added to a pile, or to a debt. A paper demands a sum by a day. Tax comes off a stub. People share a loss. You plan a month. You match your notes to a statement.

How a whole market sets a price, and how households and firms meet there, lives in the economics pack. This book names that door and stops. The walks here are a paycheck, interest on a balance, and a household bill.

Load-bearing public numbers in these chapters come from four official pages fetched on 20 September 2026: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau at [consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/), the Internal Revenue Service at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov/), the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation at [fdic.gov](https://www.fdic.gov/), and the Consumer Price Index home at [bls.gov/cpi](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/). A number that did not appear on those pages is marked unverified, or it is a worked case invented for the arithmetic. Paper first. The app calculator may check a sum after you have tried it.

## 1. A wage

A shop writes $19.50 next to each hour you stood at the counter this week. You stood 28 hours. $28 \times 19.50 = 546$. The shop owes you $546$ for those hours before anyone takes a slice off the paper.

That dollars-for-time figure is a **wage**. Written per hour, it is an **hourly wage**. The pile for the week or the two weeks, still whole, is **gross pay**. Gross means before the stub subtracts.

A second shop does not count hours on the stub. It writes $2,860$ for a half-month. Same object: money for work, named as a pile instead of as a rate. To compare the two shops you still need a time. $2,860$ for half a month is not yet an hourly wage until you name the hours.

Overtime is the same object with a second rate. Forty hours at $18.00$ is $720$. Six more hours at $27.00$ is $162$, because one-and-a-half times $18$ is $27$. Gross for the week is $720 + 162 = 882$. The extra rate did not invent a new kind of money. It changed the multiplier on some of the hours.

A national series is not your counter. The CPI home page fetched for this sitting prints, in its latest-numbers panel, average hourly earnings up $\$0.10$ (p) in August 2026. The (p) is the page's own mark. That $0.10$ is a change in a Bureau of Labor Statistics series, not the $19.50$ on your paper. Your wage is the number the shop wrote.

**Check.** Thirty-five hours at $16.80$. $35 \times 16 = 560$. $35 \times 0.80 = 28$. Gross $588$. A new case: three days at $142$ a day. $3 \times 142 = 426$. Another: 22 hours at $21.25$. $22 \times 21 = 462$. $22 \times 0.25 = 5.50$. Gross $467.50$. If any product disagrees, an hour was dropped or the cents were slid one place.

## 2. A price you pay

The bakery printed $7.40$ on a loaf on Monday. On Friday the same loaf is $7.90$. You still need bread. You pay $7.90$. The extra $0.50$ is the same loaf costing more, not a second loaf.

The number on the ticket for one thing you buy is a **price**. The price for one unit inside a pack is a **unit price**. A 4-pack of soap at $6.40$ is $6.40 \div 4 = 1.60$ per bar. A 10-pack at $14.50$ is $1.45$ per bar. The bigger pack is cheaper per bar this time. It is not cheaper if you only needed two bars and the rest sit unused.

A sale is a price with a cut named. A coat marked $55$, twenty percent off: $55 \times 0.20 = 11$. You pay $55 - 11 = 44$ if that is the only cut. The 20% is a share of this coat, not a share of your month.

One ticket is one life. A number for how a basket of many prices moved, for urban consumers across the United States, lives at the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The CPI home page fetched on 20 September 2026 defines the **Consumer Price Index** as a measure of the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a market basket of consumer goods and services. For August 2026 that page prints $+0.4\%$ seasonally adjusted, and $+3.4\%$ over the last 12 months, not seasonally adjusted. All items less food and energy: $+0.3\%$ in August, $+2.4\%$ over the year. A Beyond the Numbers blurb on the same page says the CPI increased $2.9\%$ from December 2023 to December 2024.

Those percents are basket moves. They are not your loaf. If a $95$ coat had moved with the 12-month $3.4\%$: $95 \times 0.034 = 3.23$, and $95 + 3.23 = 98.23$. Use that only as a what-if on this coat. The basket also holds fuel, rent, and food you did not buy that day. The next CPI release named on that page is the September 2026 index, scheduled for 14 October 2026 at 8:30 A.M. Eastern Time.

How shops and buyers together set a price in a market is the economics pack. Stop there.

**Check.** Rice at $3.80$ a kilogram, you buy $2.5$ kg. $2.5 \times 3.80 = 9.50$. A new case: $800$ grams is $0.8$ kg at $4.20$ a kilogram. $0.8 \times 4.20 = 3.36$. Another: a $50$ bus pass that moved with the $2.9\%$ December-to-December figure from the CPI page. $50 \times 0.029 = 1.45$. New pass $51.45$, if that one item had followed the basket. Say the if.

## 3. A pile that lasts

After rent and food left the account, $2,160$ still sat there. Next month, living costs $1,440$ if nothing else changes. $2160 \div 1440 = 1.5$. The pile covers one month and half of the next, at that cost.

Money you do not spend this month, on purpose, is **saving**. The leftover pile is **savings**. The pile is not a wage. The wage already arrived. This is what remains after some of it has gone.

A pile in a drawer can be lost, burned, or taken. A pile in a bank is a number on the bank's books, in your name. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, on the homepage fetched for this sitting, is an independent agency created by the Congress. That page says the FDIC insures deposits, and also examines and supervises financial institutions. How much of a given pile is covered is not printed as a dollar cap on that homepage. The page points to the Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator, EDIE, at [edie.fdic.gov](https://edie.fdic.gov/), and to deposit-insurance FAQs. Open EDIE with your own numbers. The cap itself is unverified here.

The same homepage warns of impersonation scams. It says the FDIC will never ask you to provide money or other personally identifiable information over the phone. BankFind Suite, linked from that page, is the door for whether a named bank is an FDIC-insured institution. I did not run a BankFind query this sitting, so no bank name in this book is certified here.

Treasury savings products, sold by the Treasury, sit at TreasuryDirect. I did not fetch that host. Rates, terms, and which products exist today are unverified here. The door is in the link index.

**Check.** Pile $3,150$. Month cost $900$. $3150 \div 900 = 3.5$ months. A new case: pile $780$, month cost $390$. Two months. Another: you add $120$ this month to a pile of $640$. New pile $760$. If the month still costs $380$, the pile now lasts $760 \div 380 = 2$ months. The added $120$ bought a second month at that cost, not a raise.

## 4. Interest

A pile of $1,250$ sat in a book for a year. The book then added $50$. Nothing else happened to the pile. The extra $50$ came from time and a rate, not from a new hour at the shop.

That extra is **interest**. The rate is the extra as a share of the starting pile, for a named time. $50 \div 1250 = 0.04 = 4\%$ per year. The starting pile is the **principal**.

Simple interest uses the original pile each time. $640$ at $5\%$ for one year: $640 \times 0.05 = 32$. The book adds $32$. After one year the pile is $672$. If the same $32$ is added for a second year, and the rate is still taken on the original $640$, two years add $64$, and the pile is $704$. The second year did not pay interest on the first year's $32$.

Compound interest uses the new pile. $400$ at $3\%$ per period, two periods. First period: $400 \times 0.03 = 12$, pile $412$. Second period: $412 \times 0.03 = 12.36$, pile $424.36$. The second extra is larger because the base grew. Same $3\%$, different bases.

A year-rate printed on a consumer product has a legal name, often **APR**. I fetched the CFPB homepage, not an APR rule page. The homepage says the Bureau supervises covered financial institutions for federal consumer financial law and enforces those laws. The exact APR formula, and which fees sit inside it, are unverified here. Open the CFPB door in the link index when a lender's paper uses that word. Do not finish the legal walk from memory.

Interest on money you owe is the same arithmetic with the sign flipped. The extra is added to what you must pay, not to a pile you keep. Chapter 5 takes that case. The rate still needs a time. $1\%$ per month is not $1\%$ per year. Twelve months of $1\%$ compounded is not $12\%$, because each month's extra joins the base. $100$ at $1\%$ per month for two months: $101$, then $102.01$.

**Check.** $2,000$ at $2.5\%$ for one year, simple. $2000 \times 0.025 = 50$. A new case: $900$ at $1\%$ for one month. Extra $9$, pile $909$. A third: two months, compound, same $1\%$. Second extra $9.09$. Pile $918.09$. If you added $9$ twice and wrote $918$, you treated it as simple. The missing $0.09$ is interest on the first month's $9$.

## 5. A debt

You walk out with $480$ of tools today and a paper that says you will pay $480$ later. You have the tools. You do not have $480$ of your own in the shop's till. You owe $480$.

Money you have promised to pay is a **debt**. What still remains unpaid is the **balance**. Pay $120$ of the $480$. Balance $360$. The tools are already in your bag. The $360$ is the leftover promise.

The shop may add interest to the balance. $1.5\%$ of $360$ is $5.40$. New balance $365.40$ if no other fee lands. The $5.40$ is not more tools. It is time and a rate on what you still owe.

A paper may ask for a slice, not the whole. Balance $365.40$, asked slice $25$. If you pay $25$, leftover $340.40$ before the next extra. If you pay $100$, leftover $265.40$. The asked slice is a floor on that paper, not a law of arithmetic. Paying more cuts the base that the next rate will use. Paying only the slice leaves a larger base.

A second walk. You owe $720$. The rate is $2\%$ per month on the balance. Extra $14.40$. You pay $80$ the same day the extra is added. $720 + 14.40 - 80 = 654.40$. Next month's extra is $2\%$ of $654.40$, which is $13.088$, written $13.09$ if you keep cents by ordinary rounding. The new balance before the next payment is $667.49$. If instead you had paid $14.40$ and nothing more, the base would have stayed $720$ and the extra would have been $14.40$ again. Same rate. Different bases. That is the interest-on-a-balance walk this pack owns.

CFPB is the federal consumer-finance door for credit products. I fetched the homepage, not a credit-card rule. Fee lists, grace days, and what a lender must print are unverified here. Open the door.

**Check.** You owe $810$. You pay $270$. Left $540$. Then $2\%$ is added: $10.80$. New balance $550.80$. A new case: owe $1,050$, pay $1,050$. Left $0$. No extra on zero. Another: owe $250$, rate $4\%$ once, no payment yet. Extra $10$. Balance $260$. Then you pay $40$. Left $220$.

## 6. A bill

A folded paper from the electric office lists 310 kilowatt-hours and $0.16$ for each of them. $310 \times 0.16 = 49.60$. A service charge of $9.75$ sits under that line. $49.60 + 9.75 = 59.35$. The paper names a day by which $59.35$ must arrive.

That paper is a **bill**. It is a demand for a named amount, by a named day, for a named service or good. Rent written as $1,050$ due the first of the month is a bill. A phone paper with $41.20$ due the 18th is a bill. The wage chapter paid you. This chapter asks you to pay.

A late fee is a second line. Miss the day, and the next paper adds $8.00$. New total $67.35$ if nothing else changed. The $8.00$ is not more electricity. It is a cost of missing the day, on that contract.

Two bills can share a month and still be two objects. Water: 18 units at $4.25$ is $76.50$, plus $11.20$ service, total $87.70$. Electric $59.35$. Together $147.05$ if both are open. Adding them does not merge the due days. Pay the electric on its day even if the water day is later.

A bill can be wrong. 310 kilowatt-hours on the paper, your own meter notes 281. The difference is 29 hours of charge you may not owe. $29 \times 0.16 = 4.64$. The walk is: read the meter, read the paper, subtract. Chapter 10 does that matching for a bank statement. The same matching belongs on a utility paper.

This pack owns the household-bill walk: units, rate, extra lines, due day, sum. What a regulator allows a utility to charge is not on the four pages fetched this sitting, so no official tariff number is printed here.

**Check.** Phone $43.18$ and a clinic copay $27.90$. Sum $71.08$. A new case: 95 therms at $1.12$ is $106.40$, plus $13.50$ service, total $119.90$. Another: a bill of $59.35$ paid five days late with an $8.00$ fee. You send $67.35$. If you send only $59.35$ after the fee has landed, $8.00$ remains on the next paper.

## 7. Tax on a pay stub

The shop paid you $1,720$ for two weeks of hours. The paper that came with the pay lists $1,720$ at the top and $1,489$ at the bottom. Three lines sit in between: federal tax taken $142$, state tax taken $51$, other taken $38$. $142 + 51 + 38 = 231$. $1720 - 231 = 1489$. The $1,489$ is what reached your account.

The top pile is **gross pay**, the same word as in chapter 1. The bottom pile, after the taken lines, is **net pay**, take-home. The taken lines on this paper are **withholding**: money sent onward now, against tax that an office will tally later. The names of the boxes, and which office gets which line, belong on a real stub and on IRS forms. They are not invented here as official labels.

I fetched [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov/) on 20 September 2026. The fetch returned almost no body text: a title that names the Internal Revenue Service as an official website of the United States government, and little else. Withholding tables, tax rates, standard deductions, and the living box names on Form W-4 or Form W-2 are unverified here. Open the IRS door for those. The $142$, $51$, and $38$ above are a walk, not a table from that host.

Social Security and Medicare, when they appear as taken lines, have their own host at the Social Security Administration. I did not fetch ssa.gov this sitting. Those rates are unverified here.

A second stub. Gross $850$ for a week of 40 hours at $21.25$, because $40 \times 21.25 = 850$. Taken $96$. Net $754$. The hourly wage did not change. The net is the wage after the stub's subtractions. If you plan a month from $850$ and the account only receives $754$, the plan is using the wrong pile. Chapter 9 uses take-home.

**Check.** Gross $2,040$. Taken $318$. Net $1,722$. A new case: gross $967.40$, taken $0$ on this stub. Net $967.40$. Zero taken is an answer, not a missing line. Another: two weeks of $19.50$ at 32 hours each week. One week gross $624$. Two weeks $1,248$. If taken is $187$, net $1,061$. Redo $32 \times 19.50$ first. If that product is wrong, the rest of the stub is a story about a different wage.

## 8. A pool for a loss

One hundred fifty houses each put $40$ into a shared box. The box holds $6,000$. One kitchen then costs $3,200$ to repair. The box pays $3,200$ if the rules of the box cover kitchens. Each house has paid $40$. No single house had to produce $3,200$ that week.

That shared box is **insurance** as a pool for a loss. The $40$ is a **premium**. The written rules are the **policy**. A **deductible** is the first slice of a covered loss that you still pay yourself. Loss $2,100$, deductible $500$: you pay $500$, the pool pays $1,600$, if the loss sits inside the rules.

The pool can be empty of your kind of loss. A flooded cellar, when the paper covers fire and theft only, does not draw $3,200$ from the box. The premium already paid does not buy a different loss. Read the event against the paper.

A second pool. Eighty people put $25$. $80 \times 25 = 2,000$. One loss of $1,150$ leaves $850$ in the box if no other claims land. A later loss of $900$ would not fit. The arithmetic of a small pool is visible. A large company pool hides the other houses, but the object is the same: many premiums, few losses, a rule for who is paid.

I did not fetch an insurance-rate page. No official premium in this chapter is a market quote. CFPB's homepage, fetched this sitting, frames federal consumer financial law and fair lending. It does not print a homeowner premium. FTC is the door in the link index for many fraud and impersonation walks. Use those hosts. Do not finish a premium from memory.

**Check.** Loss $2,800$, deductible $750$. You pay $750$. Pool pays $2,050$ if covered. A new case: 60 people $\times$ $35$ = $2,100$ in the box. One covered loss $480$, deductible $0$. Pool pays $480$. Left $1,620$. Another: loss $900$, deductible $900$. You pay $900$. Pool pays $0$ on that loss. The deductible ate the claim. The premium already paid does not come back as a refund in this walk unless the paper says so, and no such paper was fetched.

## 9. A plan for a month

Take-home for the month is $2,640$. Rent is $1,050$. Food is $385$. The bus pass is $72$. The phone is $41$. Add the four: $1050 + 385 = 1435$. $1435 + 72 = 1507$. $1507 + 41 = 1548$. Left $2640 - 1548 = 1092$.

That written list, with money in, money out, and a leftover, is a **plan** for the month. People also call it a **budget** after they have seen the list. The leftover $1,092$ is not free of the month. It is unassigned on this paper. You may assign it to the pile in chapter 3, to a debt in chapter 5, or to a bill that has not yet arrived. If you spend it twice on the page, the plan is a wish.

Use take-home, not gross. Chapter 7's first stub had gross $1,720$ and net $1,489$ for two weeks. Two such stubs in a month are $2,978$ net if both match. Planning from $1,720 \times 2 = 3,440$ would count money the stub already sent onward.

A share names the weight of one line. Rent $1,050$ of take-home $2,640$ is $1050 \div 2640 = 0.3977\ldots$, which is $39.8\%$ to the nearest tenth of a percent. Food $385$ of $2,640$ is $14.6\%$ to the nearest tenth. The percents must use the same whole. Mixing a percent of gross with a percent of net is two wholes.

If a new bill of $64$ lands, leftover becomes $1092 - 64 = 1028$ if you take it from the unassigned pile. If the unassigned pile is already spent in the world but not on the paper, the plan and the account will disagree. Chapter 10 is how you catch that.

This is one life for one month. National income tables and market prices for a country live in economics and in data. Stop there.

**Check.** In $1,980$. Rent $790$. Food $310$. Left $880$. A new case: add a $64$ bill to that list. Left $816$. Another: take-home $2,210$, rent $995$. Rent's share $995 \div 2210 = 0.4502\ldots$, which is $45.0\%$ to the nearest tenth. If someone says "rent is half" on that month, the paper says no. Half of $2,210$ is $1,105$.

## 10. A check against a statement

Your own list for the week: $18.40$ at the grocer, $6.25$ at the bus, $51.00$ at the clinic. Sum $18.40 + 6.25 = 24.65$. $24.65 + 51.00 = 75.65$. The bank's paper lists those three lines and a fourth: $4.00$. Sum $79.65$. The $4.00$ is a line you did not write. It may be a fee, a forgotten swipe, or a second grocer ticket you left on the counter.

That bank paper is a **statement**. Walking your list against it, line by line, and naming every mismatch, is a **check** against the statement. Some books call the matching **reconciliation**. The name can wait. The work is the matching.

Match in one direction, then the other. Every line on your list should appear on the statement, or sit as pending. Every line on the statement should appear on your list, or be a named stranger. A stranger of $4.00$ is small and still a stranger. A stranger of $180.00$ is the same job with a louder number.

A second week. Your notes: $120.00$, $45.50$, $9.80$. Sum $175.30$. Statement: those three, plus $3.15$. Total $178.45$. If the $3.15$ is a fee the bank named, write "fee $3.15$" on your list so next week's walk starts clean. If you cannot name it, it stays a stranger until you can.

A match is a check that passed. Notes $62.00$, statement $62.00$, same day, same place. Tick both. Do not tick from memory of the amount without the day. Two $62.00$ lines on two days are two events.

Interest from chapter 4 can appear as a small credit. A pile of $1,250$ at a tiny monthly extra might show $2.08$ on a statement. I did not fetch a bank's rate sheet. Treat the $2.08$ as a line to match, not as a national rate. A bill payment from chapter 6 should appear as a debit on the same statement on or after the day you sent it. If the electric $59.35$ is missing a week after you sent it, the statement and the utility have a gap. The gap is the next object to name, not a reason to invent the $59.35$ a second time in a new envelope without looking.

FDIC's homepage, fetched this sitting, is the door for whether the institution is insured, and for EDIE on how much of a deposit pile is covered. It is not a copy of your statement. Your statement comes from the bank. The check is yours.

**Check.** Notes $33.20 + 14.00 + 8.45 = 55.65$. Statement shows $33.20$, $14.00$, $8.45$, and $1.50$. Statement sum $57.15$. Name the $1.50$ or mark it unknown. A new case: notes include $88.00$ that the statement does not have. The $88.00$ is pending, a date mismatch, or a payment that did not leave. Do not tick it. Another: statement credit $2.08$, notes empty. Write the $2.08$ as interest or as a refund once you can see which, then the two papers agree on the new pile.
