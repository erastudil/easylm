# Who may copy

Study taught you to keep a page. This book is the next object: who may copy a page, a song, a chart, or a program, and what a license text permits.

A mood about sharing will not do the job. You need a written permission you can point at, or a statute you can open.

This book is public general education. A number that must travel comes from a host that owns the fact, or the line says unverified.

A court opinion and a statute as jurisprudence live in the living law pack. This book names that door and stops. Stay here when the task is permission, notice, copyleft, or a check against the license text.

---

## 1. Who may copy

Reedhaven mill sits on the estuary. Mara wrote a 12-page tide pamphlet for the dock: 14 tables, one map of the bar, a note on the bell. She keeps the master on the mill desk. Len, who runs the ferry kiosk, wants 48 copies for the waiting room.

The pamphlet is not a rock on the beach. The paper can be fed through a copier. The question is not whether a copier can do the work. The question is whether Len may do it.

Before any legal name, hold the two chairs. Who made the pamphlet. Who wants a copy. Mara made it. Len wants 48. Those chairs do not yet tell you the answer. They tell you whose leave you would need, if leave is required.

People name the legal handle for that leave **copyright** when the object is a writing, a picture, a song, a chart, or a program of this kind. The name arrives after the chairs.

The U.S. Copyright Office front door at copyright.gov says copyright is one type of intellectual property, and that intellectual property protects creations of the mind. The same door points at Title 17 of the United States Code as U.S. copyright law, and at Title 37, Chapter II, for the Office's regulations. It also names a Copyright Claims Board that can hear copyright disputes of up to $30,000 as an alternative to federal court. Those are door numbers. Title 17 holds the term of years, the fees, and the exceptions. Open that statute for them. Unverified here: any year, fee, or exception list. The living law pack owns the walk through a court opinion.

Len cannot settle the 48 copies by saying the waiting room would be nicer with them. Niceness is a mood. Copying is an act. If Mara has not given leave, and if no statute you can open has already given leave, the 48 copies are not Len's to print.

A copy is a new pile of the same words or pictures. Handing Mara's one master around the kiosk is not 48 copies. Feeding the master through a copier 48 times is.

**Check.** Write three lines: who made the 12-page pamphlet, who wants 48 copies, and whether a written leave exists. If the leave line is blank, Len does not yet have the act. If you wrote "people share on the dock, so it is fine," you filled the blank with a mood. Erase the mood. Leave the blank.

New case: Len copies 2 pages onto a chalkboard for one 07:00 crew briefing, then wipes the board at 07:20. That is not the 48. It is a new act. Do not invent a statute that lets him. Open Title 17. Until you have the page, the chalkboard leave is unverified.

## 2. A default

No license line sits on Mara's pamphlet. No stamp. No short name. The stack on the mill desk looks like paper anyone could take. A visitor from the 14:00 ferry slips one copy into a bag and later prints 20 more at a shop in town.

The visitor found a physical copy. Finding is not leave. The default, when no permission has been granted and no statute you can open has already granted the act, is that the person who holds the copyright still holds the right to copy. People name that starting position the **default**. The name is a handle for "no extra leave yet," not for a mood that the work wants to be free or wants to be locked.

The copyright.gov front door offers registration of works, a search of copyright records, and recordation of transfers of copyright ownership. Registration is a desk. Recordation is a desk. Neither desk is a license you invent on the ferry. If you need to know whether Mara registered the pamphlet, search the records door. If the search returns nothing you can quote, write unverified. Do not treat a missing search as proof that no copyright exists.

The GNU licenses page, which this book opens later as a software door, states a related default in its own vocabulary: the simplest way to make a program free is to put it in the public domain, uncopyrighted, and that this also lets someone else take the program private. That page is not U.S. Title 17. It is a warning from another host about what happens if you drop the legal handle entirely. Public domain, on that page, means uncopyrighted. How a U.S. work enters the public domain, and after how many years, is unverified here. Open Title 17. Do not invent a year from memory.

A default is testable. Ask: did the holder grant this act, in words you can point at. If no, ask: does a statute I can open already grant this act. If no, you are still on the default. "I found it" fails both questions. "Everyone copies tide tables" fails both questions. A rumor on the dock is not Title 17.

**Check.** The visitor prints 20 copies from a found pamphlet with no license line. Write the two questions. Grant from Mara: none on the page. Statute you opened: none in this chapter. The 20 copies sit on the default. If you wrote "found property is free to copy," you invented a rule. Cross it out.

New case: a 7-verse dock song, "Seven Gulls," sung at dusk. No page, no license line, no registration you have fetched. A tourist records all 7 verses on a phone and later sells the recording. The medium changed. The default did not. The song still has a maker. The recording is a copy of another kind. Open Title 17 for sound recordings. Unverified here: any special statute for a song on a dock.

## 3. A permission

Mara writes a short sheet and clips it to the pamphlet:

You may copy this pamphlet and hand the copies out. Keep my name on each copy. Do not sell the copies.

Len now has more than a mood. He has a grant, an act, a keep-the-name condition, and a do-not-sell condition. He can test the 48 waiting-room copies against those words. If he keeps Mara's name and gives the copies away, the sheet covers him. If he sells them at the kiosk for $2 each, he left the sheet.

A written leave to do an act the default would block is a **license**. People also say permission. The name is a handle for the sheet, not for a handshake on the dock and not for a transfer of the whole right. copyright.gov names a separate desk for recording transfers of copyright ownership. Selling the copyright itself is not the same act as letting Len print 48. If Mara later records a transfer, open that record. This chapter's object is the leave, not the sale of the right.

A permission has chairs. Who grants. Who receives. What act. What must be kept. What is forbidden. Empty chairs are allowed. Write them empty. Do not fill them from a vibe.

"Feel free to share" has no act, no keep, and no forbid. A stranger cannot test it. "Copy, keep the name, do not sell" can fail in public. Failure is how a permission earns the next sentence.

The grant can be narrow. Mara can give the leave only to the ferry co-op, not to the whole street. Then a tourist who prints 20 copies is outside the sheet even if the tourist keeps the name and does not sell. The who-receives chair did that work.

The grant can be wide. Mara can give the leave to anyone who finds the pamphlet. Then Len and the tourist may both copy, still inside the keep-the-name and do-not-sell chairs.

**Check.** Write the five chairs for Mara's clipped sheet. Who grants: Mara. Who receives: not named, so treat as anyone who can read the sheet, or mark the chair empty if you refuse to guess. Act: copy and hand out. Keep: her name. Forbid: sale. Now change "Do not sell" to "You may sell." The kiosk's $2 pile, 48 copies, is inside the new sheet and outside the old one. If both answers came out the same, the forbid chair was not doing work.

New case: Mara's sheet says the leave is only for the ferry co-op. Len is in the co-op. A tourist is not. The tourist prints 20. Name which chair the tourist fails. If you said "the tourist was rude," you named a mood. The chair is who receives.

## 4. Creative Commons

Mara does not want to draft a new sheet for every pamphlet. She wants a permission a stranger already knows how to read. A nonprofit named Creative Commons publishes ready-made license texts for that job. The licenses list at creativecommons.org/licenses/ puts the 4.0 International family at the top. Each listed 4.0 text has a short page called a Deed and a full page called Legal Code, in many languages.

Those two pages are not the same object. The Deed is a map. The Legal Code is the permission. If they ever seem to fight, the Legal Code is the grant. This book did not fetch a 4.0 Legal Code. Any clause you remember about "non commercial" or "share alike" from a talk is unverified until you open the Deed or the Code.

The SPDX License List, version 3.29.0 dated 2026-09-16 on the SPDX door, gives each of those texts a short identifier. Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International is `CC-BY-4.0`. Attribution Share Alike 4.0 International is `CC-BY-SA-4.0`. Attribution Non Commercial 4.0 International is `CC-BY-NC-4.0`. Attribution No Derivatives 4.0 International is `CC-BY-ND-4.0`. Combinations of Non Commercial with Share Alike or No Derivatives have their own identifiers. Creative Commons Zero v1.0 Universal is `CC0-1.0`.

The names on SPDX already carry the chairs in ordinary words: keep the name, pass the same freedom along, do not use the work as a commercial pile, do not make a changed version. Those are labels on a list. They are not a substitute for the Legal Code.

SPDX marks some rows FSF Free/Libre. On the list this book opened, `CC-BY-4.0`, `CC-BY-SA-4.0`, and `CC0-1.0` show Y in that column. `CC-BY-NC-4.0` and `CC-BY-ND-4.0` do not show that Y. The OSI Approved column is empty on those Creative Commons 4.0 rows. Two columns. They are not the same mark. Empty is empty. Do not average them.

The GNU licenses page says the standard copyright terms for GNU web pages are now the Creative Commons Attribution-NoDerivs 4.0 International License. That is `CC-BY-ND-4.0` on SPDX. A software license family lives in the next chapter. A web page on gnu.org is a different object with a different text.

Older Creative Commons versions sit lower on the licenses list: 3.0, including country-ported texts, then 2.5, 2.1, 2.0, and 1.0. If a pamphlet says 3.0 and you apply 4.0, you opened the wrong year.

**Check.** Mara stamps `CC-BY-4.0` on the 12-page pamphlet. Len prints 48, keeps her name, and gives them away. Write whether the identifier's full name covers keep-the-name. It does: Attribution. Then he strips her name and still gives them away. He left the identifier. If you said "Creative Commons means anyone may do anything," you dropped the name chair.

New case: she stamps `CC-BY-ND-4.0`. Len rewrites 4 of the 14 tables. The identifier's full name includes No Derivatives. This book did not fetch that Legal Code. Write unverified beside the rewrite, then open the 4.0 Deed and Legal Code before you claim the 4 tables are allowed.

## 5. A copyleft family

Nora writes a 5-file program that draws stitch charts for the mill's sail loft: 9 charts, a count of repeats, a print page. She wants people to copy it, change it, and pass those freedoms along. She does not want a middle shop to take her files, change 1 file, and ship a locked pile that nobody else may change.

The GNU licenses page names that method **copyleft**. The page says copyleft is a general method for making a program free software and requiring all modified and extended versions of the program to be free software as well. It says the simple public-domain path lets uncooperative people convert the program into proprietary software. Copyleft uses copyright, then adds distribution terms so that anyone who redistributes, with or without changes, must pass along the freedom to further copy and change it. Proprietary developers, on that page, use copyright to take freedom away. Copyleft uses copyright to keep it. That is why the name reverses.

The same page names the GNU family's specific texts. The GNU General Public License is often called the GNU GPL. The latest version is version 3. The page says it is used by most GNU programs, and by more than half of all free software packages. The GNU Lesser General Public License, latest version 3, is used by a few GNU libraries, not by all of them. The GNU Free Documentation License is copyleft for a manual, textbook, or other document. Its latest version is 1.3. The page says the FDL is meant to assure everyone the effective freedom to copy and redistribute the document, with or without modifications, either commercially or non-commercially.

If you use one of those texts, the page says you must use the entire text. Partial copies are not permitted. Likewise for the LGPL, the AGPL, and the FDL. You do not rewrite the GPL to apply it. You add notices that refer to it, and you include the whole GPL.

English is the legal text. The GNU page says unofficial translations exist to help people understand, and that the FSF does not approve those translations as officially valid. If a loft copy is only a translation, open the English original before you act.

Copyleft is a family, not one file. GPL, LGPL, AGPL, and FDL fill in the details in different ways. The next chapter is the AGPL as its own door. Do not mash them.

**Check.** Nora's 5 files carry the GNU GPL, version 3, entire text in the box, notices in the files. A shop changes 1 file, ships the result, and strips both the GPL and the source. Write the copyleft promise that broke: the shop redistributed and did not pass along the freedom to copy and change. If you wrote "they charged money, so they broke it," you named a price. The GNU FDL page, on this door, allows commercial and non-commercial copies for documents. Whether the GPL text itself allows a fee is a sentence in the GPL. This book did not fetch that sentence. Mark the fee unverified. The stripped source is already a break.

New case: the shop keeps the entire GPL, keeps the notices, and offers the 5 files including the changed one. The family is intact on the facts this chapter has. Open the GPL text for any further condition. Unverified here: any extra condition not on the licenses landing page.

## 6. AGPL as a door

Floodbell is Nora's other program. Five source files. It reads the river gauge at the mill and draws a page: height, a 24-hour trace, a bell if the bar is unsafe. The mill runs it on a box in the loft. Eight people in Reedhaven open that page each morning in a browser. They never download a package. They never hold the 5 files.

The GNU GPL, on the licenses page, is the base. Network-only use is a hole the GPL family then filled with another text. The GNU Affero General Public License is based on the GNU GPL, but has an additional term to allow users who interact with the licensed software over a network to receive the source for that program. The GNU page recommends that people consider using the GNU AGPL for any software which will commonly be run over a network. The latest version is version 3.

People name that text the **GNU AGPL**, or AGPLv3 when they mean version 3. The name arrives after the eight browsers. The eight browsers are why the extra term exists.

The licenses page gives a stable URL for version 3: `https://www.gnu.org/licenses/agpl-3.0.html`. A version-less URL, `https://www.gnu.org/licenses/agpl.html`, is the page's usual pointer at the latest text. The Affero General Public License version 1, the same page says, is not a GNU license. Do not treat version 1 as GNU AGPL.

SPDX version 3.29.0 dated 2026-09-16 lists `AGPL-3.0-only` and `AGPL-3.0-or-later`. Both rows show Y under FSF Free/Libre and Y under OSI Approved. `only` pins version 3. `or later` leaves the door open if GNU later publishes a new AGPL version. The licenses page says the latest version is version 3. A version 4 is not on that page. Do not invent one.

This book did not fetch the AGPL legal text itself. Any section number, any exact sentence about a network, any claim about how many clicks a user must be offered, is unverified here. The additional term, as the GNU licenses page states it, is the object: network users may receive the source.

Floodbell labeled `AGPL-3.0-or-later` is the teaching object. The eight morning users are network users. The extra term is about them. A laptop copy of Floodbell with the network unplugged is still under a GPL-based copyleft. The extra term is idle until someone interacts over a network. Idle is not gone.

**Check.** Eight people load Floodbell in a browser. Zero of them receive a copy of the 5 files. Write whether the extra term, as gnu.org states it, cares about those eight. It does: they interact over a network, and the term is there so they may receive the source. If you wrote "they only looked, so it is not copying," you described a browser and dropped the term.

New case: the same 5 files run on a laptop in the loft, cable out, one person at the keyboard. The extra term's network chair is empty. The rest of AGPL is still a GPL-based copyleft. Open the AGPL text for what still binds that one person on a later redistribute. Unverified here: any redistribute rule not on the landing page.

## 7. A notice

A stranger who finds Floodbell needs a short line that names the permission, and a way to reach the full text. Five files with no name are a default from chapter 2 wearing a programmer's shirt.

A short, standard name for a license, tied to a published full text, is a **notice** when it is placed on the work so a stranger can find it. SPDX exists to make that name reliable. The SPDX License List is part of the SPDX Specification. The list this book opened is version 3.29.0, dated 2026-09-16. The page says the list includes a standardized short identifier, the full name, the license text, and a canonical permanent URL for each license and exception. The purpose is efficient and reliable identification in an SPDX document, in source files, or elsewhere.

`SPDX-License-Identifier: AGPL-3.0-or-later` is such a line. `SPDX-License-Identifier: CC-BY-4.0` is such a line on a pamphlet. The identifier is not the Legal Code. It is a pointer. If the pointer and a bundled full text fight, do not average them. Read both. If a court must choose, that walk belongs to the living law pack.

The GNU licenses page says you apply a GNU license by adding notices that refer properly to it, and by using the entire text. Partial copies of the GPL, LGPL, AGPL, or FDL are not permitted. A notice that says "GPL" with no version and no text is a thin pointer. SPDX distinguishes `GPL-3.0-only` from `GPL-3.0-or-later` on the same list. Thin is how two later readers pick two different grants.

A copyright line naming who and when is a different chair from a license identifier. copyright.gov's front door talks about registration and about recordation of transfers. It gives no required notice formula on the body this book opened. Unverified here: whether a year-and-name line is required, or enough by itself.

GNU's own licenses page carries a copyright line for the Free Software Foundation, with years through 2026, and says the page is licensed under Creative Commons Attribution-NoDerivatives 4.0 International. Two chairs on one page: who holds copyright, which permission is granted. Keep both visible.

**Check.** File A is 11 lines of stitch code and no identifier. File B is 11 lines and `SPDX-License-Identifier: CC-BY-4.0`. On SPDX 3.29.0, which file can a stranger match to a full name and a URL. File B. File A still sits on the default. If you matched File A to "probably MIT," you invented a pointer.

New case: a folder's identifier says `MIT` and the folder also holds a full GNU GPL, version 3. Two notices. Do not average. Write both down. Open both texts. Until a reader can see which grant the files actually use, the permission is unverified. A court fight about that folder is a living-law walk.

## 8. Source that must travel

Copyleft's point is that a later person may change the program. Change needs the form you work in, not only the form you run.

Floodbell's running page is a picture of height and a bell. The 5 files are the form Nora changes when the gauge protocol moves. If the mill offers the page and hides the 5 files, a dock worker can look and cannot fix a wrong tide mark.

The GNU AGPL's extra term, as the licenses page states it, is there so users who interact over a network may receive the source for that program. People name that traveling form **source**. On this page, source is the files a person would use to change the program, not the picture in the browser. This book did not fetch the GPL or AGPL definition of corresponding source. Any extra list of build scripts, interface files, or generated piles is unverified here. Open the AGPL text for that list.

Travel is an act. A link that opens the 5 files is travel if a stranger can fetch them. A link that returns an error is not travel. A drawer in the loft that no network user can reach is not travel for the eight browsers. A paper printout of 2 of the 5 files is a partial pile. Partial source is a failure: the freedom to change does not arrive.

Two boxes make two paths. The mill screen and a second screen at the town hall both run Floodbell. Eight users on the mill page and 3 users on the hall page are 11 network users, not 8. Each path is an interaction. Do not count only the box you can see from the loft.

**Check.** Five source files. One running page. Eight users. Write what the extra term, as gnu.org states it, says those eight may receive: the source for that program. If the mill hands them only the page, the source did not travel. If you wrote "they can view the height, so they have everything," you named the picture.

New case: Nora posts a link to a folder of the 5 files. On Monday the link works. On Thursday the folder is gone. Eight users on Thursday do not have source that traveled. Fetch again. If the folder is still gone, write failed travel, not a mood about Monday.

## 9. A patent clause as a first look

copyright.gov's intellectual-property toolkit page, on the front door this book opened, says intellectual property protects creations of the mind, and that copyright is one type. It points at other toolkits on trademarks, patents, and trade secrets from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office.

A patent is a different object from a copyright. A copyright, in this book, is the handle on who may copy a writing, a picture, a song, a chart, or a program. A patent, as a first look, is a different handle, on a different kind of claim, run by a different office. Open USPTO for that object. This book does not teach patent law. The living law pack owns jurisprudence. Name those doors and stop.

Some license texts also speak about patents. That sentence, when it exists, is not the copy permission. It is a second chair. SPDX even has identifiers whose names include the word Patent. One row on version 3.29.0 is `BSD-2-Clause-Patent`, full name BSD-2-Clause Plus Patent License, with OSI Approved marked Y. The identifier is a label. This book did not fetch that license text. Any claim about what its patent sentence does is unverified.

`Apache-2.0` on the same SPDX list is Apache License 2.0, FSF Free/Libre Y and OSI Approved Y. Talk on the dock says Apache has a patent grant. This book did not fetch the Apache text. Mark that grant unverified. Open the text if Floodbell, or a stitch file, actually carries `Apache-2.0`.

A first look is a split, not a conclusion. Chair one: who may copy, under the license you named. Chair two: whether that same text also speaks about patents. If chair two is empty, write empty. Do not fill it with "it is open, so patents cannot apply." That slogan is not on copyright.gov, not on the GNU licenses page, not on the Creative Commons list, and not on the SPDX list this book opened.

If a neighbor wants a patent fight, that is not a license-identifier walk. That is USPTO plus the living law pack.

**Check.** Write two chairs for Floodbell labeled `AGPL-3.0-or-later`. Chair one: copy permission, GNU AGPL as the GNU page describes it, including the network source term. Chair two: a patent sentence. This book did not fetch the AGPL text, so chair two is unverified, not "none" and not "yes." If you filled chair two from a slogan, empty it.

New case: a loft file labeled `BSD-2-Clause-Patent`. The identifier itself names a patent. Still open the license text before you act. The SPDX mark OSI Approved Y is a column on a list, not a court result. Unverified here: what a judge would do with that file.

## 10. A check against the license text

The object of this book is who may copy, and what a license text permits. A check is a walk from a real pile to a living text, not a memory of a talk on the dock.

Take Floodbell as it sits. Five source files. A running page on the mill box. Eight morning users in a browser. A line at the top of each file: `SPDX-License-Identifier: AGPL-3.0-or-later`. A copy of the GNU AGPL, entire text, in the box.

Walk.

Who made it. Nora. Who wants to copy or run it. The mill, the eight users, later a hall screen.

What identifier. `AGPL-3.0-or-later`. Open SPDX 3.29.0, dated 2026-09-16. Full name: GNU Affero General Public License v3.0 or later. FSF Free/Libre Y. OSI Approved Y. That is the pointer. It is not the legal text.

Open the GNU licenses page. AGPL is based on the GPL. Additional term: users who interact over a network may receive the source for that program. Latest version: 3. Recommended for software commonly run over a network. Entire text required. English original is the legal terms.

Do the eight users interact over a network. Yes. Has the mill offered them the 5 files. If yes, the extra term as gnu.org states it has a path. If no, the walk fails even if the identifier is spelled right.

Is the AGPL text entire. If the box holds a paragraph headed "GPL summary," the GNU page already said partial copies are not permitted. Fail that chair.

Now change the pile. Mara's 12-page tide pamphlet, 14 tables, stamped `CC-BY-4.0`. Do not open the AGPL. Open the Creative Commons 4.0 Deed and Legal Code, and SPDX for `CC-BY-4.0`. Attribution is in the full name. Network source is not. If you still talk about eight browsers receiving source, you applied Floodbell's text to a pamphlet.

Change the pile again. The pamphlet has no identifier and no sheet. You are on the default. copyright.gov, Title 17. Not AGPL. Not CC-BY-4.0. Finding the pamphlet is not leave.

A last number from the copyright.gov front door: the Office announced a public event, "The Copyright Act Turns 50," for Wednesday, September 23, 2026, at 1:00 P.M. eastern time. That date is a door date. Do not turn 50 into a rule about how long Mara's pamphlet lasts. Term of years remains unverified here until you open Title 17.

**Check.** Rewrite Floodbell's identifier from `AGPL-3.0-or-later` to `CC-BY-4.0` on a 12-page pamphlet that is not a network program. Write which door you open first: Creative Commons, or SPDX for `CC-BY-4.0`. Write which door you do not use for this pile: the AGPL extra term. If your answer still offers source to eight browsers, you kept the wrong text.

New case: a folder says only "please share." No SPDX line, no CC Deed, no GNU text. Run chapters 2 and 3. There is no license text to check against. The default holds. If you treated "please share" as `CC-BY-4.0`, you invented a pointer. Write the blank.
