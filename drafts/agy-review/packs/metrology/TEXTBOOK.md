# A standard other numbers hang on

You can already keep a unit on a number, name an instrument, and walk the SI as a first look. Those live in the measurement book. Name that door and stop. This book is the next act: a shop number that hangs on a chosen object, a chain of comparisons that reaches a national lab, a correction written after the compare, and a paper that says who compared what.

The SI Brochure lives at the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures. The United States hang lives at the National Institute of Standards and Technology, through the Office of Weights and Measures and through the constants pages. How the seven base units are defined is the measurement book's walk. This book owns the hang: a standard, a chain, a calibration, a leftover band of doubt, a certificate.

Shop rods, stall scales, and mill clocks below are invented piles so you can finish every sum by hand. A date, a program name, or a door title that must travel comes from BIPM or NIST, or the line says unverified.

Public general education.

---

## 1. A comparison

Ivo lays a steel rod on the mill bench. Next to it he lays a second rod that the mill already trusts as 250 millimetres. He looks at the two ends. They meet. For this job, the first rod is as long as the second.

He did not read a number off the air. He put two things beside each other and asked whether they matched. That act is a **comparison**. A measurement you already know how to name is this act with a unit written on the result. The unit lives in the measurement book. The act lives here.

A comparison can fail to match. Ivo's next pin sits beside a 180 millimetre block. The pin sticks out. He slides a 4 millimetre spacer against the block. The pin still sticks out a little. He adds a 1 millimetre leaf. Now the ends meet. The pin matches $180 + 4 + 1 = 185$ millimetres of trusted pieces, not 180.

A comparison has a direction. "The pin against the block" is not the same sentence as "the block against a national bar," even if both sentences use millimetres. Chapter 3 is that chain. Here, one pair, one question: same or not, and by how much.

A comparison also has a least count. Ivo's eye and the millimetre marks cannot settle a fight smaller than one mark. If two ends look the same at that mark, the comparison result is "match at 1 millimetre," not "the true lengths are identical forever." Chapter 5 is that leftover band.

Do not add a comparison in millimetres to a comparison in grams. Numeracy already refused to add metres to seconds. The hang does not lift that rule.

**Check.** A new pin beside a 210 millimetre block sticks out. A 12 millimetre spacer still leaves a gap. A 2 millimetre leaf closes it. The pin matches $210 + 12 + 2 = 224$ millimetres of trusted pieces. A second new case: two 90 millimetre rods laid end to end beside a 180 millimetre bar. Ends meet. $90 + 90 = 180$. If the ends do not meet, one of the three pieces is not the length you named. A third: a 40 millimetre plug in a 40 millimetre ring. The plug drops through with no catch and no rattle you can feel. That is still a comparison. The answer is "fits," which you may later write as a length with a unit. The fit came first.

## 2. A standard

Three tapes hang on the mill wall. Ivo picks tape A and declares, for this mill, that tape A's one-metre mark is the metre the mill will use this month. Tess then lays tape B beside tape A. Tape B's one-metre mark sits 2 millimetres past tape A's mark. Quin lays tape C beside tape A. The marks meet.

Tape A is not magic. It is the object the other two hang on. An object other numbers hang on is a **standard**. The mill can hang a day's work on tape A even if tape A itself still hangs on something else. Chapter 3 is that next hang.

If Quin cuts four metres of rail using tape B as if it were tape A, each metre is 2 millimetres long. Four metres of rail are $4 \times 2 = 8$ millimetres long. The rail will not meet a part cut to tape A. The fight is not "who is careful." The fight is which object the numbers hang on.

A standard can be a block, a weight, a clock, a flask, or a sentence on a national page. The shop object is a working standard: close enough for the mill, handled every day. A national lab keeps a higher hang. The SI itself is the top hang for length, mass, time, and the rest of that family. How those units are defined lives in the measurement book and on the BIPM SI Brochure door. Name that door and stop.

The NIST Metric (SI) Program page names seven defining constants and seven base units. It points at NIST Special Publication 330 and Special Publication 811 for writing with those units, and at the BIPM brochure as the international text. This book does not reprint those defining sentences. Open the brochure if you need the sentence. Open SP 330 or SP 811 if you need the writing rules.

A name without a hang is a wish. "One kilogram of flour" on a stall card is a promise that some mass standard was in the chain. Chapter 8 is that stall. Here, the lesson is smaller: pick the object, then hang the other numbers on it.

**Check.** Tape D is 3 millimetres short of tape A at one metre. Five metres cut to tape D are $5 \times 3 = 15$ millimetres short of five metres on tape A. A new case: a 25 millimetre shop block is the mill's length standard this week. A rod compared to four of those blocks plus a 5 millimetre leaf matches. The rod hangs as $4 \times 25 + 5 = 105$ millimetres on that standard. Another: two mills each pick a different tape as "the metre." Both write 1.000 metre on a drawing. The parts do not mate. The drawings used the same word. They did not use the same hang.

## 3. A chain of comparisons

Mara's 500 gram stall weight sits on Tess's lab balance next to the lab's 500 gram weight. Mara's weight reads 0.04 gram heavy against the lab weight. Last month Tess compared that lab weight to a national 500 gram. The lab weight was 0.01 gram light against the national.

Write the two hangs as sentences.

Mara's weight $=$ lab weight $+ 0.04$ gram.

Lab weight $=$ national weight $- 0.01$ gram.

Put the second into the first:

Mara's weight $=$ national weight $- 0.01 + 0.04 = $ national weight $+ 0.03$ gram.

Mara's stall weight is 0.03 gram heavy against the national, not 0.04. The 0.04 was only against Tess. Skip a link and you hang the stall on the wrong story.

A line of comparisons from the working object up to a national, and through that national to the international hang, is a **chain of comparisons**. People also call a named chain like that **traceability** when they want one word. The word can wait until the links are on the page. Each link is one comparison from chapter 1. The chain is the list of links, with signs, with dates.

The NIST Office of Weights and Measures states its mission as establishing uniformity, SI use, and traceability in weights and measures by advancing national and international legal metrology standards, procedures, capabilities, and training. That sentence is on the OWM page. This book uses it as the name of the hang the office is for. How a particular state lab is recognized this year is on that office's living pages, not in this paragraph.

A chain with a missing link is not a chain. "We compared it to a block we like" is one comparison. It is not a hang on NIST or BIPM. "The catalogue said class F" is a label. It is not a date, a result, and a next standard.

Links age. Tess's 0.01 gram result was last month. If someone drops the lab weight this week, last month's link no longer describes the object. The chain is only as fresh as its last comparison.

**Check.** A shop 2 kilogram mass is 0.12 gram heavy against a county 2 kilogram. The county mass is 0.05 gram heavy against a national 2 kilogram. Shop against national: $0.12 + 0.05 = 0.17$ gram heavy. A new case: a 300 millimetre shop rod is 0.4 millimetre long against a lab bar. The lab bar is 0.1 millimetre short against a national bar. Shop against national: $0.4 + (-0.1) = 0.3$ millimetre long. Another: you have the shop-to-lab result and no lab-to-national result. You may write the shop against the lab. You may not write the shop against the national. The missing link is a blank, not a zero.

## 4. Calibration

Quin's caliper reads 20.00 millimetres on a 20.00 millimetre standard. On a 150.00 millimetre standard it reads 150.18 millimetres. The tool is not "wrong in general." It matched at 20. It ran 0.18 millimetre long at 150.

Writing how an instrument compares to a standard, at named points, is **calibration**. You may later turn a screw so the next reading matches. That turn is an adjustment. Calibration is the writing. An adjustment without a writing is a hope. A writing without an adjustment is still a calibration. The mill can keep the 0.18 and subtract it.

At 150 millimetres, a reading $R$ on this caliper hangs as $R - 0.18$ millimetres on the standard, if the leftover at 150 stays the leftover you wrote. A later cut that reads 150.00 on the caliper is $150.00 - 0.18 = 149.82$ millimetres on the standard. If you skip the correction, you cut long.

Two points do not pin every point. Matching at 20 and running long at 150 does not tell you the leftover at 80 until you compare at 80, or until you have a named model you trust. This book does not invent that model. Compare at the points you will use, or say the leftover between them is unverified.

A scale is the same object. A 2.50 kilogram standard on Mara's stall scale reads 2.50. A 5.00 kilogram standard reads 5.04. At 5 kilograms the scale is 0.04 kilogram high. A bag that reads 5.00 on that scale is $5.00 - 0.04 = 4.96$ kilograms on the standard. Forty such bags would be $40 \times 0.04 = 1.60$ kilograms light against the standard if nobody applies the correction. Legal hang for a stall is chapter 8. The arithmetic of the correction is here.

OWM's training list includes a two-week hands-on mass metrology seminar. How to handle a particular class of weight is on that training door and on OWM publications. Unverified here as a procedure. The idea you need in this chapter is smaller: put the standard on the instrument, write the reading, keep the difference with its sign.

**Check.** A 25.00 millimetre pin standard reads 25.06 on a micrometer. Correction at 25 millimetres: subtract 0.06. A part that reads 25.00 on that micrometer is $25.00 - 0.06 = 24.94$ millimetres on the standard. A new case: a flask marked 500 millilitres, filled to the mark with water from a 500 millilitre volumetric standard, comes up 6 millilitres short of the standard's mark. The flask's 500 is 6 millilitres small. Filling it to its own mark delivers 494 millilitres on the standard: $500 - 6 = 494$. Another: a clock compared at noon to a trusted time signal is 20 seconds behind. At 15:00, three hours later, you have not yet compared again. You may not write "20 seconds behind at 15:00" unless you also know how the clock runs. Chapter 7 is that run.

## 5. Uncertainty

Tess writes that Mara's 500 gram weight is 500.03 grams on the national hang. She also writes a band: 0.02 gram. She is not saying the next digit is 0. She is saying the hang is not claimed closer than $500.03 - 0.02 = 500.01$ grams to $500.03 + 0.02 = 500.05$ grams.

A measured number with a leftover band is a number plus an **uncertainty**. The band is part of the result. Write 500.03 grams with band 0.02 gram, which is 500.01 grams to 500.05 grams. Hide the band and a later reader treats every digit of 500.03 as known.

The band is not a permission to round however you like. If two stall weights differ by 0.01 gram and each band is 0.02 gram, the two hangs overlap. You have not shown they differ. If they differ by 0.10 gram and each band is 0.02 gram, the hang is wide enough to tell them apart for this job.

How a lab builds that band, including any coverage factor, lives on NIST pages for constants, units, and uncertainty, and in lab procedures this book did not open. Unverified here as a formula. Do not finish a coverage table from memory. What you can do by hand is carry the band Tess wrote, add bands when you add hangs, and refuse a claim that is narrower than the band.

When two leftovers add, the bands travel with them. Chapter 3 had Mara 0.04 gram heavy against Tess, band 0.01 gram on that link, and Tess 0.01 gram light against the national, band 0.01 gram on that link. The combined leftover was 0.03 gram. A careful chain adds the bands as well. One honest way, if the two links are separate stories, is to add the widths: $0.01 + 0.01 = 0.02$ gram on the combined leftover. Other ways to combine bands exist. They are unverified here. If someone writes 0.03 gram heavy with no band, they hid the width.

A least count is a first band. Ivo's millimetre marks cannot claim 0.1 millimetre. A digital readout that flips by 0.01 millimetre cannot claim the next digit. Calibration leftover and least count are two reasons the band is not zero. Both stay on the page.

**Check.** A rod is 312.4 millimetres on a lab hang, band 0.3 millimetre. The hang runs from $312.4 - 0.3 = 312.1$ to $312.4 + 0.3 = 312.7$ millimetres. A drawing that says "must be 312.4 exactly" is asking for a point the lab did not sell. A new case: two pins, 18.20 millimetres and 18.28 millimetres, each with band 0.02 millimetre. First pin 18.18 to 18.22. Second pin 18.26 to 18.30. The bands do not overlap. For this band, they differ. Another: same pins, band 0.06 millimetre each. First 18.14 to 18.26. Second 18.22 to 18.34. Now they overlap. You have not shown they differ.

## 6. A certificate

Tess hands Mara a page.

| field | what it says |
|---|---|
| object | stall weight, 500 g, serial M-17 |
| result | 500.03 g |
| band | 0.02 g |
| standard used | lab mass L-4 |
| date | 4 March |
| lab | Tess's lab |

That page is a **certificate**. It is the chain of chapter 3 written so another person can read the hang without standing in the lab. A number with no page is a memory. A page with no result, no band, no standard, and no date is a letterhead.

Use the page as a hang, not as a charm. On 4 March, M-17 is 500.03 grams on Tess's hang, width 0.02 gram. On 4 September, if nobody compared M-17 again, the page is six months old. Whether six months is still good is a rule for that class of weight. OWM publications and state lab rules own that interval. Unverified here as a number of months. What you can say without a rule: an old page describes an old comparison.

A certificate can report "as found" and "as left." As found is the hang before anyone turned a screw. As left is the hang after an adjustment. If the stall scale in chapter 4 was adjusted from 5.04 down to 5.00 on a 5.00 kilogram standard, the as-found leftover was $+0.04$ kilogram and the as-left leftover was 0 at that point, plus whatever band Tess still writes. Bags sold before the adjustment still used the as-found hang. Bags sold after use the as-left hang. Mixing the two dates is two stories glued into one.

Serial numbers matter because two 500 gram weights are not one weight. M-17 is not M-18. A certificate for M-17 does not hang M-18. If the stamp on the weight and the serial on the page disagree, you do not have a hang. You have two objects.

The international writing of the SI sits on the BIPM SI Brochure door: 9th edition (2019), complete text updated in 2026, DOI 10.59161/AUEZ1291. Appendix 2 on that host is the practical realizations of important units. A shop certificate is not that brochure. The brochure is the top hang's sentence. The shop page is one working object, one date, one band.

**Check.** A tape certificate: 1 metre mark is $+0.4$ millimetre against the lab, band 0.2 millimetre, serial T-9, date 11 July. A 3 metre cut on T-9, if the leftover stays 0.4 millimetre per metre, is $3 \times 0.4 = 1.2$ millimetres long, with a band you must not shrink below the page's 0.2 millimetre per metre without a new comparison. A new case: a page for flask F-2 says 250 millilitres, leftover $-1$ millilitre, band 0.5 millilitre, date last spring, and the flask on the bench is stamped F-3. Stop. The page is not this flask. Another: as-found 2.03 kilograms on a 2 kilogram standard, as-left 2.00, same serial, same day. Sales before the adjustment used 2.03. Sales after use 2.00 plus the new band.

## 7. Time as a standard

The mill horn is supposed to blow at 12:00. Ivo's wall clock reads 12:00. Quin's watch reads 12:02. Which horn is on time depends on which clock is the standard, not on who is louder.

A clock other clocks hang on is a **time standard**. The mill can pick the wall clock for lunch. A town can pick a national time lab for trains, radios, and stamps. The SI second, and the sentence that fixes it, live in the measurement book and on the BIPM brochure. Name that door and stop. This chapter is the hang: compare two clocks, write the leftover, then ask how the leftover grows.

Ivo compares the wall clock to a trusted time signal at 08:00. The wall clock is 6 seconds behind. He compares again at 18:00, ten hours later. The wall clock is 16 seconds behind. In 10 hours the clock lost $16 - 6 = 10$ seconds. Per hour it loses $10 \div 10 = 1$ second.

That run is a calibration of rate, not only of a single instant. If the run holds, at 13:00, five hours after 08:00, the wall clock should be $6 + 5 \times 1 = 11$ seconds behind the signal. A job timed as "4 hours on the wall clock" is $4 \times 3600 = 14400$ seconds on the wall, which is 4 seconds short of 4 hours on the signal if the clock loses 1 second per hour: $4 \times 1 = 4$.

Do not invent the national time-of-day reading. NIST keeps time as part of its measurement work. The four doors opened for this book were the SI brochure, OWM, the Metric (SI) Program, and the CODATA constants page. None of those four printed a clock display. A civil time for right now is unverified here. Open a living NIST time door when you need the second on the wall.

Constants that sit under many formulas live on the NIST CODATA 2022 page, Standard Reference Database 121, last update to data content May 2024. The 2026 CODATA adjustment is the next regularly scheduled adjustment. Data for that adjustment must have been discussed in a publication or preprint before 31 December 2026. Those sentences are on the constants door. They are not a shop clock. They are the hang for numbers that must not drift between papers.

**Check.** A timer runs 3 seconds fast in 600 seconds against a trusted signal. In 1800 seconds it should run $1800 \div 600 \times 3 = 9$ seconds fast if the rate holds. A new case: a watch 20 seconds behind at 09:00 and 8 seconds behind at 13:00. Four hours passed. It gained $20 - 8 = 12$ seconds in 4 hours, which is 3 seconds per hour. Another: two mill horns, no shared clock, each set by a different phone. You can compare each phone to a named time door. Until you do, you have two horns and no hang.

## 8. Mass as a standard

Mara sells grain by the kilogram. A buyer puts a sack on the stall scale. The scale reads 2.00 kilograms. What 2.00 means is: this sack matches, on this scale, whatever mass standard the scale last hung on.

A mass other masses hang on is a **mass standard**. In the stall it is often a metal weight with a stamp. In a national lab it is a working copy that hangs on the SI kilogram. How the kilogram is defined lives in the measurement book. Name that door and stop. This chapter is the stall hang and the legal reason it exists.

OWM works to ensure that consumers get what they pay for and sellers get fair payment for the goods and services they sell, by promoting a uniform and technically sound system of weights and measures. That is the office's own sentence. A light sack sold as 2 kilograms is a broken hang. A heavy sack sold as 2 kilograms is a hang that favors the buyer. Fair here means the same standard on both sides of the counter.

Put a 2.00 kilogram test weight on Mara's scale. The scale reads 2.03. At 2 kilograms the scale is 0.03 kilogram high. A sack that reads 2.00 is $2.00 - 0.03 = 1.97$ kilograms on the test weight. Twenty such sacks are $20 \times 0.03 = 0.60$ kilogram light against the test weight. The certificate from chapter 6 is how Mara shows the test weight itself hangs on Tess, and Tess on the national.

A test weight is a working standard. It wears. It picks up dust. It chips. A comparison from last spring does not describe a chipped corner this week. The chain from chapter 3 has to include this object, this date.

Do not mix mass with weight-as-a-pull. A scale that uses a spring answers a pull. A balance that compares two pans answers a pile against a pile. For stall work, the hang you want is the mass standard on the certificate, used the way the lab used it. Procedure details for a class of weight live in OWM training. Unverified here as a class letter.

**Check.** A 1 kilogram test weight reads 0.994 kilogram on a stall scale. The scale is 0.006 kilogram low at 1 kilogram. A sack that reads 1.000 on that scale is $1.000 + 0.006 = 1.006$ kilograms on the test weight. The buyer gets a heavy sack. A new case: 15 sacks, each reading 5.00 kilograms on a scale that is 0.02 kilogram high at 5 kilograms. Each sack is 4.98 kilograms on the standard. Fifteen sacks are $15 \times 0.02 = 0.30$ kilogram light. Another: the test weight's own certificate says it is 0.01 kilogram heavy against the lab. The scale reads 2.03 on that weight. The scale against the lab is $0.03 + 0.01 = 0.04$ kilogram high at that point. Skip the weight's own leftover and you hang the stall on the wrong link.

## 9. Length as a standard

A timber yard sells posts "8 feet." Quin has a tape. Ivo has a rod the mill calls 2.000 metres. They will fight unless they hang on the same length standard.

A length other lengths hang on is a **length standard**. A shop rod, a gage block, a tape's marks, a surveyor's chain: each is an object. The SI metre is the top hang. How the metre is defined lives in the measurement book and on the BIPM brochure. Name that door and stop.

Names can split. The NIST Metric (SI) Program page states that on 1 January 2023 the U.S. survey foot was deemed obsolete and the international foot definition officially superseded the U.S. survey foot. NOAA and NIST planned that retirement. NOAA's National Geodetic Survey has long used the metre as its official unit. The same page says the international foot is based on the metre, and that all U.S. customary units are based on the International System of Units. Two different feet had been in use. One name, two hangs. The date on that page is the date the obsolete hang was retired.

This book did not open the survey-foot conversion factor. The exact number of metres in a survey foot is unverified here. What you can use is the date, the fact of two hangs, and the fact that the surviving customary hang is based on the SI. A drawing that still says "survey feet" after that date is using a retired hang unless a project rule still names it. Open the NIST survey-foot door for the factor.

Quin's tape reads 2004 millimetres against Ivo's 2.000 metre rod. The tape is 4 millimetres long on 2 metres, which is 2 millimetres long per metre. An 8 metre cut on that tape is $8 \times 2 = 16$ millimetres long against the rod. If the yard sells "2.000 m posts" cut to that tape, the posts will not match a mill that hung on the rod.

As of 16 August 2023, NIST retired the historic physics.nist.gov SI Units site. Current SI information sits on the Metric (SI) Program page. If a bookmark still points at the retired host, you are not on the living hang.

**Check.** A 1.500 metre rod against a tape reads 1497 millimetres. The tape is 3 millimetres short on 1.5 metres. Per metre that is $3 \div 1.5 = 2$ millimetres short. A 6 metre cut on that tape is $6 \times 2 = 12$ millimetres short against the rod. A new case: two prints of the same map, one in metres, one in feet, no statement of which foot. After 1 January 2023 the living customary hang is the international foot based on the metre. If the map is old, open the survey-foot door before you add the two prints. Another: a gage block set, 25 millimetres plus 8 millimetres plus 1.2 millimetres, wrung for a 34.2 millimetre job. $25 + 8 + 1.2 = 34.2$. If the ends do not meet the part, the hang is the blocks, and the part is not 34.2 on that hang.

## 10. A check against BIPM or NIST

A shop leftover you can redo by hand does not need a door. A definition, a national date, a constant, or a program name does. Walk:

1. Name the object: a rod, a weight, a clock, a flask, a tape, a scale.
2. Name the standard it hangs on, and the date of that comparison.
3. Write the leftover with its sign, and the band if you have one.
4. If the next sentence needs SI, a constant, a legal-metrology rule, or a retired unit, open the door.
5. If NIST or BIPM and a remembered line fight, keep the host. If that host is dark, mark the standard unverified.

Four official doors were opened for this book.

BIPM SI Brochure: 9th edition (2019), complete text updated in 2026, DOI 10.59161/AUEZ1291, with Appendix 2 as practical realizations of important units. The brochure PDF was not opened here. Defining sentences stay in the measurement book. Name that door and stop.

NIST Office of Weights and Measures: uniformity, SI use, and traceability; consumers get what they pay for; sellers get fair payment; training, including a two-week hands-on mass metrology seminar; a Regional Measurement Assurance Program for state legal metrology laboratories. Recognition status for a named state lab this year was not on the landing page. Unverified here.

NIST Metric (SI) Program: SI as the preferred system for U.S. trade and commerce; seven defining constants and seven base units; SP 330 and SP 811; U.S. survey foot obsolete 1 January 2023; historic SI Units site retired 16 August 2023. Conversion factors not printed on that page stay unverified.

NIST CODATA 2022 constants: Standard Reference Database 121, last update to data content May 2024, online since October 1994, next adjustment 2026 with a 31 December 2026 preprint-or-publication deadline. Individual constant values were not copied off that index. Do not finish a constant from memory.

**Check.** Someone says Mara's 0.03 gram leftover is a CODATA constant. Open the constants door. 0.03 gram is a stall hang. It is not a fundamental constant. A new case: someone writes a length in U.S. survey feet for a job started in 2024 and treats that as the living U.S. hang. Open the Metric (SI) Program page. The living date on that page is 1 January 2023 for the retirement. Another: someone quotes an SI defining sentence from memory and uses it to correct a caliper. Open the brochure. This book did not reprint that sentence. A fourth case with no door: caliper 150.18 on a 150.00 millimetre standard. Leftover $+0.18$ millimetre. $150.18 - 0.18 = 150.00$. That is taking away. If you fetched BIPM to subtract 0.18, you walked into the wrong house.
