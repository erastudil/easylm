# Heat, temperature, and a cycle

You already have a push, a mass, and a motion. Mechanics taught you work as a push through a path. This book is the next act: energy that crosses because one body is hotter than another, a mark of how hot, and a loop that comes home so a shaft can keep turning.

Measurement owns the kelvin and the sentences that fix SI temperature. Name that pack and stop. Energy owns fuel in a can and a town's grid. Name that pack and stop. This pack owns a heat engine and a first look at what cannot be undone.

## 1. Heat as energy on the move

A kettle sits on a ring. The ring is red. The water is not. Mara waits. The water is hotter than it was. No crate rolled across the floor. No sack rose onto a shelf. Energy still arrived in the kettle.

Mechanics already named work: a push that actually moves a thing through a path. The kettle did a different job. Energy crossed because one body was hotter than the other. That crossing is **heat**.

Heat is the walk, not the pile sitting in the water, and not the number on the wall thermometer. The pile the water holds can rise because heat arrived. The pile can also rise because someone stirred hard, which is work. Same pile, two doors in.

A steady flame under three equal pots of the same water. One pot sits 5 minutes, one sits 10, one sits 15. The 10 minute pot received twice the time of the 5 minute pot. If the flame held still, it received twice the heat. The 15 minute pot received three times the 5 minute pot: $15 \div 5 = 3$. The thermometer on the water does not have to read three times as high. Heat added is not a percent of the temperature mark.

Toy units for this bench, not a calorimeter reading. 2400 units leave the flame toward the kettle. 1650 stay in the water. 750 leave into the air and the kettle wall. $1650 + 750 = 2400$. The 1650 changed the water. The 750 still exist. They are heat that missed the water.

Heat on its own walks from hotter toward less hot. A red ring warms a cool kettle. A cool kettle does not, on its own, make a red ring. You can pump heat the other way only with a machine that spends something else. A refrigerator is that machine. The spend is work, or a plug. Chapter 9 returns to a machine that spends.

The SI unit of this pile, and of work, is the joule. How the joule is fixed lives in the measurement pack and on the BIPM brochure door. Unverified here. Do not finish that sentence from memory. This chapter counts toy units you can add.

**Check.** Two pans, same water, same flame. One for 4 minutes, one for 12 minutes. The second received three times the time, so three times the heat if the flame held. A new case: 1800 units toward a pot. 1100 stay in the water. Leftover $1800 - 1100 = 700$ into air and metal. Put 1100 and 700 back: 1800. Another: a crate pushed 3 metres by a 5 newton force. Mechanics already named that 15 units of work. If you added those 15 to a temperature mark of 19 and called 34 a heat total, you mixed a push-through-a-path with a stick of hotness. Stop.

## 2. Temperature

Tess holds two cups of tea. She has no thermometer yet. She touches the first cup, then the second. One bites her fingers more. That bite is how hot, not how much energy crossed the table.

A number on an agreed stick for that bite is **temperature**. Two rooms at 19 and 26, both on the same scale, differ by 7 of that scale: $26 - 19 = 7$. The 7 is a gap on a mark. It is not a count of heat. Do not add a temperature to a joule. The two counts do not share a stick.

A rise of 8 from 14 lands on 22. A fall of 11 from 30 lands on 19. Same stick, ordinary taking away. Fill a letter. Let $t$ be the mark. $t + 8 = 22$ unwraps to $t = 14$. Put 14 back: $14 + 8 = 22$. The letter was the old mark. The 8 was the rise.

Do not add a Celsius reading to a Fahrenheit reading. 18 on one scale plus 64 on the other is not 82 of anything. Convert first, using the factor on the NIST temperature door, or refuse the sum. Measurement owns that conversion and the kelvin. Name those doors and stop.

A freezer dial at $-16$ on a Celsius kitchen scale and a room at 21 on that same scale: the gap is $21 - (-16) = 37$ degrees of that scale. Negative is a legal reading. It means below the zero that scale chose, not "no temperature."

How the kelvin is fixed, and the exact link between a Celsius reading and a kelvin reading, live on the BIPM brochure door and on NIST SI units temperature. The brochure PDF was not opened. Those sentences are unverified here. Do not finish 273 from memory.

Water's freeze and boil marks on a given scale are load-bearing if you use them as fixed points. Fetch NIST or BIPM for the living statements. Unverified here as exact official figures. A kitchen thermometer may print those marks. Printing is not the brochure.

**Check.** 13 on a scale, then a rise of 9 on that same scale, lands on 22. A new case: $t - 6 = 17$. Add 6 to both sides: $t = 23$. Put 23 back: $23 - 6 = 17$. Another: a freezer at $-16$ and a porch at 11, both on the same Celsius dial, differ by $11 - (-16) = 27$ degrees of that scale. A fight: 18 °C plus 64 °F. Stop. Two sticks. Open the measurement pack if you must name one room in both writings.

## 3. Pressure and volume

A bicycle pump has a handle, a barrel, and a hose. Cal pushes the handle. The air in the barrel has less room. The hose hisses harder into the tyre. Two objects changed together: how much space the air had, and how hard the air pushed on the walls.

How much space a sample takes is **volume**. How hard the sample pushes on a face, per unit of that face, is **pressure**. Mechanics already gave you force. Pressure is that force shared over the area it acts on.

Write $P = F / A$. Fill $F = 12$ newtons and $A = 0.03$ square metres. Then $P = 12 \div 0.03 = 400$ of those pressure units. Same force on a larger face: $A = 0.06$. Then $P = 12 \div 0.06 = 200$. Twice the area, half the pressure, same push. Undo: $P A = F$. $400 \times 0.03 = 12$. The 12 is the force you started with.

Volume of a piston barrel is the face area times the length the face can travel. $A = 0.03$ square metres, length $0.20$ metres: $V = 0.03 \times 0.20 = 0.006$ cubic metres. Push the handle so the length becomes $0.12$ metres: $V = 0.03 \times 0.12 = 0.0036$ cubic metres. The air still in the barrel now has $0.006 - 0.0036 = 0.0024$ cubic metres less room.

A tyre that reads 220 on a gauge, then 180 after a night, fell by 40 of that gauge. The 40 is a pressure change. Volume stayed unnamed. Temperature stayed unnamed. Name which reading you hold.

SI names for the pascal, the cubic metre, and how those units are fixed live on BIPM and NIST. Unverified here as defining sentences. The algebra $P = F / A$ still holds in any honest pair of force and area units, as long as you do not mix sticks in one line.

**Check.** $F = 9$ newtons on $A = 0.015$ square metres. $P = 9 \div 0.015 = 600$. A new case: the same 9 newtons on $0.045$ square metres. $P = 200$. Three times the area, one third the pressure. Another: a barrel face $0.04$ square metres, length $0.25$ metres. $V = 0.010$ cubic metres. Shorten the length to $0.10$ metres: $V = 0.004$ cubic metres. Lost volume $0.006$ cubic metres. If you added the 600 pressure to the 0.010 volume and wrote 600.010, you added two different objects.

## 4. A model of a gas

The barrel holds a puff of air you cannot see as grains. The puff still pushes. A useful picture: many small pieces, each in motion, each hitting the wall. A hotter puff means those pieces move harder. A tighter barrel means they hit more often.

People name that picture a **model of a gas**. The model is not the air. It is a rule that tracks three readings at once: pressure, volume, and an absolute temperature mark.

Measurement owns the living absolute stick, the kelvin. Name that door and stop. In this flask Tess writes $T$ for an invented absolute mark, a letter in the same family, not a brochure number. She also writes $P$ and $V$. For a fixed amount of this puff, the model says the share $P V / T$ stays one number.

Invented for this flask: that number is 96.

At $T = 6$, $V = 8$, the rule unwraps to $P = 96 \times T / V = 96 \times 6 / 8 = 72$. Put-back: $72 \times 8 / 6 = 96$. True.

Hold $T = 6$ and stretch $V$ from 8 to 12. Then $P = 96 \times 6 / 12 = 48$. Twice the volume, half the pressure, same hotness, same amount of puff. The hits spread over more wall-time.

Hold $V = 8$ and raise $T$ from 6 to 10. Then $P = 96 \times 10 / 8 = 120$. The pieces hit harder. Pressure rose. $120 / 72 = 10 / 6$, same ratio as the two $T$ marks.

A real sample is not this flask. The NIST Chemistry WebBook keeps thermophysical property data for 74 fluids: density, heat capacity at constant pressure, heat capacity at constant volume, enthalpy, internal energy, entropy, viscosity, thermal conductivity, and more. Fetch a fluid page when a density or a heat capacity must travel. Unverified here as digits. Do not finish a gas constant from memory.

If $P V / T$ fails a put-back on a real cylinder, the model is the thing that broke, or the amount of puff changed, or the marks were mixed. The WebBook is then the door, not a second memory of 96.

**Check.** Same 96. $T = 8$, $V = 4$. $P = 96 \times 8 / 4 = 192$. Put-back: $192 \times 4 / 8 = 96$. A new case: hold $P = 72$ and $T = 6$, find $V$. $V = 96 \times T / P = 96 \times 6 / 72 = 8$. That is the first fill, arrived at from another side. Another: someone quotes a universal gas constant from a poster and writes it into a lab report. Open NIST constants, or the WebBook, or write unverified. This flask's 96 is not that constant.

## 5. Work from heat

The same puff can lift a plate. Heat the barrel while a weight sits on the plate. The puff wants more room. The plate rises. A mass went up a path. Mechanics already named that work.

At constant pressure the face area does not change, and the force on the plate holds still. Work on the plate is force times path: $W = F d$. Pressure is $F / A$, and the extra volume is $A d$. So $W = P \Delta V$. The two writings are one object.

Invented: $P = 400$ of the flask's pressure units, $V$ from $0.002$ to $0.007$. Then $\Delta V = 0.005$, and $W = 400 \times 0.005 = 2$ of the flask's work units. Undo: $2 \div 0.005 = 400$, the pressure you started with.

If the plate does not move, $\Delta V = 0$, and this work is 0. Heat can still enter. The pile inside the puff then rises with nowhere to lift. If the plate falls while heat leaves, the surroundings did work on the puff. Sign is a choice you must name: work the puff does on the plate, or work done on the puff. This book counts work the puff does as positive when the plate rises.

A letter walk. $W = P (V_2 - V_1)$. Fill $P = 250$, $V_1 = 0.010$, $V_2 = 0.018$. Then $V_2 - V_1 = 0.008$ and $W = 2$. Same $P$, $V_2 = 0.010$: $W = 0$. The heat you paid did not become a lift.

Heat in can become this work, leftover pile in the puff, or leftover heat dumped to the room. Chapter 6 puts those legs in a loop so the puff itself comes home.

**Check.** $P = 180$, $V$ from $0.004$ to $0.014$. $\Delta V = 0.010$. $W = 1.8$. A new case: $W = P \Delta V = 6$ and $P = 300$. Then $\Delta V = 6 \div 300 = 0.020$. Another: a locked plate, heat in, $V$ fixed. Work from this expansion formula is 0. If you billed that heat as work on the plate, you named a crossing as a lift.

## 6. A cycle

The plate rose. Cal wants it to rise again. If he leaves the plate high, the next heat has nowhere cheap to push. He must get the puff back to the start: same $P$, same $V$, same $T$, same amount of stuff. A closed loop on those marks is a **cycle**.

A cycle can still do net work. The puff returns. The weight on the plate, or a shaft that clicked, need not return. That leftover lift is the point of an engine in chapter 9.

Toy loop, invented, four legs on $P$ and $V$.

1. Expand at $P = 300$. $V$ from 2 to 5. Work out $W = 300 \times 3 = 900$.
2. Cool at $V = 5$. $P$ from 300 to 180. The plate does not move. Work 0.
3. Compress at $P = 180$. $V$ from 5 to 2. Work in $W = 180 \times 3 = 540$.
4. Heat at $V = 2$. $P$ from 180 to 300. Work 0.

Net work of the loop: $900 - 540 = 360$. The puff is back at $P = 300$, $V = 2$. The 360 did not vanish into the puff. It left as a lift, a turn, or a raised weight.

Heat must match that arithmetic if energy is to close. Invented close for this loop: heat in 800, net work 360, heat out 440. Check: $360 + 440 = 800$. If a page claims heat in 800, work 360, heat out 0, the 440 is missing. The loop cannot hide it in the puff, because the puff came home.

Algebra of the close: $Q_\text{in} = W + Q_\text{out}$ for a cycle, with $W$ the net work. Unwrap $W = Q_\text{in} - Q_\text{out}$. Fill $Q_\text{in} = 800$, $Q_\text{out} = 440$: $W = 360$. Same 360 as the $P V$ legs. Two paths, one number. If they disagree, a leg was dropped or a sign flipped.

**Check.** Expand at $P = 220$, $V$ from 1 to 4. Work out $220 \times 3 = 660$. Compress at $P = 100$, $V$ from 4 to 1. Work in $100 \times 3 = 300$. Net $660 - 300 = 360$. Same net as the engine above, new pressures. A new case: $Q_\text{in} = 50$, $Q_\text{out} = 33$. $W = 17$. Put-back: $17 + 33 = 50$. Another: a loop that returns $P$, $V$, and $T$, and claims net work 17 with heat in 17 and heat out 0. The close fails unless 0 dump is real. Chapter 7 says why a heat engine cannot live on that claim.

## 7. What cannot be undone

A cup falls and breaks. The film does not run backward. Shards do not leap into a cup. The energy is still in the room, as sound that died into warmth, as a slightly hotter floor. You cannot spend that warmth, on its own, to rebuild the cup.

Heat does the same one-way walk. A kettle cools. The kitchen warms a little. The kitchen does not, on its own, gather that warmth back into the kettle. You can force a reverse with a machine that spends work. The spend is the point. The reverse is not free.

That one-way character, counted, is the first look at **entropy**. This pack owns this walk. Later courses write a formula with heat and an absolute temperature. That formula is unverified here. Do not finish $Q / T$ from memory. OpenStax *University Physics Volume 2* is the school door. The WebBook lists entropy among thermochemical data for over 7000 compounds. Fetch a compound page for a number.

A picture that stays in this paragraph: ink in a glass of water spreads. Stirring more does not unspread it. Entropy in this first look is that spread, written as a count that does not run backward on its own.

Toy close from chapter 6 again. Heat in 800. Work 360. Heat out 440. The 440 into a wide river is a spread. You do not get 800 of work from 800 of heat by wishing the 440 back into the boiler. A claim that a cycle takes 20 of heat and puts out 20 of work with no dump fights this chapter. Stop. Either the dump was hidden, or the working stuff did not come home, or the page is false.

A refrigerator spends work to pump heat from cold to hot. The kitchen behind the fridge warms. The spend is how you buy a reverse that nature will not give you free. Entropy still does not run backward. You paid.

**Check.** Heat in 90, work 28, dump $90 - 28 = 62$. The 62 is the one-way leftover for this loop. A new case: a page says dump 0, work 90, heat in 90, puff back at the start. Refuse the page. Another: 14 units of heat leave a kettle into a room. To put those 14 back in the kettle you need a machine and a spend, not a wait. If you wrote that the 14 vanished, you lost the pile. If you wrote that they will climb back at dawn, you ran the film backward.

## 8. Equilibrium

Two irons sit on a bench, faces touching. One reads 31 on a stick. The other reads 19 on the same stick. Wait. Heat crosses the face. Later both read 25. Invented marks, not a measured pair of irons. The crossing stopped by itself.

When two bodies in contact no longer exchange heat on their own, they are in **thermal equilibrium**. The marks match on one honest stick. Matching is the reading. Equilibrium is the stop.

A third iron reads 25 on that stick. Touch it to the first. No new walk of heat, if the marks were true. If A matches B, and B matches C, then A matches C. People name that the **zeroth law** of thermodynamics, after the picture. It is why a thermometer is legal: the stick comes to equilibrium with the tea, and then with your eye's reading of the stick.

Equilibrium is not "everything still." A room at one mark can still have air moving. The thermodynamic stop in this chapter is the heat stop: no net crossing from hot to cold inside the pair you named. A piston held by a pin is a mechanical stop, a different object. Name which stop you mean.

Two flasks, a tap closed between them. Left $P = 72$, right $P = 48$, same $T$, same model as chapter 4. Open the tap. The puff shares the two volumes. If the volumes were equal, the new $P$ is the shared value $(72 + 48) / 2 = 60$. Invented equal-volume case. A real pair of tanks needs the WebBook or a measured $V$, not this 60.

**Check.** Marks 28 and 16, same stick, then both 22. Gap closed: $28 - 16 = 12$, and each moved 6 if they met in the middle of this toy. A new case: A matches B at 19. B matches C at 19. A with C should match at 19. If a page says A reads 19 and C reads 24 after that chain, a stick was swapped or a contact was false. Another: two equal volumes, $P = 90$ and $P = 30$, tap open, same $T$. Shared $P = 60$. Put-back: $60 + 60 = 120$, and $90 + 30 = 120$. If you averaged 90 with 30 and then added a temperature of 20, you mixed a pressure share with a hotness mark.

## 9. An engine

A lamp burns under a small boiler. Steam pushes a piston. A crank turns a pinwheel. Steam then meets a cool can and collapses. The piston comes home. The pinwheel is still turning. Heat went in at the lamp. Work left on the shaft. Heat left into the can.

A machine that takes heat from a hot place, dumps leftover heat to a cold place, and delivers work from a cycle is a **heat engine**. This pack owns that walk. Energy owns fuel in a can and a town's grid. Name that pack and stop. The lamp in this engine is a hot source, not a fuel-economy table.

Let $Q_\text{h}$ be heat in from the hot place, $Q_\text{c}$ heat dumped to the cold place, $W$ the net work of the cycle. For a puff that comes home, $W = Q_\text{h} - Q_\text{c}$. The useful share is $W / Q_\text{h}$. People name that share the **efficiency**.

Invented engine: $Q_\text{h} = 80$, $Q_\text{c} = 52$, $W = 28$. Check: $28 + 52 = 80$. Efficiency $28 / 80 = 0.35$. Per hundred, 35 of each 100 that entered became work, and 65 were dumped. Undo: $0.35 \times 80 = 28$.

Fill letters. $W = Q_\text{h} - Q_\text{c}$. $e = W / Q_\text{h}$. Then $e = 1 - Q_\text{c} / Q_\text{h}$. Fill $Q_\text{c} = 52$, $Q_\text{h} = 80$: $Q_\text{c} / Q_\text{h} = 0.65$, and $e = 1 - 0.65 = 0.35$. Same 0.35.

Efficiency 1 would mean $Q_\text{c} = 0$. Chapter 7 refused that for a heat engine that must dump in order to come home. Efficiency 0 means $W = 0$: heat in equals heat out, a loop that warms a river and lifts nothing.

A hotter hot place and a colder dump raise the ceiling on $e$. The living formula in absolute temperature lives at OpenStax *University Physics Volume 2*. Unverified here as a formula. Do not finish $1 - T_\text{c} / T_\text{h}$ from memory, and do not treat the invented $T$ ticks of chapter 4 as kelvin.

A refrigerator is this machine run as a pump: work in, heat lifted from cold to hot. A heat engine is work out. Same family of cycle, opposite job.

**Check.** $Q_\text{h} = 60$, $W = 21$. Dump $60 - 21 = 39$. Efficiency $21 / 60 = 0.35$. Same 0.35 as the engine above, new size. A new case: $e = 0.20$, $Q_\text{h} = 45$. $W = 9$. Dump 36. Put-back: $9 + 36 = 45$. Another: a poster says this engine turns 80 of heat into 80 of work. Refuse it. Open the cycle close. If a page quotes a Carnot ceiling from a remembered kelvin pair, open OpenStax Volume 2 and the measurement kelvin door, or write unverified.
