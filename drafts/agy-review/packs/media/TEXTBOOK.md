# A report, an ad, and a feed

You can already keep a page and read a table. This book is the next object: a page that arrived as news, as a paid pitch, or as a list mixed by someone else.

The object is one working item in front of you. Not a mood about "the media." Not a slogan about truth. A report of an event, an ad that wants an act, or a feed item that showed up in an order you did not pick.

EasyLM is public general education. These pages are textbooks plus official doors. The app's license is AGPL-3.0-or-later. A number that must travel comes from a host that owns the fact, or the line says unverified.

This pack starts at read. It names study and data as prior books. Study taught you to keep a page. Data taught you a table, a rate, and a claim about a group. You will use both.

---

## 1. A report

Someone is telling you that a thing happened. A river rose. A bridge closed. A vote was counted. The telling sits on a page, a screen, a radio, or a paper you can hold.

Before any name, look at the telling as a pile of sentences. One sentence tries to be the news: what happened, who it happened to, when, and where. The rest of the page may explain, quote, or sell.

Walk outside after rain. The street is wet. That wet street is the event. A neighbor later says "Mill Street closed four nights after the river rose." That saying is not the street. It is a later pile of words about the street.

That telling of an event, offered as a public fact, is a **report**.

A report is not the event. Two reports can describe the same river and disagree about the hour, the street, or the count of houses. The river did what the river did either way.

A report has a maker. A person wrote it, or a program assembled it, under a name you can sometimes see: a paper, a station, a site, a newsletter. The name of the maker is not the name of the event.

Study already taught you to write a question at the top of the paper. For a report the first question is: what event is this claiming. If you cannot name the event in one short sentence, you have a mood, not a report.

A report you can keep names four chairs: what, who, when, where. A chair can be empty. Empty is not filled. Write the empty chair so you can see it. Chapter 8 treats holes. Here the job is to see which chairs are present.

I asked the National Archives front door this sitting. The host answered as National Archives. I did not get a record body I could quote. So I will not print a remembered date of a flood, a war, or a law from that shelf. Open the door in `LINK_INDEX.md` when a report about a US public record must travel. If the door will not open, write unverified.

**Check.** A page says "Mill Street closed four nights after the river rose." Write the event: Mill Street closed. Write the time: four nights. Write the place: Mill Street. Write the claimed cause: the river rose. Now a new case: "Chaos on Mill Street." That sentence names a mood. It does not name the close, the nights, or the river. It is not yet a report you can keep.

New case: "The river rose." That names an event and leaves time and place blank. It is a report with holes. Write the holes on the paper. Do not fill them from a feeling about rain.

## 2. Who paid

A page costs money to make and to send. Someone paid. That someone wants something: a true public record, a sale, a vote, a click, a gift, or a mix.

Look for the payer the way you look for a name on a shop. A town minutes page may be paid by the town. A shop flyer is paid by the shop. A video that is free to you was paid by someone else, often a mix of ads and owners.

The payer is not always the writer. A writer can be hired. A station can be owned. An ad can sit next to a report. The person who paid for the ad did not have to pay for the report, and the other way around.

People name this **funding**, **ownership**, or **sponsorship** when they want a handle. The object is simpler: who paid, and what they get if you believe the page.

A payer does not automatically make the sentences false. A town can tell the truth about a road close. A shop can tell the truth about a price. A payer does make a reason. Write the reason next to the claim.

A "free" page is not a page with no payer. It is a page whose payer is not you, at the moment you read. You may pay later with a purchase, an account, or a long look that a ranking can sell. The look is still a payment of attention. Name who wanted that look.

I asked the FCC front door this sitting and was refused. I will not print a remembered rule, fine, or license fee as law. The FCC door is in `LINK_INDEX.md`. Open it for who may broadcast and what an ad on air has to say about who paid. If it will not open, write unverified.

**Check.** A bakery prints "Two loaves for 9, this week only." The payer is the bakery. What they get if you believe it: you walk in with money. A new case: a town page says "Mill Street closed four nights." The payer, if the page is the town's own board, is the town. What they get: you do not drive that street. Two payers, two wants. Neither sentence is a proof. Both name a want you can write down.

New case: a video with no price to you, then a 30-second pitch for shoes, then a report about the ferry. Three pieces. Who paid for the pitch is the shoe seller. Who paid for the report is still a separate question. If you cannot name either payer, write "payer unnamed" and do not treat unnamed as "no one."

## 3. A picture with a caption

A picture is one look at one moment. It does not include the second before or the street behind the camera. Words under it tell you what to see. Those words are a **caption**.

The picture and the caption are two objects. They can agree. They can pull.

Stand in a shed with 14 people and a lot of empty floor. A wide photograph shows a small group in a large room. A tight photograph of 14 shoulders can look like a crush. The bits of the picture did not have to lie. The edge of the frame did the work.

A caption that names what you can check in the frame is doing work: "Ferry dock, 14 people in line, Tuesday 8 morning." A caption that names a feeling is doing a different job: "Record crush at the dock." If you can count 14 heads, "record" is a claim about other days you cannot see.

Cropping is a choice. So is the moment. A line of 14 at 8 morning can be a line of 2 at 9. One frame cannot tell you which is typical. Data already taught you that a sample is not the lake. A photograph is a sample of one instant.

A health picture is a special case. MedlinePlus, fetched for this book, is an online health information resource for patients and their families and friends. It is a service of the National Library of Medicine, which that page calls the world's largest medical library, part of the National Institutes of Health. A picture of a scan is not a diagnosis you should write for yourself from a feed. MedlinePlus's own featured topic on that fetch, diagnostic imaging, says those tests create pictures of the inside of the body and help providers understand health problems and make decisions about care. The picture is a tool for a provider. The caption on a feed is not that provider.

**Check.** You are handed a photograph of a hall. You count 11 empty seats in the front row of 12. The caption says "Packed house." Write two sentences: what you can count, and what the caption added. The added part is "packed," which your count does not support for that row.

New case: the same photo, caption "Front row, 11 of 12 seats empty, 19 May rehearsal." That caption is a report about the frame. Keep the 11 of 12. The word "rehearsal" is still a claim about time you cannot see in the chairs. Mark it. If the caption had said 3 empty of 12 and you counted 11 empty, believe the count you can do, and mark the caption.

## 4. An ad

Some pages are not trying to tell you what happened. They are trying to get you to do an act: buy, click, vote, donate, download, show up.

That paid pitch is an **ad**.

An ad can tell a true fact and still be an ad. "Two loaves for 9, this week" can be true at the counter. The test is the want. If the page is there so you will act, it is an ad even if a report sits next to it.

Ads borrow the clothes of reports. A column that looks like news, with a small word "paid" or "sponsored" in the corner, is still an ad. The clothes are a choice. Read the want, not the font.

A shop window is an old ad. You can walk past it. A screen ad can follow you into the next page. The following does not make the sentence true. It makes the want harder to leave. Name the want anyway.

Health ads are a place people get hurt. A bottle, a smile, a number, a "doctor." Walk the load-bearing health claim to MedlinePlus, the patient door fetched for this book. That front door points at health topics, a medical encyclopedia, genetics, drugs and supplements, and medical tests. It also points at easy-to-read pages and pages in more than one language. I did not fetch a drug monograph body this sitting, so I will not print a dose, a side effect, or a cure. Open the door. If the ad's sentence does not appear there, the ad is not your source.

ClinicalTrials.gov sits on the same MedlinePlus front door as a related host for registered trials. I did not fetch that trials page this sitting. Any "in a study of 12 people" on an ad is unverified here until you open a door that owns the study.

**Check.** An ad says "Walk 20 minutes, feel new." The want: you buy the shoes in the picture. The number 20 is a time you can test. The words "feel new" are not a number. A new case: "Nine of ten dentists." Write the whole: ten dentists, of which dentists, paid by whom. If the ad will not name the ten, you have a slogan. You do not have a table. Data already taught you that a count without a whole is not a claim you can keep.

New case: "Sleep 8 hours with our tea." 8 hours is a time. Tea is a product. MedlinePlus is the door for whether a health claim about sleep and a supplement has a page you can read. I did not open a tea page this sitting. Write unverified next to the sleep claim until you do. Do not finish the sentence from a memory of a cure.

## 5. A feed

Open a screen that keeps handing you the next item. A joke, a report, an ad, a friend, a song. You did not set that order with a list you can see. Something else ranked the pile.

That incoming ranked list is a **feed**.

A newspaper front page was already a ranking: editors put one story above another. A feed does that all day, often by a rule that watches what you linger on. Linger on jokes, get more jokes. Linger on fires, get more fires. The river on Mill Street may have risen either way. The feed is not the river.

Think of a conveyor in a shop. You did not load it. You still take what stops in front of you. If the conveyor is full of sweets, you can believe sweets are what the shop is. The back room can still hold bread. A feed is that conveyor. The town can still have a ferry notice you never saw.

A feed mixes kinds. If you cannot say which item is a report, which is an ad, and which is a friend's joke, the mix is doing work on you. Name the kind before you keep the claim.

A feed is also a sample in the data sense. You are not seeing every public sentence about the event. You are seeing the ones the ranking sent you. A sample from a ranking is not a sample of the lake. Data already warned you about dock fish.

I did not fetch a company's ranking paper this sitting. Treat any remembered "the algorithm does X" as unverified. The object you have without that paper is the list in front of you and the fact that its order is a choice.

**Check.** Write down the first five items in a feed you actually use, in order. Mark each as report, ad, joke, or other. Count how many of each. A new case: the same five, now ask which ones name an event you could walk to a door. If zero of five can walk, you were fed a mood. If two of five can walk, do those two. Do not average the five.

New case: the same feed tomorrow morning, first five again. If the five have changed and the town has not, you have measured the conveyor, not the town. Write both lists. The difference is the ranking at work, not proof that Mill Street reopened.

## 6. A source you can name

A claim wants a name behind it. Not a vibe. A name you could write on paper and find again: a person, an office, a paper, a host.

That named origin is a **source**.

"People say" is not a source. "Scientists say" is not a source. "A senior official" is a source with the name removed. "The town clerk's minutes, 19 May" is a source. "MedlinePlus health topics" is a source. "The National Archives" is a source. "A clip a friend sent" is a source of a copy, not of the event.

A source you can name is still not automatically true. It is a door you can knock on. Study taught you that a load-bearing number goes to a host that owns the fact. This chapter is that move for news.

Two kinds of name matter. The first is who spoke. The second is who published. A clerk can speak, and a paper can publish the speaking. If you only have the paper, you have the publisher. If you only have "someone at town hall," you have neither.

A phone, a site, a chat, a video app: each is a pipe, a way a copy moved. Naming the pipe tells you how you saw it. The source is still the speaker or the publisher.

I asked the Library of Congress newspapers door this sitting and did not get a page I could read. I will not quote a remembered first-issue date or a count of digitized pages. That door is in `LINK_INDEX.md`. It is one place a stranger can go to see newspapers as records, not as a feed. If it will not open, write unverified and use a paper you can actually hold.

C-SPAN is listed in this pack as a door to public proceedings you can watch. I did not fetch that host this sitting. Do not invent a date, a vote count, or a quote from memory of a hearing. Open the door, or write unverified.

GovInfo is listed as a door to official US publications. I did not fetch that host this sitting either. A report that says "the government says" without a document you can name has not yet given you a source.

**Check.** Take one sentence from a report you saw today. Write the source as a name you could search. If you wrote the name of an app or "a video," you named a pipe. Try again until you have a speaker or a publisher.

New case: "Clinic waits are down, a nurse said." The speaker is a nurse with no name. The publisher is whoever put it on the page. You cannot knock on "a nurse." Mark the sentence as unnamed. Then write the sentence you would need: a named person, a place, a day, and a count with a whole. If the page will not give those, you are done. You do not invent a nurse to finish the story.

## 7. A number in a headline

Headlines love short numbers. "Down 40." "Wait cut in half." "Record year." A number in a title is still a number. It still needs a count, a whole, and a time. Data taught you that. This chapter is that law on a shouting line.

"Down 40" might mean 40 people, 40 of 100, or 40 events. Those are not the same pile. Write the unit.

"Cut in half" needs the old number and the new number, from the same whole, in the same kind of time. Half of 40 minutes is 20 minutes. Half of 8 people is 4 people. If the headline halves a time and you hear a count, the unit slid.

"Record" is a comparison to every past case the writer is willing to count. You need that past, or "record" is a feeling.

Percents are rates per hundred. They still need the whole. A jump from 2 of 8 to 3 of 8 is a jump of 1 person. It is also 25 of 100 becoming 38 of 100 if you round the second. The person who wants a panic will print the percent. The person who wants a shrug will print the 1. Both can be arithmetic. Neither is the whole story unless the whole is named.

A headline can also hide the time. "This year" against "last month" is two different lengths. A count of 12 in 30 days is not the same rate as a count of 12 in 365 days. Write the window.

Do not use a headline percent as a door. Walk it to the table. Census numbers about people and housing in the United States live at the Census doors in `LINK_INDEX.md`. I did not fetch those inner tables this sitting. Any population, any town count, any "the country is N" is unverified here. Open the door for the row.

**Check.** Headline: "Support soars." Body: last week 7 of 10 said yes, this week 8 of 10 said yes. The count rose by 1 of 10. Write that. The word "soars" is not in the table.

New case: "Crime down 40%." Old pile: 10 reports in 30 days in one town. New pile: 6 reports in 30 days in the same town. The drop is 4 of 10, which is 40 of 100. Another new case, smaller pile: 5 reports down to 3 is also 40 of 100, and it is a drop of 2 events. Same percent, different count. Keep both numbers on the paper. If a headline gives you only the 40, you do not yet have the story.

## 8. What was left out

A report is a small pile taken from a larger day. The maker chose what to put in. The rest is still real. It just is not on the page.

Ask, after you can name the event: what would I have to add to make this a different story. A close of Mill Street for four nights that leaves out the detour is a different page from one that prints the detour. A clinic story that prints "faster care" and leaves out that the sample was 8 people on a Tuesday morning is a different page from one that prints the 8.

Leaving out is not always a lie. A page has a length. A feed has a slot. The skill is to see the hole and not fill it with a guess.

Three common holes: time, whole, and other side. Time: when, and compared to when. Whole: of how many. Other side: who disagrees, and on what number.

A picture leaves out everything outside the frame. A quote leaves out the sentence before it. A share, in the next chapter, can leave out the date.

Silence is a fact you can write. "The page does not say who paid" is a complete sentence. "So it must be independent" is not. You added a guess to a hole.

A health page that names a benefit and leaves out who should not take the thing has a hole you can walk to MedlinePlus. That host, fetched for this book, keeps drugs and supplements as their own door from the front page. I did not fetch a label this sitting. The move is still the same: write the missing chair, then open the door, or write unverified. Do not complete a dose from a memory of an ad.

**Check.** A page says "Ferry line gone in 12 minutes." Write three holes: 12 minutes starting when, of how many boats, compared to which other day.

New case: you add "Tuesday, 8 morning, 14 people, last Tuesday 22 minutes." The story changed. 12 against 22 is a drop of 10 minutes for that morning sample. If you cannot add those, you do not have them. Do not invent 22 to make the 12 look good. A third case: the same 12 minutes, 2 people in line. The time is the same. The whole is not. Write both.

## 9. Share and copy

You tap share. A second pile is born. The computers pack already taught this for files: copy writes a second pile. Change the second, the first may stay. News works the same way.

A **share** is a copy you send. A **screenshot** is a copy of what the screen showed, including whatever the crop kept. A **repost** is a copy under a new name. None of those is the event. None of those is the original file unless the tool moved it, and tools almost never move the original.

Each copy can drop a piece. The date falls off. The caption is rewritten. The link to the source is gone. The ad is gone and the report remains, or the other way around. What you send is what you had on the screen, not what the host still has.

A photocopy of a ticket is not the trip. A screenshot of a headline is not the report. If you would not keep the photocopy as proof of a train, do not keep the screenshot as proof of a river.

Internet Archive is listed in this pack as a door that keeps copies of pages as they were. I did not fetch that host this sitting. Do not invent a capture date. If you need to see an old public page, open that door, or write unverified.

A copy of a health claim is still a health claim. Walk it to MedlinePlus the same way you would walk the first ad. Easy-to-read pages and pages in more than one language sit on that host. A share in a second language is not a second fact. It is a second pile of words.

Forwarding is a new act with a new payer of attention: you. You chose to load someone else's conveyor. The want may now be yours, even if you did not write the sentence. That does not make you the source. It does make you the sender of this copy.

**Check.** Copy this sentence onto paper: "Mill Street closed four nights, town board minutes 19 May." Now copy it again, but drop "19 May." You now have two piles. The second cannot be checked against a day's minutes.

New case: screenshot a headline and nothing else. Hand the screenshot to a friend. Ask them to name the source. If they cannot, you shared a shouting line. You did not share a report. A third case: the same screenshot, now with the host name and the time you took it written under the picture in your own hand. That caption is yours. It is the piece the crop would have dropped.

## 10. A check: two doors on the same event

One event. Two official doors. Write what each says. Keep the overlap. Do not average the fight. Mark the silence.

This is the whole method of the book, done on purpose.

Pick an event you did not invent: a public meeting, a weather day, a health test name, a town count. Open two hosts that own that kind of fact. Not two posts. Two doors.

Law in this library is the same here as in study: if two official pages disagree, say so. Do not average them. If a textbook and a door disagree on a number, believe the door and mark the textbook. If a door will not open, write unverified and stop building on a remembered figure.

A health event: an ad names a test. MedlinePlus names diagnostic imaging as tests that create pictures of the inside of the body and help providers understand health problems and make decisions about care. That is one door, fetched for this book. A second health door might be a medical tests page on the same host, or a clinic's own handout you can hold. If the ad says "this picture means you are well" and MedlinePlus says the picture helps a provider decide, the ad added a comfort the door did not sell. Keep the door. Mark the comfort.

A public record: a social post shows an old letter and says it is a law. The National Archives door is where US federal records of that kind live. I fetched that front door this sitting and got the host's home title, not a record body. So this check, for you, is: open that door, find one record you can actually see, write its title and date as the page prints them. Then open a second door, a newspaper collection at the Library of Congress newspapers page, and see whether that event is reported there. I could not read that newspapers page this sitting. If you also cannot, write unverified for the newspaper side and do not finish the story from a memory of a headline.

A count of people: a headline says a town "emptied." The Census doors own official counts of people and housing. Open one. Write the number on the row. Then open a second door, the town's own minutes or a state page, and write that number. If they disagree, do not average. If one is a different year, you do not have a disagreement yet. You have two times.

Poynter sits in the link index as a journalism-education host. I did not fetch that host this sitting. It is a door for craft, not a second Census. Do not treat a craft page as a count of people.

**Check.** On paper, make three columns: event, door A, door B. Fill one row with a health claim walked to MedlinePlus and to one other named host. Fill a second row with a public-record claim walked to the National Archives and to one newspaper door. If a cell is empty because a door would not open, write unverified in that cell. Do not fill it with a remembered number. The new case is the second row. If both rows reuse the same door, you did one check twice.

That is the object of this book, used: a report, an ad, or a feed item, walked until it has a named source, a number with a whole, a visible hole, and a second door.
