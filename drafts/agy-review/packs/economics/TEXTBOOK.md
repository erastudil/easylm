## 1. A household

Three people share a kitchen, a rent bill, and a jar of coins on the shelf. Mira takes home $1,840 this month. Ken takes home $960. Ivo is a child and does not bring pay. The jar this month is $1,840 + $960 = $2,800.

Rent takes $1,100. Food takes $380. Two bus passes take $70. Heat takes $90. Those four bills are $1,100 + $380 + $70 + $90 = $1,640. What stays in the jar is $2,800 − $1,640 = $1,160.

The people who share the roof and the jar are a **household**. The coins that came in are **income**. The coins that went out for the month's living are **spending**. What stayed is **saving** for this month, if they do not owe it already.

A household can be one person with a key. It can be five. The object is the shared roof and the shared pile. A cousin who sleeps on the sofa and eats from the pot sits in this household for the month. A Sunday visitor does not.

Write the month as a table so the jar can be checked.

| line | dollars |
|---|---:|
| Mira take-home | 1840 |
| Ken take-home | 960 |
| rent | −1100 |
| food | −380 |
| bus | −70 |
| heat | −90 |
| left in the jar | 1160 |

$1,840 + $960 = $2,800. $1,100 + $380 + $70 + $90 = $1,640. $2,800 − $1,640 = $1,160. If those three lines disagree, a bill was skipped or a pay was counted twice.

On 15 September 2026 the U.S. Census Bureau homepage stated that median household income was $87,460 in 2025. That is a middle of a national pile, for a year. $87,460 ÷ 12: twelve times $7,288 is $87,456, so about $7,288.33 a month. Mira and Ken's $2,800 month is an invented flat on Reed lane, not a Census cell. Fetch [census.gov](https://www.census.gov/) or the 15 September 2026 income press release when the $87,460 must travel. A mean household income would be a different number, pulled by the upper tail. Census printed a median.

Unpaid work in the kitchen still uses hours. BEA keeps a household-production account for that work. I did not fetch the dollar table, so no unpaid-work total is printed here.

**Check.** A new flat. Rio takes home $1,260. Sam takes home $1,140. Rent $980, food $410, bus $55, heat $0 this month because the stove is off. In: $1,260 + $1,140 = $2,400. Out: $980 + $410 + $55 = $1,445. Left: $2,400 − $1,445 = $955. A second new case: one person, take-home $2,200, rent $1,300, food $240, bus $40, heat $80. Out $1,660. Left $540. If rent alone is $2,400 on $2,200 take-home, the jar goes negative by $200 before food. That household must borrow, share a roof with someone else, or change a line.

## 2. A firm

A copier runs in a rented room. Paper goes in. Posters come out. Vale stands at the desk and takes coins. This month Vale Print sells 80 posters at $6 each. Receipts are $80 × $6 = $480.

Paper costs $70. Ink costs $55. Room rent costs $90. Vale pays Joss $180 to wash trays and stack paper. Those bills are $70 + $55 + $90 + $180 = $395. Receipts take away the bills: $480 − $395 = $85. The $85 is what remains for Vale after the inputs and the pay.

A group that buys inputs, sells a good or a service, and keeps or loses the remainder is a **firm**. The coins from the sales are **revenue**. The bills spent to make those sales are **cost**. Revenue take away cost is **profit** when the remainder is positive, and a **loss** when it is negative.

Profit is not the $480. People point at the till and call the till "what the shop made." The till is revenue. The $85 is profit. Hide the $395 and the story is false.

The $180 to Joss is cost to the firm and income to Joss's household. Same coins, two books. Chapter 1's jar and this chapter's cost column meet there.

Vale could spend the afternoon mending coats instead of running the copier. Four coats at $20 each would have brought $80, with no paper bill. Running the copier means those coats stay unmended. The coats she did not mend are the **opportunity cost** of the posters: the next best use of the same afternoon. The $85 profit already took paper, ink, rent, and Joss off the $480. It did not take the missed coats off. Write both when you choose.

Census counts firms. The homepage lists an Economic Census every five years, and an Annual Integrated Economic Survey. I fetched the homepage, not those questionnaires, so no firm-count cell is printed here. NAICS, trending on the same homepage, names what kind of work a firm does. I did not fetch the code list.

**Check.** A new stall. 45 jars at $8 = $360 revenue. Fruit $110, sugar $40, stall fee $25, pay $90. Cost $265. Profit $360 − $265 = $95. Drop the price to $6 and keep 45 jars: revenue $270, profit $5. A second new case: 45 jars at $8, but 10 jars spoil and cannot be sold. Revenue $35 × $8 = $280. Cost still $265 if the fruit was already bought. Profit $15. The spoiled 10 are a cost that already sat in the fruit line. A third: revenue $200, cost $265. Loss $65.

## 3. A price

A rye loaf sits on a stall. The stall keeper will hand it over for three coins, not for two. The buyer who wants the loaf must give up those three coins, which could have bought a bus ride instead.

How many coins a seller asks, and a buyer pays, for one unit is a **price**. Write the unit. $3 per loaf. $6 per soap. $2.50 per bus ride. "$3" with no unit is a count of coins, not a price.

Two prices make a ratio. Soap $6, loaf $3. One soap costs two loaves. That ratio is a **relative price**: how many of one good you give up for one of the other. If both prices double, $12 soap and $6 loaf, the soap still costs two loaves. The coins changed. The trade-off between soap and bread did not.

A household buys more than one thing. Hold the list still and let only the prices move. That is how you see prices as a pile, not as a single loaf.

Reed-lane basket for one week, five loaves, two soaps, four bus rides.

| good | count | week-1 price | week-1 spend | week-2 price | week-2 spend |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| rye loaf | 5 | 2.00 | 10.00 | 2.40 | 12.00 |
| soap | 2 | 5.00 | 10.00 | 5.00 | 10.00 |
| bus ride | 4 | 3.00 | 12.00 | 3.00 | 12.00 |
| basket |  |  | 32.00 |  | 34.00 |

Week 1 costs $32. Week 2 costs $34 for the same counts. The ratio is $34 / $32 = 1.0625. Put week 1 at 100 and week 2 at $1.0625 × 100 = 106.25. The extra spend is $2 on $32, which is $2/32 = 0.0625 = 6.25%.

A number that compares the cost of a held basket at two sets of prices is a **price index**. 100 is the home week. 106.25 means the held basket costs 6.25% more.

The loaf moved. Soap and the bus did not. Saying "prices went up 6.25%" is true of this basket and false of soap. A single loaf rose $0.40 on $2.00, which is 20%. The basket rose 6.25% because soap and rides sat still and still had weight.

This pack owns that walk. The official US shopper index lives at BLS. On 11 September 2026 the BLS homepage stated that the CPI for all items increased 0.4% in August, and that gasoline rose. CPI here is the **Consumer Price Index**. The same homepage describes the CPI Inflation Calculator as using the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, CPI-U, U.S. city average, all items, not seasonally adjusted. I did not run the calculator, so no dollar pair from it is printed here. I fetched the homepage, not the August release body, so the item weights and the index level are unverified here. Open [bls.gov](https://www.bls.gov/) and the CPI news release for those.

BLS also printed, on 10 September 2026, that the PPI for final demand moved up 0.4% in August, with goods up 1.1% and services up 0.1%. PPI is a **Producer Price Index**: prices firms receive, not the Reed-lane shopper basket. The two 0.4% headlines match by chance on that month's all-items line. Goods and services inside PPI do not match each other. Do not average CPI and PPI.

On 16 September 2026 BLS printed that US import prices rose 0.7% in August and export prices rose 0.6%. Those are border prices, a third door on the same host. 0.7% is not 0.4%. Leave them separate.

**Check.** New basket: 8 pencils at $0.50 and 1 notebook at $4. Week 1: $4 + $4 = $8. Week 2: pencils $0.60, notebook $4. Cost $4.80 + $4 = $8.80. Index $8.80/$8 × 100 = 110. Up 10%. Pencils alone went from $0.50 to $0.60, which is 20%. The notebook did not move. A second new case: hold prices, change counts. Week 1 still $8. Week 2 they buy 4 pencils and 1 notebook at the old prices: $2 + $4 = $6. That is a smaller pile, not a price fall. A price index holds the counts still.

## 4. Trade

Nia has twelve eggs and no flour. Omar has eight cups of flour and no eggs. Nia wants a cake. Omar wants an omelette. Each can eat only what they already hold, or they can pass something across the table.

Nia gives Omar six eggs. Omar gives Nia two cups of flour. Nia still has six eggs and now two cups of flour. Omar still has six cups of flour and now six eggs. If Nia values two cups of flour more than six eggs, she is better off. If Omar values six eggs more than two cups of flour, he is better off. Both can gain from the same swap.

Passing a good for a good, or a good for coins, is **trade**. The rate in this swap is six eggs for two cups, which is three eggs per cup of flour. That rate is a price in eggs.

Trade does not invent eggs. It moves them to the person who wants them more, from the person who wants the flour more. The pile of eggs in the room is still twelve. The pile of flour is still eight cups. What grew is how well the piles fit the two uses.

A town line works the same way. Reed lane bakes jam and sells it to the next town. The next town sends nails. BEA and Census count those crossings for the United States. On the BEA homepage fetched this sitting, the goods and services trade deficit increased from $71.2 billion in June (revised) to $88.6 billion in July 2026, as imports increased and exports decreased. The goods deficit increased $17.6 billion to $119.6 billion. The services surplus increased $0.2 billion to $31.0 billion. Check the pieces: $119.6 billion goods deficit take away $31.0 billion services surplus is $88.6 billion. The three lines close. I fetched the homepage, not the commodity table, so which goods moved is unverified here. Open the July 2026 trade release.

A **deficit** in that sentence means the country bought more goods and services from abroad than it sold abroad, in dollars, that month. A **surplus** on services means the service sales abroad were larger than the service buys. Goods and services can lean opposite ways in the same month. July did.

**Check.** New swap. Pia has 10 candles, Quin has 4 kg of wax leftover. Pia gives 3 candles, Quin gives 1 kg of wax. Pia: 7 candles and 1 kg. Quin: 3 kg and 3 candles. The room still holds 10 candles and 4 kg. A second new case: 9 apples for 3 pears. Rate: 3 apples per pear. If the next stall posts 4 apples per pear, Pia's 3-for-1 is a better apple-price of pears for the apple holder. Walk to the better rate, or the 3-for-1 was a gift.

## 5. Work and pay

Joss stands at a sink at Vale Print for seven hours. At the end of the day the shop puts $91 in an envelope.

$91 for seven hours is $91 ÷ 7 = $13 per hour. Fourteen trays washed at $6.50 a tray is $14 × $6.50 = $91. Same pile, three names: a day's pay, an hourly rate, a piece rate. The **wage** is the pay per hour, here $13. A **salary** would be a posted pile for a month or a year, not cut by the hour on the envelope. Joss does not have one.

Take-home can be smaller than the posted rate. If the lane office takes $7 off the envelope, Joss holds $84. Chapter 9 takes that cut from the other side.

A wage buys loaves, not only coins. At $3.25 a loaf, $91 buys $91 ÷ $3.25. $3.25 × 28 = $91, so 28 loaves. If the loaf moves to $3.50 and the envelope stays $91, $91 ÷ $3.50 = 26 loaves. The coins held still. The loaves fell. Pay measured in goods is **real** pay. Pay measured in coins is **nominal** pay.

On 11 September 2026 the BLS homepage stated that real average hourly earnings for all employees decreased 0.1% in August. That line already did the loaf-cut to an official price index. I fetched the homepage, not the earnings table, so the nominal hourly dollar figure for August is unverified here. Open the real-earnings release.

People who want work and do not have it sit in a different pile from people who are not looking. BLS counts both kinds of fact. On the homepage this sitting, for August, unemployment rates were lower in 8 states and the District of Columbia and stable in 42 states. Nonfarm payroll employment increased in 4 states and was essentially unchanged in 46 states and the District. I did not fetch the state table, so no named state's rate is printed here. The Current Population Survey, listed on Census as conducted monthly, is the household survey behind much of that work story. Open CPS and BLS for the method.

**Check.** New envelope. 6 hours at $15 = $90. At $2.50 a loaf, $90 buys 36 loaves. Loaf moves to $3.00, envelope stays $90: 30 loaves. Nominal pay unchanged. Real pay fell by 6 loaves. A second new case: 6 hours at $16 = $96, loaf still $3. $96 buys 32 loaves. The extra dollar per hour bought back 2 of the 6 lost loaves, not all 6. A third: posted $90, office takes $12, take-home $78. At $3 a loaf that is 26 loaves.

## 6. Money as a tool

Mira owes Len four loaves. She could carry four loaves across town. She could also hand him a token they both trust. Len can take that token to Vale and buy posters, without ever eating Mira's rye.

A token that settles a debt, and that the next person will take, is **money**. The loaves are goods. The token is a tool for moving claims. If Len will not take the token, it is not money in that lane. If only Len will take it, it is a private IOU, not yet a token the stall will hold.

Three jobs show up once the token is in use. It prices the loaf: $3 per loaf, chapter 3. It pays the loaf: hand over three tokens. It holds a claim overnight in the jar, chapter 1, so Mira does not have to keep extra eggs until Saturday. Those three jobs are unit of account, medium of exchange, and store of value, once you have seen them. The names are labels on work the token already did.

Money is not the same pile as income. Mira's household took home $2,800 this month. The jar might hold $200 on the first morning. The $2,800 is a flow through the month. The $200 is a stock on a morning. Mixing them is the till mistake from chapter 2, in a new coat.

The US token is the dollar. The Federal Reserve is the public door for the central bank that issues it. The Treasury is the public door for federal debt and the dollar as a government tool. I did not fetch those two hosts this sitting. No policy rate or debt total is printed here.

A chalk debt of "4 loaves" dies when the loaves arrive. A token can pass through six hands before anyone bakes. If people stop trusting the token, they want the rye itself, and the tool stops doing the middle job.

**Check.** New debt. Ash owes Bo 12 nails. Ash hands Bo 12 tokens Bo's stall will take. Bo buys wax from Quin with those tokens. Ash never carried nails. The debt is settled. A second new case: tokens in the jar, 40. Loaves $4. The jar buys 10 loaves tonight. Loaves $5, jar still 40: 8 loaves. The token count held. The store-of-value job, in rye, shrank. That is chapter 3's index, felt in the jar.

## 7. Want and how much is for sale

Eight people walk toward a stall that has five rye loaves left. At $2, more hands go up. At $6, most hands go down and Vale would rather bake more, because each loaf brings more coins.

Ask each buyer the most they will pay today, and ask Vale how many loaves she will bake at each posted price. Write both lists.

| price per loaf | loaves wanted | loaves Vale will bake |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 20 | 4 |
| 3 | 14 | 8 |
| 4 | 10 | 10 |
| 5 | 6 | 14 |
| 6 | 3 | 18 |

At $2, people want 20 and Vale bakes 4. Sixteen people walk away empty, or the first four take the loaves and the rest do not. At $6, Vale bakes 18 and people want 3. Fifteen loaves sit. At $4, want is 10 and bake is 10. The two lists meet.

How many units buyers will take at a price is **quantity demanded**. How many units sellers will offer at a price is **quantity supplied**. The whole list of wants against price is **demand**. The whole list of offers against price is **supply**. The meeting price in the table is $4, and the meeting count is 10 loaves.

A gap with more want than loaves is a **shortage**. A gap with more loaves than want is a **surplus**. $2 is a shortage of 16. $6 is a surplus of 15. The names are the gaps you already counted.

Hold income still in this table. If Mira's take-home jumps and rye is a good she buys more of when the jar is fuller, the want column rises at each price. That is a new table, not a walk along the old one. A walk along the old one is a price change. A new column is a change in something else: income, the price of jam, the weather, Vale's oven.

**Check.** New table, candles. At $1, want 12, for sale 3. At $2, want 8, for sale 6. At $3, want 5, for sale 9. No exact meet on a whole dollar. Between $2 and $3, want falls from 8 to 5 and sale rises from 6 to 9. The lists cross in that gap. A second new case: freeze the want column at 8, 8, 8 and let sale run 3, 6, 9. Then a price rise does not cut want, and the meet is where sale hits 8, between $2 and $3. Same method. Different table.

## 8. A market

Saturday the lane fills with stalls, lists of prices, and people with coins. Several bakers post rye. Buyers walk the row. If one baker posts $6 and the next posts $4 for a loaf that looks the same, the $6 loaf sits. If one baker posts $2, that stall empties, and the neighbors see it.

The meeting of the want list and the for-sale list, in a place and a time, is a **market**. Reed lane Saturday is a market for rye, for soap, for bus tokens at the kiosk. One lane can hold several markets at once, one per good.

A posted $4 that keeps repeating, with loaves neither stacking nor running out, is a **market price** for that Saturday. Chapter 7's table met at $4 and 10 loaves. Many bakers, and buyers who can walk, push the posts toward that meeting. One baker who tries $6 without a better loaf loses the row. One baker who tries $2 sells out and then raises, or bakes more.

When walking is hard, the post can stick away from the meeting. One stall in a locked station, no other bread, can post $6 and still sell 3 loaves, because the want list at $6 was 3. That is still a market. It is a thin one. The table from chapter 7 still works. The walk along the row does not.

Census and BLS do not post Reed-lane rye. They post series built from many stalls and firms. World Bank Open Data is the door when the market you mean is a country table. I did not fetch a cell. No country price is printed from memory.

**Check.** Three stalls post $5, $4, and $4 for the same rye. Buyers who can walk take the $4. The $5 stall sells zero unless it cuts or shows a reason. A new case: rain closes two of three stalls. The remaining stall has 6 loaves. Want at $4 was 10. Shortage 4 at that post. The remaining stall can raise the post and still sell its 6. The want column did not jump. The for-sale column collapsed. That is a supply cut, read on the same table.

## 9. What an office takes and spends

The lane office paints the stones, clears the drain, and pays a clerk. It takes a stall fee from each keeper to do that work. Five stalls pay $8 each. The office takes $40. It pays a carpenter $40 to reset the drain. The $40 left the keepers and came back as drain work.

A public office that takes coins by rule and spends them on work the lane shares is a **government** in this book, at town scale. The take is a **tax**. The spend is **government spending**. The stall fee is a simple tax. Mira's $7 cut from Joss's envelope in chapter 5 is the same object from the worker's side.

A country adds the same pieces, only larger. Households spend. Firms buy shelves that last. Offices spend. Goods cross the border. Add those flows for a year, take care with the border, and you have a picture of what was produced.

Reed lane, one week, invented, so you can add it.

Households buy local bread $70 and imported nails $8. Vale buys a shelf from a carpenter, $18, to use for months. The office pays that carpenter $22 to mend a public bench. Vale sells jam to the next town, $14. The nails, $8, came from outside.

Write household spending as **consumption**, C = $70 + $8 = $78. Write the shelf as **investment**, I = $18. Write the bench as government spending, G = $22. Write jam sold out as **exports**, X = $14. Write nails bought in as **imports**, M = $8. **Net exports** are X take away M: $14 − $8 = $6.

Add them: $78 + $18 + $22 + $6 = $124.

That $124 is the lane's **gross domestic product** for the week, GDP, on the spending side. Imports sat inside the $78, then were taken back off, so the nails do not count as Reed-lane production.

The same $124 from production. Vale's sales: $70 bread + $14 jam = $84. No intermediate goods this week. Vale's value added is $84. The carpenter's sales: $18 shelf + $22 bench = $40. Value added $40. $84 + $40 = $124. The nails were made elsewhere.

The same $124 from income. Vale pays wages $52 and keeps profit $32. The carpenter pays wages $28 and keeps profit $12. Wages $52 + $28 = $80. Profit $32 + $12 = $44. $80 + $44 = $124.

Three walks, one pile. If they disagree, a flow was double-counted or dropped.

The office takes $22 in stall fees, equal to G this week. Households and firms receive $124, pay $22, and hold $102 after tax. They spend $78 on consumption and save $24. Saving $24 matches I + net exports: $18 + $6 = $24. The table closes.

BEA owns the US walk. On the homepage fetched this sitting, real GDP increased at an annual rate of 1.5% in the second quarter of 2026 (April, May, June) on the second estimate. In the first quarter, real GDP increased 2.1%. **Real** here means the pile of goods after a price adjustment, the national-account cousin of chapter 3's held basket. I fetched the homepage, not the full release, so the dollar level of GDP and the contribution table are unverified here. Open the second-estimate release. BEA also printed that it will send this year's annual update beginning 30 September 2026. A later vintage can revise 1.5%.

Personal income increased $115.1 billion, 0.4% at a monthly rate, in July 2026. Disposable personal income, personal income less personal current taxes, increased $125.9 billion, 0.5%. Personal consumption expenditures increased $36.3 billion, 0.2%. Those three lines are the country-scale jar: income, income after tax, spending. I did not fetch the outlays table beyond the homepage, so interest payments and the saving rate are unverified here.

A tiny real-versus-nominal check, invented, so the 1.5% is not lonely. Year 1: 10 loaves at $3 = $30. Year 2: 11 loaves at $4 = $44. Coins went $44/$30 = 1.466..., up about 46.7%. Hold year-1 prices: 11 × $3 = $33, which is 10% more loaves. The extra coins were mostly price. BEA's 1.5% is the loaf-count kind of growth, at a national scale, annualized for a quarter.

**Check.** New town, one week. C = $50, I = $10, G = $12, X = $7, M = $9. Net exports $7 − $9 = −$2. GDP = $50 + $10 + $12 − $2 = $70. Production: one mill value added $45, one shop $25, sum $70. Income: wages $48, profit $22, sum $70. A second new case: leave every piece still except M rises to $15. Net exports $7 − $15 = −$8. GDP = $50 + $10 + $12 − $8 = $64. Extra imports, with C held still, means more of the $50 was made elsewhere. GDP falls $6, which is the extra $6 of imports.

## 10. A check on a claim about prices

A poster says "prices jumped four tenths of a percent last month, so your rye should cost four tenths more."

Take the sentence apart the way data taught: who, which pile, which time, which typical name. Then do the basket walk this pack owns.

BLS, on 11 September 2026, printed that the CPI for all items increased 0.4% in August, and that gasoline rose. That is an official claim about one national urban basket, CPI-U as the calculator page names it, for one month. It is not a claim about Reed-lane rye. It is not a claim about Vale's paper. It is not a claim about every good in the basket moving 0.4%.

Reed-lane rye in chapter 3 went from $2.00 to $2.40, which is 20%, while the held basket went 6.25%. Four tenths of a percent on a $2.00 loaf is $2.00 × 0.004 = $0.008. The stall does not post eight mills. The stall posted forty cents. The poster used a national index to dictate one loaf. That step fails.

Goods and services can split. PPI final demand in August moved 0.4% on the BLS homepage of 10 September 2026, but goods advanced 1.1% and services rose 0.1%. Import prices rose 0.7% and export prices 0.6% on the 16 September 2026 line. CPI 0.4% and PPI 0.4% are not one fact repeated. They are two baskets. Import 0.7% disagrees with both all-items lines. Do not average 0.4, 0.4, 0.7, and 0.6 to make a friendlier number. LAW: if two official pages disagree, say so.

Real and nominal still split. BEA's real GDP +1.5% in 2026 Q2 is quantities. CPI +0.4% in August is prices. A poster that says "the economy was up 0.4%" from the CPI line has named the wrong object.

Worked close, new basket. Claim: "school kit prices up 25%." Kit: 10 pencils and 1 ruler. Week 1: pencils $0.40, ruler $2. Cost $4 + $2 = $6. Week 2: pencils $0.40, ruler $3.50. Cost $4 + $3.50 = $7.50. $7.50/$6 = 1.25. Up 25%. The claim holds for the held kit. Pencils did not move. A parent who only buys pencils saw 0%. Write the kit, or the 25% is a poster.

Second close, new numbers. Claim: "pay kept up with prices." Envelope $80 to $82, up $2 on $80, which is 2.5%. Basket $40 to $42, up 5%. Real envelope in basket units: week 1 buys $80/$40 = 2 baskets. Week 2 buys $82/$42 ≈ 1.95 baskets. Pay did not keep up. BLS real average hourly earnings, −0.1% in August on the 11 September 2026 homepage line, is that check at national scale. Open the release before you reuse the −0.1%.

Third close. Claim: "July trade proves shops charged 0.4% more." July's goods and services deficit, $88.6 billion on the BEA homepage, is exports against imports, not a shopper index. A deficit can widen because import volumes rose, because import prices rose, because exports fell, or because a revision moved June. The homepage said imports increased and exports decreased. That is a quantity-and-price mix. It does not print 0.4%. Stop.

When the number must travel as a US price fact, fetch BLS. When it must travel as production or income, fetch BEA. When it must travel as a household or firm count, fetch Census. OpenStax *Principles of Economics 3e* is the next long walk. Open the door. Do not copy the book into this file.
