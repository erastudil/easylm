# Shots in time

This book is shots in time.

On Lark Pier the ferry Gull noses into the slip. Mira holds a camera still on the bow. The camera runs. She stops. What she kept is not one picture. It is a stretch of looking with a start and an end.

This pack owns the walk of a shot, a cut, and a frame. A still picture lives in photography. Name that door and stop. A reported fact lives in journalism. Name that door and stop. A feed lives in media. Name that door and stop.

The National Film Preservation Board about page did not return a usable body. The Archives home returned a title without counts. Registry numbers stay unverified until those doors speak. Worked cases on this pier are invented.

## 1. A shot

Mira stands 12 metres from the Gull's bow. She starts the camera. The bow fills the glass. A rope goes slack. A deckhand steps once. She stops. The stretch on the tape is 8.0 seconds long. Nothing else is on that stretch. She did not swing to the kiosk. She did not follow the deckhand down the pier.

That run, from start to stop, is a **shot**. The name is a handle for one stretch of looking the camera kept without a break. You already have the 8.0 seconds. The word arrives after.

If the camera had kept running while Mira turned from the bow to the kiosk, that turn would still be one shot. The glass would change. The run would not. A turn inside a run is not a stop. Chapter 2 owns the stop that joins two runs. This chapter owns the run.

Tess writes on a card: "Gull bow, 12 metres, 8.0 s." She asks Mira to do it again because a gull crossed the glass. The second run is 7.0 seconds. Same place, same job, two tries. People name each try a **take** when they want a handle for a repeat of the same shot. Take 1 is 8.0 seconds. Take 2 is 7.0. They are not two shots of two jobs. They are two tries at one job. Keep the card. Write which take you will use.

Rafi's hands on crate 41 are a different job. Mira starts again. Hands, stencil, a scrape of wood on the dock. She stops at 11.0 seconds. That is a second shot, not a third take of the bow. The card now has two lines: bow, hands.

A still picture of the bow, made with no run, lives in photography. Name that door and stop. The 8.0 seconds have a before and an after inside them. A still does not.

How a second is fixed lives in measurement. Name that door and stop. The 8.0 and the 11.0 here are Mira's timer on Lark Pier. A remembered rule for how long a shot "should" be is unverified here. NIST's home names the institute as the National Metrology Institute for the United States, established 1901, with 125 years of work. It did not print a shot length. Those seconds stay on the card.

**Check.** Write two cards. Card A: camera runs 8.0 seconds on the bow, stops, runs 11.0 seconds on the hands. Card B: camera runs 13.0 seconds while Mira turns from bow to kiosk without stopping. New case: take 3 of the bow, 6.5 seconds, because a cloud moved. Three takes, one shot job. If your card lists three shots, you counted tries. Another: Jonah stands at the kiosk and Mira never starts the camera. There is a looking. There is no shot.

## 2. A cut

Tess lays the 8.0 second bow next to the 11.0 second hands. She joins the end of one to the start of the other. Play. Bow, then hands. No 12 metres of walking in between. The join is sudden. The pier did not jump. The tape did.

That join is a **cut**. The name is a handle for where one shot ends and the next begins. You already saw the jump. The word is for the jump.

The two shots add. $8.0 + 11.0 = 19.0$ seconds of picture. The clock on the kiosk in the bow shot reads 14:10. In the hands shot it is not in the glass. On the dock, Mira walked 40 seconds between the two runs, and Rafi had not yet touched crate 41 when the bow was filmed. The cut hides the 40 seconds and the wait. Hiding is the job. Write what you hid if you know it.

A third shot: the kiosk window, 3.0 seconds, Rafi's face behind glass. Tess can put it between bow and hands, or after hands, or not at all. Between: $8.0 + 3.0 + 11.0 = 22.0$ seconds. After hands: still 22.0 seconds, different order. Left out: 19.0 seconds, and Rafi's face never arrives. A cut is a choice of neighbor, not a law of the dock.

A fade is a different join. Picture goes to black over 2.0 seconds at the end of the bow. Then the hands come up. That 2.0 seconds is not a third shot. It is a way of leaving. Stay on the hard join while the question is who sits next to whom. Name the fade if you use one. Do not call the fade a cut and stop thinking.

The photography door still owns a still. Two stills side by side on a table are not a cut. A cut needs two runs, or two pieces of runs, and a play that crosses the join.

**Check.** Bow 8.0, window 3.0, hands 11.0. Write the three orders Tess can play without dropping a shot. Each order is 22.0 seconds. If all three feel the same in your head, play them. The first thing you see changes: bow, or face, or hands. The dock did not change. The joins did.

New case: Tess splits the 11.0 second hands at 4.0 seconds. She puts the 8.0 second bow between the 4.0 and the leftover 7.0. Picture time $4.0 + 8.0 + 7.0 = 19.0$ seconds. The hands now wrap the bow. Rafi did not pack, stop, and pack again. The cut says he did. Write that the dock and the tape now disagree, and which one you are claiming.

## 3. Sound

Rafi says "crate forty-one" while Mira's camera runs on his mouth. The mouth move lasts 0.8 seconds on the picture. Jonah holds a recorder 0.6 metres from Rafi's chest. The same words last 0.8 seconds on Jonah's tape. In the room that night Tess starts both tapes. The mouth opens. The voice is late. She slides Jonah's tape until the first hard sound of "crate" hits the frame where the lips close on the t. Now mouth and voice sit together.

That sitting-together is **sync**. The name is a handle for picture and sound sharing a mark. You already moved the tape. The word is for the match.

Tess also has a clap. At the head of the hands shot she hit two sticks. The picture shows the sticks meet. The recorder shows a spike. The meet and the spike are one event. Line those, and the 0.8 seconds of "crate forty-one" that follow should line up too. If they do not, one tape stretched or one clock drifted. Write the miss in seconds. Do not guess which tape is honest until you have a reason.

Now delay Jonah's words by 0.5 seconds after a good sync. The mouth says "crate." The voice arrives later. Anyone can see the split. That split is useful as a check. It is a fault if you wanted the match.

Sound can live on a shot that has no mouth. The 8.0 second bow has rope, water, a gull. Jonah's recorder, 12 metres from the bow and 2.0 metres from a generator, will hold the generator louder than the rope. Mira's camera, if it has a small mic on top, will hold a thinner rope and less generator. Two records of the same 8.0 seconds. Write which pile of air you will keep.

A feed of clips with a song under them lives in media. Name that door and stop. This chapter owns the 0.8 seconds and whether they sit on the mouth.

NIST's home did not print a delay that counts as "late." 0.5 seconds is late on this mouth because you can see it. A smaller miss, 0.04 seconds, may also be late for some ears. Unverified as a public limit. Write the miss you can measure.

**Check.** Mouth 0.8 seconds. Voice lined up, then voice 0.5 seconds late. Write what a stranger sees in each play. If both writes say "Rafi talks," you hid the miss.

New case: the 3.0 second window shot, glass between Jonah and Rafi. Picture of a mouth. Sound of a generator. If you call that sync because both tapes started at 14:10, you used a clock instead of a mouth. Sync is the meet, not the hour. Another: Tess uses the clap, then drops the clap from the finished 11.0 seconds. The finished shot can still be in sync. The clap was a tool. It does not have to stay in the picture.

## 4. Time in a sequence

Tess now has a **sequence**: bow 8.0, window 3.0, hands 11.0, played in that order. $8.0 + 3.0 + 11.0 = 22.0$ seconds of picture. The kiosk clock in the bow reads 14:10. In the window shot, over Rafi's shoulder, the same clock reads 16:40.

From 14:10 to 16:40 is 2 hours and 30 minutes. That is 150 minutes of clock. $150 \times 60 = 9000$ seconds of afternoon claimed by the two clock faces. The sequence offers 22.0 seconds of looking. $9000 \div 22.0 \approx 409$. The tape is not claiming to be a 409-times law of nature. It is claiming that an afternoon passed, and it is showing you 22.0 seconds. Write both numbers. Do not blend them into one "time."

People name the time the tape lets you feel **screen time**, and the time the faces and the story claim **story time**, when they want two handles. You already have 22.0 and 9000. The names are for not mixing them.

A different order changes story time. Hands first, then bow: you might read the packing as before the Gull arrives, even if Mira filmed the bow at 14:10 and the hands at 16:20. The clock in the glass, if you leave it readable, can fight that reading. If Tess crops the clock out of the bow, the fight goes away. Cropping the clock is a time choice.

Play the 11.0 second hands twice in a row. $8.0 + 3.0 + 11.0 + 11.0 = 33.0$ seconds of picture. Rafi packed once. The sequence packs twice. Story time and dock time have split. Write the split.

A still from 14:10 lives in photography and holds one clock. Journalism owns a reported hour in a sentence you can test. Name those doors and stop. This chapter owns the two clocks in two shots, and the 22.0 seconds that carry them.

**Check.** 14:10 in the bow, 16:40 in the window, 22.0 seconds of picture, 9000 seconds of claimed afternoon. A new case: Tess cuts the window shot. Only bow and hands. No second clock. Story time is now unverified from faces. You may still know the dock hours from Tess's card. The sequence no longer shows them. Write what the stranger can know from the tape alone.

Another: the window clock reads 14:12, two minutes after 14:10, in a 3.0 second shot. Story time and screen time are close. $2 \times 60 = 120$ seconds claimed, 3.0 seconds shown. Do not call that "real time" as a law. Call it two minutes claimed in three seconds shown.

## 5. A frame

Tess stops the 5.0 second bow take on a player that can step. Each step is one still. She counts 125 stills from the first picture to the last. $125 \div 5.0 = 25$ stills in each second of that take.

One of those stills is a **frame**. The name is a handle for one picture in the run. You already stepped. The word is for the step.

Frame 1 is the bow with the rope taut. Frame 40 is the rope slack. Frame 125 is a deckhand's heel. The 5.0 seconds are those 125 pictures in order, shown fast enough that a stranger sees motion instead of 125 stills. How fast stills must go for that seeing is unverified here. NIST's home did not print a frame rate. A hallway number of 24 frames in a second is unverified here. This take is 25 stills per second because 125 stills sat in 5.0 seconds.

A print of frame 40, laid on a table, is a still picture. Photography owns that still as a picture. Name that door and stop. In this pack the same still has a before and an after: frame 39, frame 41. If you cannot name those neighbors, you are using the photography object.

Mira's other camera, on a phone, records the 3.0 second window. Tess steps it. She counts 90 stills. $90 \div 3.0 = 30$ stills per second. Two cameras, two rates. Do not average 25 and 30 into 27.5 and call 27.5 the pier's rate. Write which camera.

**Check.** 125 stills, 5.0 seconds, 25 per second. New case: a 2.0 second clap at the head of the hands shot. Tess counts 50 stills. $50 \div 2.0 = 25$ per second. Same camera as the bow take. If she had counted 48, the two counts would disagree. Do not average 25 and 24.

Another: print frame 40 of the bow and put it on a wall. A neighbor says "that is the film." It is one frame. The film is the run. Send the neighbor to photography for the still, and back here for the neighbors of frame 40.

## 8. A document vs a story

Len keeps the night log. At 02:10 he writes: "Crate 41 not on the dock. Counted 40 crates. Bay 3 empty." The sentence names an hour, a count, a bay. A stranger can walk to Bay 3 at the next 02:10 and count again. The next night may differ. The line is still the kind of line you can test.

Hold them side by side. The log is a **document** as this book uses the word: a record that claims a count and an hour. The short is a **story**: shots arranged so a stranger feels a crate leave. Same pier. Two jobs. The names arrive after you have the pad and the 22.0 seconds.
