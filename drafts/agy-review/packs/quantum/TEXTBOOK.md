# Two marks and a look

Piet throws a pebble along a bench. The pebble has a path. Mechanics already taught that path, the mass, and the push that started it. Force lives in that pack. Open it if a push is the object. Charge and the field around a charge live in electromagnetism. Open that pack if a field is the object. How a metre, a second, and a kilogram are fixed lives in measurement. Open that pack for SI. This book does not reprint those walks.

This book is a thing that only ever lands on two marks when you look, and the look itself as an act that forces a mark. Stay for the two marks, the look that forces one of them, and a click that counts as one arrival.

The long school walk, the constants table, and the first course door are in `LINK_INDEX.md`. A constant, an ISBN, or an equation body that page did not show is unverified.

Counts below are invented lab runs for this book, or arithmetic you can redo by hand.

## 1. A two-state thing

Nia watches a thin stream of atoms fly through a gap between two magnet poles. The stream is quiet. On a screen past the gap she expected a smear, the way a spray of sand would fan. She gets two spots. One spot sits high. One spot sits low. Nothing lands in the middle.

She counts an invented run of 80 atoms. 48 hit the high spot. 32 hit the low spot. $48 + 32 = 80$. The high share is $48 \div 80 = 0.6$. The low share is $32 \div 80 = 0.4$. Put the shares back: $0.6 \times 80 = 48$, and $0.4 \times 80 = 32$. The middle bin is empty. A third mark did not appear.

A thrown pebble can sit at any height on a wall. This stream will not. After the magnet, each atom answers with one of two marks and no other. Ordinary English: a thing with two answers. People name that a **two-state** thing.

Let $p$ be the high share. Then $p = n_\text{high} / n$. Fill $n_\text{high} = 48$ and $n = 80$: $p = 0.6$. The low share is $1 - p = 0.4$. If a page writes a third share of 0.1 and still keeps 0.6 and 0.4, the three shares add to 1.1. That page has invented a third answer the screen did not give.

Mechanics will still tell you how a magnet pushes a moving charge if you open electromagnetism for charge and field, then mechanics for the push. Those packs own the force walk. This chapter owns the two spots. The living magnet strength, if you need a tesla, is a NIST or BIPM fetch. Unverified here as a digit.

**Check.** A new run of 70 atoms. 21 high, 49 low. $21 + 49 = 70$. High share $21 \div 70 = 0.3$. Low share $0.7$. Put-back: $0.3 \times 70 = 21$. Another: 55 atoms, 11 high, 44 low. Share $11 \div 55 = 0.2$. If someone draws a fan of sand across the same screen and calls the fan a two-state run, they counted a smear as two marks. The object of this chapter is the pair of spots, not the fan.

## 2. Both until you look

Rafi leaves the detector off and lets the same stream fly. He still has two doors past the magnet, high and low. He does not yet have two piles. Each atom is on the way. The screen has not clicked.

A pebble in flight already has one height at each time. Mechanics taught that. These atoms, before a look, do not already occupy one of the two spots. Both spots are still in play for each atom. The high door and the low door both still matter for what happens later. Ordinary English: both until you look. People name that **superposition**.

A weight for the high door, a weight for the low door. This book keeps those weights real so the arithmetic stays on paper. Let $a$ be the high weight and $b$ the low weight, with $a^2 + b^2 = 1$. The high share after many looks is $a^2$. The low share is $b^2$. Fill $a = 3/5$. Then $a^2 = 9/25$. So $b^2 = 1 - 9/25 = 16/25$, and $b = 4/5$. Put-back: $9/25 + 16/25 = 25/25 = 1$. For 25 invented looks, 9 high and 16 low is the share that matches those weights. $9 + 16 = 25$.

If Rafi later joins the two doors without looking, the two weights can add and cancel. A joined run can land where a single door would not. That is why "both" is not a polite way of saying "we have not peeked at a pebble that already chose." The unlooked atom is not a hidden pebble.

A page that sets $a = 3/5$ and $b = 3/5$ has $a^2 + b^2 = 18/25$, which is not 1. Refuse the page or rescale. A page that treats $a$ itself as the share has mixed a weight with a count of spots.

**Check.** New weights: $a = 5/13$. Then $a^2 = 25/169$. $b^2 = 144/169$, so $b = 12/13$. $25 + 144 = 169$. For 169 invented looks, 25 high and 144 low. Another: $a = 8/17$, $a^2 = 64/289$, $b^2 = 225/289$, $b = 15/17$. $64 + 225 = 289$. If you added $5/13$ to $12/13$ and called $17/13$ a share of looks, you added weights as if they were already counts. Square first, then count.

## 3. A measurement

Soren turns the detector on. One atom hits the high spot. The detector rings high. That atom is no longer both. The look did not read a label that was already glued on. The look forced one of the two marks, and the other mark is gone for that atom.

A second look, right away, on that same atom, rings high again. Soren does this on an invented stack of 200 first looks: 72 high, 128 low. $72 + 128 = 200$. He then looks a second time at each of the 72. All 72 ring high. $72 + 0 = 72$. The first look wrote the mark. The second look reads what the first look wrote.

Ordinary English: a look that forces a mark. People name that a **measurement**. This pack owns that walk. The measurement pack owns SI: the metre, the second, the kilogram, the sentences that fix those units. Open that pack for a unit sentence. Do not copy SI here. A BIPM brochure line is a fetch, or the line stays unverified.

Let $n$ be the number of first looks and $h$ the number that ring high. The high share is $h / n$. Fill $h = 72$, $n = 200$: $72 \div 200 = 0.36$. That 0.36 is a share of forced marks, not a hidden pebble fraction you could have read without forcing. Undo: $0.36 \times 200 = 72$.

If Soren looks high, then turns the magnet to a new angle, the next look is a new question. Chapter 6 takes that angle. If he looks high and then expects a low ring with no new angle, he is asking the same question to give a new answer. The second look in this chapter does not.

**Check.** A new stack of 150 first looks: 45 left, 105 right. $45 + 105 = 150$. Share left $45 \div 150 = 0.3$. Second look on the 45: 45 left, 0 right. Another: 88 first looks, 22 high, 66 low. $22 + 66 = 88$. Share $22 \div 88 = 0.25$. If a page says the 22 were already high before the detector rang, and the ring was a polite peek, that page has put the pebble back into chapter 1's flight. This chapter's object is the forcing.

## 4. A wave as a first look

Quinn opens two slits in a wall and puts a screen of tiny counters behind it. She sends one thing at a time. Each counter either clicks or does not. After many clicks the screen is not two piles behind two slits. It is a stripe of more clicks, then fewer, then more, then fewer.

Invented run: 200 clicks with both slits open. Center bin 48. Next bins 29 and 29. Next 17 and 17. Next 6 and 6. Far bins 8 and 8. $48 + 29 + 29 + 17 + 17 + 6 + 6 + 8 + 8 = 168$, and the leftover 32 sit in thinner bins she did not name. The named bins already show a rise and a fall twice. Close one slit: the stripes go. The same 200 clicks make one pile behind the open slit. Two open doors did a job one door cannot do.

Ordinary English: a walk of chance that can add and cancel. People name that a **wave** as a first look. MIT 8.04 introduces wave mechanics. The 8.04 landing page shows a split beam, one path through a potential and one free path of the same length, then a join that compares phase. That sketch is theirs. Quinn's 200 clicks are a different invented wall.

A repeat length on the wall is a toy **wavelength** in this chapter. A pattern that repeats every 8 marks, five times, covers $8 \times 5 = 40$ marks. Undo: $40 \div 8 = 5$. Shorter repeat, sharper motion as a first look. The living rule that ties a wavelength to a momentum is a constants fetch. Unverified here as a formula with a digit. Do not finish a remembered $h$ on this page.

Electromagnetism owns the field that moves as ordinary light in that pack. This chapter owns the click pattern when you send one thing at a time. Open the field pack if a radio wave is the object. Stay here if the object is the striped wall of clicks.

**Check.** A new pattern repeats every 10 marks. Seven repeats cover $10 \times 7 = 70$ marks. Put-back: $70 \div 10 = 7$. Another: both slits open, 120 clicks, center bin 32, neighbors 19 and 19. $32 + 19 + 19 = 70$ in the middle three bins. Close one slit and those three bins will not keep that rise-and-fall. If you added 32 to 10 and called 42 a wavelength, you added a count of clicks to a repeat length. Two objects.

## 5. A bound on how sharp

Bea tries to say both where a click happened and how hard the thing was moving. She narrows the slit so the "where" is a short interval. The motion spread on the far wall grows. She opens the slit so the motion looks calmer. The "where" interval grows.

A short clap has no clear pitch. A long pure tone has no clear "when." You can have a sharp time or a sharp pitch. You do not get both as sharp as you like. The click on Bea's wall is that kind of pair: a spread in where, a spread in how hard.

This book uses a toy bound so the arithmetic stays on paper. Let $\Delta x$ be the where-spread in toy length marks, $\Delta p$ the motion-spread in toy motion marks. The product $\Delta x \times \Delta p$ is not allowed below 6 in this toy. Fill $\Delta x = 2$. Then $\Delta p$ cannot sit under $6 \div 2 = 3$. A claim of $\Delta x = 2$ and $\Delta p = 2$ has product 4, which is under 6. Refuse it. Fill $\Delta x = 3$. Then $\Delta p$ cannot sit under 2. Product $3 \times 2 = 6$, allowed as a touch of the bound.

Ordinary English: a bound on how sharp both can be at once. People name that an **uncertainty** bound as a first look. The living bound uses a constant from the NIST CODATA 2022 values. This pack does not print that row. Open the constants door, or write unverified. Do not finish a remembered $\hbar$ here.

If Bea measures where and then measures motion, the second look is a new forcing. Chapter 3 already taught that a look writes a mark. The bound is why two sharp marks of this pair cannot both be written at once.

**Check.** $\Delta x = 12$. Then $\Delta p$ cannot sit under $6 \div 12 = 0.5$. Product $12 \times 0.5 = 6$. A new case: $\Delta x = 4$, $\Delta p = 1.5$. Product 6, a touch of the toy bound. Another: $\Delta x = 4$ and $\Delta p = 1$. Product 4, under 6. Refuse. If a hallway poster quotes the living inequality with a remembered constant, open NIST, or write unverified. The 6 in this chapter is a toy floor, not a CODATA row.

## 6. Spin

Jules turns the magnet a little, then a lot. Along the bench the two spots stay two spots. He rotates the magnet a quarter turn. Still two spots, now on a new axis. He never gets a third spot. He never gets a smear at an angle.

A top on a table can tilt to any angle. This stream will not print a tilt. The screen prints two marks for whichever way Jules points the magnet. Ordinary English: a two-state that the magnet sees. People name that **spin** as a first look. The word "spin" is a handle. A top you could watch going around is a different object.

Invented run, magnet along the bench: 90 atoms, 66 up, 24 down. $66 + 24 = 90$. Share up $66 \div 90 = 0.733\ldots$ to three places 0.733, which is $66/90 = 11/15$. Put-back: $(11/15) \times 90 = 66$. Jules then takes atoms that already rang up, and asks the quarter-turn question. New invented split: 47 along the new "up," 43 along the new "down." $47 + 43 = 90$. The first look did not lock the second axis. It locked the axis he asked.

If he prepares up along the bench and asks the bench question again, chapter 3 returns: 90 up, 0 down. Same question, same mark. New axis, new shares.

Electromagnetism owns a magnet as a field object. Open that pack for current and field. This chapter owns the two spots on a chosen axis. A living value of the electron magnetic moment is a NIST constants fetch. Unverified here as a digit.

**Check.** A new stack of 120 atoms along the bench: 90 up, 30 down. $90 + 30 = 120$. Share up $90 \div 120 = 0.75$. Put-back: $0.75 \times 120 = 90$. Another: 40 atoms already up, asked again along the same axis: 40 up, 0 down. A third: 40 already up, asked on the quarter-turn axis, invented 21 and 19. $21 + 19 = 40$. If you averaged 0.75 with a pebble's tilt of 20 degrees and wrote 20.75, you mixed a share of spots with a top's angle. Stop.

## 7. A photon

Kenzo sits with a detector so quiet it rings once per arrival. He turns a lamp down. The wall does not grow dim as a smooth sheet. The rings get rarer. Each ring is the same kind of click. Half a ring does not happen.

Invented lamp: 11 clicks, each carrying 7 toy energy units. Total $11 \times 7 = 77$. Undo: $77 \div 7 = 11$. If the energy had arrived as a smear, 77 could have shown up as a half-click of 3.5. The detector never rings 3.5. Ordinary English: a packet of light. People name that a **photon**.

A bluer lamp in this toy carries more per click. Invented: 8 clicks at 9 units each, total 72. A redder lamp: 8 clicks at 3 units each, total 24. Same number of clicks, different pile per click. How many waves per second is a frequency. The living factor that turns a frequency into an energy per packet is a NIST constants fetch. Unverified here as a digit. Do not finish a remembered Planck row.

Electromagnetism owns charge, current, and the field that moves as ordinary light. Open that pack if a radio wave or a circuit is the object. This pack owns the photon walk: one click, one packet. NIST's home page, this sitting, listed work on detecting faint photons among its news. That headline is a host note, not a lab number for Kenzo's 77.

Let $E$ be the toy energy of one click and $N$ the number of clicks. The pile is $N E$. Fill $N = 11$, $E = 7$: pile 77. Fill pile 52 and $E = 4$: $N = 52 \div 4 = 13$. Thirteen clicks, no leftover.

**Check.** 13 clicks at 4 units: pile 52. Put-back: $52 \div 13 = 4$. A new case: pile 72 at 9 units each. $N = 8$. Another: 8 clicks at 3 units, pile 24. If a page splits one click into 1.5 and 1.5 to make the arithmetic prettier, it has broken the object. The object is the whole ring. If a page prints a joule-second constant from a poster, open NIST, or write unverified.

## 8. Two things that stay linked

Willa makes two atoms from one source and sends them to two benches. Nia sits at the left bench. Piet sits at the right. They pick a magnet axis after the atoms have left. They look.

Invented run of 40 pairs, same question on both benches: 40 disagree. 0 agree. $40 + 0 = 40$. Every left-up is a right-down. Every left-down is a right-up. The pairs were not two independent coins. Two independent half-and-half coins would not lock 40 out of 40.

They pick different axes. New invented split on the same 40 pairs: 22 disagree, 18 agree. $22 + 18 = 40$. The lock was for the same question. A new pair of questions is a new share.

Ordinary English: two things that stay linked after they part. People name that **entanglement** as a first look. Each side is still a two-state thing. The new object is the pair. You cannot write a high-share for the left and a high-share for the right and multiply, then get the 40-out-of-40 lock. Independent shares do not make that lock.

If Willa looks at the left and rings up, the right is already down for that same question, even if Piet has not looked yet. Piet's look will force the down mark when he asks that same question. Chapter 3 still holds on each bench. The link is extra.

A hidden pair of gloves, left and right in two boxes, is already decided before anyone opens a box. These 40 pairs are not that. Willa can choose the axis after the atoms leave. Gloves do not change which question they answer. These pairs do.

**Check.** A new source, 28 pairs, same question: 28 disagree, 0 agree. Another: 16 pairs, same question, 16 disagree. A third, different axes, 16 pairs: 9 disagree, 7 agree. $9 + 7 = 16$. If you took left share 0.5 and right share 0.5, multiplied, and called 0.25 the chance they disagree, you treated the pair as two coins. The 28-out-of-28 lock refuses that product.

## 9. An equation as a named door

Oskar copies a heading from a course page: a rule that walks a wave of chance from one time to the next. MIT 8.04 names Schrödinger's equation in one dimension and in three dimensions as what the course introduces. OpenStax Volume 3 is the other long door. This sitting did not receive an equation body from the OpenStax details page. Unverified as a printed line. Do not finish a remembered $i\hbar$ from memory.

Ordinary English: a named rule that walks the wave. People name that rule **Schrödinger's equation**. This chapter owns the door, not a dump of the symbols. The constants inside a printed form live at NIST. Open that door for a digit.

A first look you can do on paper: a thing trapped between two walls, a **box**. Only some standing waves fit. Number them $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$ in this toy. Let the toy energy be $n^2$. Then the allowed pile marks are 1, 4, 9, 16. Fill $n = 3$: energy 9. Fill $n = 2$: energy 4. A jump from $n = 1$ to $n = 2$ costs $4 - 1 = 3$. A jump from $n = 2$ to $n = 3$ costs $9 - 4 = 5$. A jump from $n = 3$ to $n = 4$ costs $16 - 9 = 7$. Undo a cost of 5: that cost is the 2-to-3 jump in this toy.

Until you look, the thing in the box can be a mix of $n = 1$ and $n = 2$, both weights still in play, chapter 2. A look for energy then forces 1 or 4, never 2.5. The named equation is the door that walks those weights in time. 8.04 is the course that introduces that walk. 8.05 and 8.06 continue it.

If a page gives energy 2.5 in this box as an allowed standing mark, it has put a mark between the rungs. Refuse it, or say you left this toy. A living hydrogen spectrum is a fetch, not this $n^2$ ladder.

**Check.** $n = 5$ in the same toy, if the box allows it: energy 25. Jump from $n = 4$ to $n = 5$ costs $25 - 16 = 9$. A new case: $n = 1$ energy 1, $n = 4$ energy 16, cost 15. Another: a mix of $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ until a look, then a ring of 1 or a ring of 4. If the ring is 2.5, the look did not match this box. If a page prints the full named equation with a remembered constant, open 8.04 or Volume 3 or NIST, or write unverified.

## 10. A check

A poster in a hallway prints a Planck digit with no host. That digit does not travel. Open the NIST constants door, or write unverified. An invented share of 80 atoms does travel on paper, because you can redo the divide.

When a number must travel:

1. Name the object: two spots, both until a look, a forcing look, a striped wall of clicks, a bound on two spreads, two spots on an axis, a click of light, a linked pair, or a named walk in time.
2. Say whether the number is an invented run for this book, or a claimed living constant, or a claimed SI sentence.
3. Redo the arithmetic: shares that add to 1, $a^2 + b^2 = 1$, second look equals first look on the same question, $\Delta x \times \Delta p$ against the toy floor 6, $N E$ for clicks, $n^2$ for the box.
4. If the number is a constant, a unit, or a printed equation body, open NIST, BIPM, OpenStax Volume 3, or 8.04.
5. If the door and a remembered line disagree, believe the door. If the door will not open, write unverified and stop building on it.
