# A plant as a system

Nia's runner bean climbs a string on the south wall at 9 Larch Walk. This book is a plant as a system.

The object is that bean: mix in a tub, roots in the dark, a stem on a string, leaves in the light, later flowers and pods. This pack owns the leaf, the root, the flower, and the seed as walks. Ecology owns a living place. Genetics owns a copied trait. Biology is the survey.

NCBI Taxonomy, fetched for this book, says the Taxonomy Database is a curated classification and nomenclature for organisms in the public sequence databases, and that this currently represents about 10% of the described species of life on the planet. USDA PLANTS, Smithsonian, and OpenStax Biology 2e are doors in `LINK_INDEX.md`. A plant-count that did not appear on a fetched page is unverified.

## 1. A plant as a system

The tub sits on a crate against the brick. A stem as thick as a pencil at the base runs up a 2.7 metre string to a hook under the eaves. Fourteen green plates hang off the stem. Under the mix, white threads hold the tub from tipping when Nia brushes past. On Tuesday eight red openings stand out from the vine. None of those parts works as the plant by itself.

People name a living body that stays in place, builds its food with light, and keeps green working parts a **plant**. The whole of Nia's bean, roots through flowers, is the **system** this book walks: parts that keep one life going only when they stay joined.

Cut the stem at the crate and put the top in a jar of water on the kitchen sill. The fourteen plates still look green at noon. By Friday they hang. The jar has no mix and no white threads making new drinkers. The cut top is a piece. It is not the system that was on the wall.

Ivo's squash at allotment plot 14 is another system of the same kind: mix, roots, a thick runner on the soil, huge plates, later a fruit. The dandelion in the kerb at Larch and Fennel is a third. Three bodies. Three systems. Same jobs, different sizes.

A houseplant Cal keeps in a north room has six plates and no flower. Still a plant. The missing flower does not kick it out of the group. A dry stick in the same pot, left from last year's mint, does no jobs. The stick is not the system.

On day 29 the bean has used 1.9 metres of the 2.7 metre string. $2.7 - 1.9 = 0.8$ metres of string still empty above the tip. The tip is still building the system. The empty string is not extra plant. It is room the plant has not taken yet.

**Check.** Name the parts you can point at without a book: mix, white threads, stem, plates, red openings. If you pointed only at the plates, you named a piece. New case: the kitchen jar on Friday. Write what is missing that the wall still has. A third case: Cal's six plates and no flower. Still a plant. If you waited for a flower before you would use the word, you made the Tuesday openings a rule they are not.

## 2. A leaf

On day 29 Nia counts fourteen green plates on the string. One full plate, laid on paper, runs 9.6 centimetres from stem-join to tip and 7.5 centimetres across the widest point. A box that size is $9.6 \times 7.5 = 72.0$ square centimetres. The plate is smaller than the box because the edges curve in. The 72.0 is a ceiling, not a measured area.

Each plate is a **leaf**. The name is a handle for the flat green tool that faces the light. The join where the leaf meets the stem is a **node**. Nia can count nodes with a finger. She does not need a second book for that count.

A younger plate near the tip is 4.1 centimetres by 3.0 centimetres. Box: $4.1 \times 3.0 = 12.3$ square centimetres. Same job, less surface. Fourteen leaves are not fourteen copies of 72.0. Adding $14 \times 72.0 = 1008$ square centimetres would pretend every leaf filled a large box. Three of the fourteen are still small. Write the boxes you actually taped.

Turn one full leaf over. The top is darker. The underside is paler, with a branching pattern you can feel as ridges. Those ridges are **veins**. Water uses them as a path. Sugar the leaf builds uses them as a path the other way. Inner pipe names and a counted rate of flow are unverified here. The OpenStax details page did not print a rate.

Hold the leaf to the south sky at 11:00. Light comes through a little. The green is the working stuff. People name that green working stuff **chlorophyll** when they want a handle. How many nanometres of light it takes, and any absorption peak, did not appear on the four pages. Unverified.

What the leaf does with light, water, and air, when it builds sugar, is the act people name **photosynthesis**. Ordinary words first: the leaf takes in air and water, uses the day's light, and builds a store the rest of the plant can spend. Air the leaf puts out is part of the same act. The exact molecule count, and any sugar-per-hour number, did not appear on the OpenStax details page. Unverified. Do not finish an equation from a poster.

Ivo pinches one squash leaf so it hangs torn. The rest of the vine stays up. One leaf is a tool. Losing one tool is not losing the system. Losing every leaf is a different case. Then the sugar act stops until new plates open.

A leaf in the shade of the eaves, the top 0.4 metres of the vine, is thinner and paler than a leaf at 1.1 metres that takes the full south light. Same plant, two light jobs. Chapter 6 owns the hours on the wall.

**Check.** Tape a leaf. Write length, width, and the box product. If you wrote 72.0 for a 4.1 by 3.0 leaf, you used the wrong plate. New case: three leaves taped at 9.6 by 7.5, 8.0 by 6.2, and 4.1 by 3.0. Boxes: $9.6 \times 7.5 = 72.0$, $8.0 \times 6.2 = 49.6$, $4.1 \times 3.0 = 12.3$. Do not add those three and divide by three and call the result "the leaf." A third case: the torn squash leaf. The vine still has other plates. Write the torn one as damaged, not as the whole plant.

## 3. A root

Nia tips the tub on day 40 over a tray. Mix falls. A pale tangle holds the shape of the tub, about 22 centimetres across. One thicker white line runs 18 centimetres down from the old seed coat, now a flake. Finer lines fill the rest. The tangle smells of wet earth, not of the red flowers.

Those underground lines are **roots**. The job you can see without a lab is two-fold: hold, and drink. Hold: the tub does not dump when Nia brushes the stem. Drink: mix that was wet at 07:00 is lighter the next morning because water left through the plant. Chapter 6 owns the pour. This chapter owns the drinkers.

The fine felt on the mix, almost hair, is where most of the drinking happens. You will not count those hairs on a kitchen table. A count of root hairs is unverified here. What you can write is the 18 centimetre line and the 22 centimetre ball.

Press a finger into the mix at the wall of the tub. Hard. Roots have run out of room. People name that packed state **root-bound** when the drinkers circle the pot instead of spreading. Ivo's squash in plot 14 has a bed 1.2 metres across. The squash drinkers can walk. Nia's drinkers walk in a circle.

The dandelion in the kerb has a single thick root that Nia cannot pull with two hands. 1.2 centimetres of grit at the surface. The thick root goes farther than the grit. A tub bean and a kerb dandelion both drink. The kerb plant holds a crack. The tub plant holds a crate.

Cut every leaf and leave the roots. The drinkers still sit in wet mix. They have nothing above to spend the water on in the sugar act, and no new plates on the way unless a bud you missed is waiting. Cut every root and leave the leaves in the tub. The plates wilt by evening. The drinkers were the intake.

Cal pot-washes a dead mint and finds a brown tangle, dry, that breaks. That tangle is old root, no longer drinking. Colour and snap are the check you have without a lab. White and flexible: still a drinker on this bean. Brown and brittle: not.

**Check.** After the tip-out, write 18 centimetres for the thick line and 22 centimetres for the ball. If you wrote the 2.7 metre string as a root length, you measured the air part. New case: Ivo's 1.2 metre bed against Nia's 22 centimetre ball. Same job, different room. A third case: the kerb dandelion you cannot pull. The hold is working. Do not call the 1.2 centimetre grit the full root.

## 4. A flower

Tuesday morning eight red openings stand off the vine, each about 2.2 centimetres from base to tip. A bee lands on one, works, and leaves with yellow dust on its legs. Thursday three of the eight have dropped on the crate. Five remain. $8 - 3 = 5$. At the bases of two dropped openings, a small green swelling has started.

Each red opening is a **flower**. The name is a handle for the short-lived part whose job is to get dust to the place that can become a seed. The yellow dust is **pollen**. The swelling that stays after the red parts fall is the start of a **fruit**. On this bean the fruit is a pod. Chapter 5 owns the seeds inside it.

Nia does not need a Latin family name to count what she can see: coloured flaps, a place a bee can land, a centre, a base that may swell. Inner part names, a petal formula, and a timed bee visit as a required number did not appear on the four pages. Unverified. Count the openings. Watch which bases swell.

Two openings swell. Three drop and leave a dry scar and no swelling. The flower can fail. No bee, or a cold night, or a dry tub, and the red part was a try that did not become a pod. Eight openings are not eight pods. Write the swellings.

Ivo's squash flowers are larger and sit on the soil among the plates. Some sit on a tiny fruit already. Some sit only on a stem and wither. Same job as the bean's red openings. Different shape. The kerb dandelion opens a yellow head made of many small flowers packed as one. You can still watch dust and later a seed clock. Do not steal the dandelion's clock count from memory. Count it, or write unverified.

A flower is not the plant. Tuesday's eight openings are one day's try. The fourteen leaves and the 18 centimetre root are still the standing jobs. People who meet a plant only as a bouquet meet the try, not the system.

Cal's north-room plant has no flower in June. It still makes leaves. Flowering can wait. A plant that never flowers in its whole life in that pot still had a system of leaf and root. Seeds then have to come from somewhere else, or not at all.

**Check.** Tuesday 8, Thursday 5 still open, 2 swellings. Write $8 - 3 = 5$ for the drop, and 2 for the future pods you can already see. If you wrote 8 pods, you counted tries as results. New case: Ivo finds 11 squash flowers at dawn and 4 swellings at dusk. 11 is not 4. A third case: the dandelion head. Count the yellow pack as many small flowers if you can split them with a pin. If you cannot, write one head and stop. Do not invent a floret number.

## 5. A seed

Four pods hang by day 62. Nia opens the first when it rattles. Six dry beans sit in a row, each with a pale scar where it held the pod wall. Three of them, on a kitchen scale that reads to 0.01 grams, weigh 0.42 grams, 0.38 grams, and 0.51 grams. $0.42 + 0.38 + 0.51 = 1.31$ grams for those three. The other three stay in a cup, unweighed. Unweighed is not zero.

Each dry bean is a **seed**. The name is a handle for the packed next plant, plus a store of food, plus a coat. Soak one 0.51 gram seed in a saucer overnight. In the morning it is swollen and the coat has split. A pale root tip is out. That tip is the next system's first drinker. The store in the seed feeds that tip until a leaf can face the light.

The other three pods, still on the vine, hold 5, 7, and 4 seeds Nia can feel as lumps. $6 + 5 + 7 + 4 = 22$ seeds from four pods if every lump is a full seed. Feel is a hint. Open them to confirm, or write the 5, 7, and 4 as unverified counts until the pods rattle.

Plant the 0.38 gram seed 3 centimetres down in a second tub. Keep the 0.42 gram seed in a dry jar. After twelve days the planted one has two small plates above the mix. The jar seed still weighs near 0.42 grams and shows no plates. Same starting object. Water and mix decided which one became a system this month.

How a trait runs from Nia's vine to the seedling lives in the genetics pack. Name that door and stop. This chapter owns the packed body, the store, the coat, and the count.

A seed is not a soil. Drop the 0.51 gram soaked seed on the crate, no mix. The root tip dries by afternoon. The store was not enough without a drink and a hold. Mix is part of the next system. The seed is the start, not the whole.

Ivo saves squash seed on a plate on the shed shelf. Twenty-eight seeds from one fruit. He does not plant 28 vines in plot 14. He plants 4. $28 - 4 = 24$ stay in a tin. A seed count is not a plant count until mix, water, and light are spent on them.

**Check.** Four pods, 6 opened, 5 and 7 and 4 still lumps. Write 6 as a seen count. Write 22 only if you are willing to mark 16 of them as felt, not seen. New case: 0.42 and 0.38 and 0.51 grams. The 0.51 gram seed is not "the seed" for the other two. A third case: twelve days, two plates in the second tub, jar seed unchanged. If you called both of them plants, you named a dry store as a system.

## 6. Water and light

At 07:00 Nia pours 240 millilitres from a jug into the mix. At 07:20, 70 millilitres still sit in the saucer under the tub. $240 - 70 = 170$ millilitres went into the mix. The leaves are already lifted from the dawn droop. The 70 millilitres in the saucer did not enter the plant. It is spare.

Water in this chapter is the pour you can measure, the drink the roots take, and the loss you can weigh. Light is the hours the south wall actually receives. Together they feed the leaf's sugar act. Inner rates, a watt per square metre, and a millilitre-per-hour transpiration table did not appear on the four pages. Unverified. Use the jug and the clock.

The next morning the tub plus mix plus plant weighs 11.81 kilograms on the crate scale. Yesterday after the pour, once the saucer was emptied back into the jug, the same pile was 12.00 kilograms. $12.00 - 11.81 = 0.19$ kilograms gone. That 0.19 kilograms left as water through leaves and as a little extra evaporation from the mix surface. Do not split the 0.19 into leaf versus surface without a second measurement. Write the loss.

On 12 June the brick at 9 Larch Walk takes direct sun from 09:20 to 16:50. From 09:20 to 16:20 is 7 hours. Add 30 minutes. 7 hours 30 minutes of direct sun on that brick. The eaves cut the top 0.4 metres of the vine out of the last hour. The lower leaves keep the sun. Light on a plant has a shape, the way shelter has a shape.

Move the crate to the north side of the house for three days as a test. Same 170 millilitre pours. The north brick gets no direct sun those three June days. New plates at the tip come paler and smaller. The sugar act ran on less light. Put the crate back south.

Two days with no pour, late July. At 15:00 the fourteen plates hang. Nia pours 400 millilitres. Forty minutes later the plates have lifted. Wilt was the system short of water, not a dead plant. If she had waited until the stem went slack and brown, that would have been a different check.

Ivo waters plot 14 with a can that holds 8 litres, two cans on a dry Sunday, $2 \times 8 = 16$ litres on a bed 1.2 metres across. Nia's 170 millilitres is a different body of mix. Do not copy 16 litres onto the tub. Do not copy 170 millilitres onto the allotment.

**Check.** 240 poured, 70 in the saucer, 170 in the mix. If you wrote 240 as what the roots drank, you counted the saucer as the plant. New case: 12.00 kilograms to 11.81 kilograms, 0.19 kilograms gone. A third case: 7 hours 30 minutes on the south brick, and the top 0.4 metres losing the last hour. Write the hours for the leaf you mean, not "the sun" as one number for the whole street.

## 7. A name

Ivo writes "runner bean" on a stick and pushes it into Nia's tub. A seed packet in the kitchen says "scarlet runner" and, in smaller type, *Phaseolus coccineus*. Three strings of words. One vine on the wall.

People need a **name** so two readers can point at the same kind of plant after the stick rots. A common name is the stick: runner bean. A **binomial** is the two-word Latin form on the packet. Whether USDA PLANTS treats *Phaseolus coccineus* as the accepted name for this vine, and what symbol it would print, did not appear on the plants.usda.gov page this book received. Unverified. Open that door. Do not finish a symbol from memory.

NCBI Taxonomy, opened for this book, is a curated classification and nomenclature for all of the organisms in the public sequence databases. That index currently represents about 10% of the described species of life on the planet. The 10% is the load-bearing official number this chapter has. It is a share of described life in that database, not a share of plants on Larch Walk, and not a count of runner beans. Whether this vine's binomial has a Taxonomy identifier, and what the identifier is, takes the Taxonomy Browser. Inner identifiers are unverified here.

Ranks in that index can change. NCBI published an update that new ranks replaced superkingdom. The inner table of ranks is on that door, not on this page. Unverified as a list. What you need in this chapter is the idea: a name sits in a nest of broader names, and the nest is a curated object, not a memory.

Two plants, one stick. Ivo also wrote "runner bean" on a stick in plot 14 next to a French bean he bought in error. The sticks match. The flowers do not. The French bean's openings are smaller and paler. A shared common name can lie. The packet binomial, if it is the one USDA PLANTS would accept, is the tighter handle. Confirm on the door.

Cal says "bean" for Nia's vine, for the dry seeds in the jar, and for a lentil soup. Three objects. One short word. Ask which object. The system on the wall, the packed next plant, and the food in the bowl are not one row in NCBI Taxonomy.

**Check.** Write three columns: stick name, packet binomial, door. Fill USDA PLANTS as unverified until the live page speaks. Fill NCBI as about 10% of described species in that database, not as a taxid for this vine. New case: two sticks that both say "runner bean," two different flowers. The stick failed. A third case: someone says there are a million plant species on Earth. That million did not appear on the four pages. Unverified. Do not average it with 10%.

## 8. A habitat

The south wall at 9 Larch Walk is brick, warm after noon, dry under the eaves for 0.6 metres out from the wall. Rain on a still day never hits the tub. Rain on a south wind does. The crate sits in that strip. The vine lives there, not in "the city" as a whole.

That lived strip is the plant's **habitat** in this book: the light, the wet, the mix, the wind, the brick heat, the hours from chapter 6. How a living place is a web of who eats whom, a cycle of stuff, a population, a disturbance, lives in the ecology pack. Name that door and stop. Stay here when the task is what this one plant is using.

Same binomial, two habitats. Ivo's plot 14 is open sky, 1.2 metres of bed, cans of 8 litres, no eaves. Nia's tub is 34 centimetres across, 28 centimetres deep, 170 millilitres on a morning pour, 7 hours 30 minutes of direct sun in that June day. Copying plot 14's 16 litres onto the tub would drown it. Copying the tub's 170 millilitres onto plot 14 would leave the squash short.

The kerb dandelion's habitat is 1.2 centimetres of grit, compacted, with runoff from the pavement after rain and almost no mix. It still flowers. The habitat is poor by garden standards and enough for that plant. "Good soil" is not a door number. It is a comparison you have to write in centimetres and wetness.

A willow on the canal bank has wet feet all year. Nia cannot put the bean tub in the canal. The bean's drinkers rot in standing water in a clogged saucer after three days of 70 millilitres left sitting. Habitat includes what kills. Empty the saucer.

Move the crate 4 metres east along Larch Walk, still south-facing, but now under a balcony that cuts sun after 12:10. Direct sun from 09:20 to 12:10 is 2 hours 50 minutes. The June 7 hours 30 minutes is gone. New plates will tell you, paler, in a week. The plant did not change binomial. The habitat did.

**Check.** Write the strip: eaves 0.6 metres, tub 34 by 28 centimetres, south sun 7 hours 30 minutes on 12 June, rain only on a south wind. If you wrote "sunny garden," you used a mood, not a habitat. New case: the balcony cut to 2 hours 50 minutes. Same vine, new light. A third case: plot 14's 16 litres against the tub's 170 millilitres. Two habitats. Two pours. Do not average them.

## 9. A fossil as a first look

Wren's school drawer holds a piece of dark stone, 8.1 centimetres by 5.4 centimetres. A paler print sits on it, 6.3 centimetres long, split into eleven small leaflets. No colour. No mix. No water. You cannot pot it.

That print is a **fossil** as this book uses the word: a trace of a plant that died, kept in stone. First look only. How long ago, what period name, what millimetres of sediment, did not appear on the Smithsonian page this book received. The Smithsonian body did not arrive. Collection counts and ages are unverified. Open naturalhistory.si.edu. Do not finish a million-year figure from a label in memory.

What the print keeps is shape: length 6.3 centimetres, eleven leaflets, a mid-line. What it does not keep is the sugar act, the 170 millilitre pour, the wilt and lift, the red flower, the 0.51 gram seed. A fossil is a plant as a system with almost every job stripped, one outline left.

Compare Nia's living leaf, 9.6 by 7.5 centimetres, green, veined, lifting after a pour, with Wren's 6.3 centimetre print. You may guess the print was a leaf. You may not guess the plant's habitat from the print alone. The stone is not the canal and not the south wall.

NCBI Taxonomy has a door for extinct organisms. That door was named on the Taxonomy home page. Inner extinct names and counts were not in the home-page body this book used. Unverified. The 10% figure is for organisms in the public sequence databases, which is a different pile from a school-drawer print.

Ivo finds a brown leaf, dry, between two pages of a telephone book, from last autumn. Pressed. Not stone. Not a fossil in the sense Wren's print is. The telephone-book leaf still has brown tissue you can crumble. Wren's print is mineral where a leaf was. Keep the two words apart.

**Check.** Tape the stone: 8.1 by 5.4 centimetres. Tape the print: 6.3 centimetres, eleven leaflets. Write "shape kept, jobs not kept." If you wrote a year, mark it unverified. New case: Nia's 9.6 by 7.5 living leaf next to the print. Two objects. A third case: the telephone-book leaf. Pressed, not fossil. ## 10. A check
