# Counting, place, share, and measure

If you can read this sentence, you can begin. Each chapter adds one move. Paper and a pencil do the work. The app calculator may check a sum after you have tried it.

This pack is general education for EasyLM. License AGPL-3.0-or-later.

---

## 1. Names for how many

Hold out your fingers. Each finger is one. Two hands are ten ones.

We need names so we can talk when the pile is bigger than our hands. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine. After nine we have run out of single marks. We bundle ten ones and call that bundle **ten**. Ten tens is **one hundred**. Ten hundreds is **one thousand**. Ten thousands is **ten thousand**. A thousand thousands is **one million**.

None is a pile too. The name for none is **zero**. Zero is how you say the empty hand.

Write a pile as a row of digits. The rightmost digit is the leftover ones. The next digit left is how many tens. The next is how many hundreds. The next is how many thousands.

243 is two hundreds, four tens, and three ones. Say it: two hundred forty-three.

40 is four tens and no leftover ones. 14 is one ten and four ones. The same two marks, 4 and 1, name different piles because they sit in different places. That chair-work is **place value**: the same mark, 2, means two, or twenty, or two hundred, depending on which place it sits in.

7,042 is seven thousands, no hundreds, four tens, and two ones. The comma is only for the eye. The number is seven thousand forty-two.

A bundle can be opened again. One ten is ten ones. One hundred is ten tens, or a hundred ones. The name did not change the pile. It grouped it.

**Check.** Write 508 in words. Five hundreds, no tens, eight ones. The zero is a place that must stay empty so the 5 stays in the hundreds place. Then write 5,080 in words. Five thousands, no hundreds, eight tens, and no ones. The two zeros are doing different jobs.

## 2. Putting piles together

You have 7 and you are given 5. Count on from 7: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12. The new pile is 12.

When a place fills past nine, you bundle. 8 ones and 5 ones are 13 ones, which is 1 ten and 3 ones. If you already had 4 tens, you now have 5 tens and 3 ones: 53.

The written move is **addition**. The sign is $+$. The result is the **sum**. Adding zero leaves the pile as it was: $12 + 0 = 12$.

Line the places. Ones under ones. Tens under tens. If you slide a digit one chair, you added a different pile.

```
  47
+ 36
  83
```

7 ones and 6 ones are 13: write 3, carry 1 ten. 4 tens and 3 tens and the carried ten are 8 tens.

Order does not change the sum. $47 + 36 = 36 + 47$. You may add three piles by adding any two first, then the third. $2 + 5 + 8$ is $7 + 8$ or $2 + 13$ or $10 + 5$. All three ways give 15.

A longer column, same rule.

```
  246
+ 378
  624
```

6 ones and 8 ones are 14: write 4, carry 1 ten. 4 tens and 7 tens and the carried ten are 12 tens: write 2, carry 1 hundred. 2 hundreds and 3 hundreds and the carried hundred are 6 hundreds.

$99 + 1$ fills the ones and the tens. 9 ones and 1 one are 10 ones: write 0, carry 1 ten. 9 tens and that ten are 10 tens: write 0, carry 1 hundred. The sum is 100. The new 1 sits in a place that was empty.

**Check.** $58 + 27$. 8 ones and 7 ones are 15: write 5, carry 1 ten. 5 tens and 2 tens and the carried ten are 8 tens. The sum is 85. Undo it by taking the added pile back off. $85 - 27$ should return 58. If it does not, a place was bundled wrong. Then do $246 + 378$ the other way: $378 + 246$. You should land on 624 again.

## 3. Taking away

You have 12 and you give away 5. Count backward, or think of 5 as the piece that was added to 7 to make 12. What remains is 7.

The written move is **subtraction**. The sign is $-$. The result is the **difference**. Subtracting zero leaves the pile: $12 - 0 = 12$. Taking the whole pile leaves zero: $12 - 12 = 0$.

If a place has too few, unbundle from the left. 30 take away 4: the ones place is empty, so open one ten into ten ones. Now you have 2 tens and 10 ones. 10 take away 4 is 6. Two tens remain. The difference is 26.

The same move answers "how many more." 81 is how many more than 47? That is $81 - 47$.

```
  81
- 47
  34
```

1 one is too small to take 7. Open one ten: 11 ones take away 7 is 4. 7 tens remain, take away 4 tens is 3 tens. The difference is 34. 34 more apples in the 81-pile than in the 47-pile.

A hole of zeros in the middle needs two opens. 200 take away 58. The ones and the tens are empty, so open one hundred into 10 tens, then open one of those tens into 10 ones. You now have 1 hundred, 9 tens, and 10 ones. 10 take away 8 is 2. 9 tens take away 5 tens is 4 tens. 1 hundred remains. The difference is 142.

If the pile you want to take is larger than the pile you have, stop. With the numbers in this book so far, you cannot take 9 from 4. Count the two piles again. You may have the subtraction written the wrong way.

**Check.** Put the taken pile back. $26 + 4$ must be 30. $34 + 47$ must be 81. $142 + 58$ must be 200. Then a new case: $70 - 23$. Open one ten so the ones are 10. 10 take away 3 is 7. 6 tens take away 2 tens is 4 tens. The difference is 47. Put 23 back on 47 and you should see 70.

## 4. Groups of the same size

Three bags of four apples is four, and four, and four. That repeated add is **multiplication**. The sign is $\times$. Three times four is twelve.

An array makes the same fact visible: three rows of four dots. You may also see four rows of three. Order does not change the count: $3 \times 4 = 4 \times 3$.

Times one leaves the pile: $7 \times 1 = 7$. Times zero is the empty pile: $7 \times 0 = 0$. There is nothing in seven empty bags.

A place-value shortcut: $7 \times 10 = 70$. Multiplying by ten slides every digit one place to the left and parks a zero in the ones place. Multiplying by one hundred slides two places: $7 \times 100 = 700$.

When a product is awkward, split one factor, multiply the pieces, and add. $7 \times 8$ is five eights and two eights. $5 \times 8 = 40$ and $2 \times 8 = 16$, and $40 + 16 = 56$. The split is legal because the seven groups were five groups and two groups of the same size.

$15 \times 12$ splits the 15: ten twelves and five twelves. $10 \times 12 = 120$ and $5 \times 12 = 60$. The product is 180.

$23 \times 4$ splits the 23: twenty fours and three fours. $20 \times 4 = 80$ and $3 \times 4 = 12$. The product is 92.

A rectangle of tiles is this count with a shape. Four tiles along one edge and three along the other holds 12 tiles. The count is the same product. Name what you counted: 12 tiles, not 12 of nothing.

**Check.** $6 \times 8$: six eights. $8 + 8 = 16$, plus 8 is 24, plus 8 is 32, plus 8 is 40, plus 8 is 48. Also $5 \times 8 = 40$ and one more 8 is 48. A new case: $7 \times 9$. Seven tens would be 70. Seven nines is seven less than that, 63. Another: $12 \times 11$. Twelve tens is 120. One more twelve is 132. Undo each product by sharing: 48 shared into 6 equal groups is 8.

## 5. Sharing

Twelve apples in three bags, the same number in each bag. How many per bag?

You are undoing multiplication. $3 \times \text{?} = 12$. The missing number is 4. The written move is **division**. The sign is $\div$. $12 \div 3 = 4$.

The same twelve apples can be read the other way: bags of three apples, how many bags? $12 \div 3$ is still 4. One reading asks how many in each group. The other asks how many groups. The arithmetic is the same. The sentence is not.

Sometimes the pile does not split evenly. 13 apples in three bags: each bag gets 4, and 1 apple is left over. We write $13 \div 3 = 4$ remainder 1. Later that leftover will become a fraction. Until then, name it as leftover so you do not pretend the bags were equal and empty-handed.

Zero apples in four bags is zero in each bag: $0 \div 4 = 0$. Sharing a pile into zero bags has no meaning. Do not write a number there.

$24 \div 4 = 6$. Picture A: four bags, six in each. Picture B: bags of four, six bags. $4 \times 6 = 24$ both ways.

$20 \div 6$: 6 goes into 20 three times, because $3 \times 6 = 18$, and 2 are left. 3 remainder 2.

$100 \div 8$: 8 goes into 100 twelve times, because $12 \times 8 = 96$, and 4 are left. 12 remainder 4.

**Check.** Multiply back. $4 \times 3 + 1 = 13$. $3 \times 6 + 2 = 20$. $12 \times 8 + 4 = 100$. A new case: $17 \div 5$. Five goes three times ($3 \times 5 = 15$), remainder 2. $3 \times 5 + 2 = 17$. Another: 36 shared equally among 9. $9 \times 4 = 36$, so 4 each, remainder 0.

## 6. Place and zero

Write 5, then a gap, then 8. Now write 58. Those two writings are not the same pile. 58 is five tens and eight ones. 5 8 with a spoken gap might have been five hundreds and eight ones. On paper the gap must be a mark, or the 5 slides.

Zero is that mark. It holds a place open. 508 is five hundreds, no tens, eight ones. 58 is five tens and eight ones. 580 is five hundreds, eight tens, and no ones. Take the zero out of 508 and you have claimed a different pile.

70 is seven tens. 7 is seven ones. 700 is seven hundreds. The zeros are not decoration. They keep the 7 in its chair.

The same chairs continue to the right of the ones, if you cut the whole into smaller equal pieces.

Cut a whole into ten equal pieces. One piece is $\frac{1}{10}$. We also write $0.1$. The dot, the **decimal point**, marks the ones place. The first chair to the right is tenths. The next is hundredths. The next is thousandths.

$0.25$ is 2 tenths and 5 hundredths, which is $\frac{25}{100}$, which is $\frac{1}{4}$.

$0.5$ is 5 tenths, which is $\frac{5}{10}$, which is $\frac{1}{2}$.

Money is already this system. One dollar is the whole. A dime is ten cents, written $0.10$. A cent is $0.01$.

Compare by lining up the dots. $0.19$ and $0.2$: write $0.2$ as $0.20$. 20 hundredths is more than 19 hundredths, so $0.2$ is the larger amount. $1.07$ and $1.7$: write $1.7$ as $1.70$. 170 hundredths is more than 107 hundredths.

Adding tenths and hundredths is the same as adding tens and ones. Line up the dots.

$0.3 + 0.12$. 3 tenths is 30 hundredths. 30 hundredths and 12 hundredths are 42 hundredths: $0.42$.

$1.4 + 0.75$. 4 tenths is 40 hundredths. 40 hundredths and 75 hundredths are 115 hundredths, which is 1 one and 15 hundredths. Add the ones: $1 + 1 = 2$. The sum is $2.15$.

Dividing by ten slides every digit one place to the right: $70 \div 10 = 7$, and $7 \div 10 = 0.7$. The reverse of the slide in chapter 4.

**Check.** What pile is 0.04? Four hundredths. What pile is 0.4? Four tenths, which is forty hundredths. They are not the same. Write $0.04 + 0.4$ with the dots lined up. Four hundredths and forty hundredths are forty-four hundredths: $0.44$. Then a new case: $2.06 + 0.9$. 9 tenths is 90 hundredths. 6 hundredths and 90 hundredths are 96 hundredths. The ones are 2. The sum is $2.96$.

## 7. Parts of a whole

Cut a loaf into two equal pieces. One piece is one of two. We write $\frac{1}{2}$.

The bottom number, the **denominator**, is how many equal pieces the whole was cut into. The top number, the **numerator**, is how many of those pieces you are holding.

$\frac{3}{4}$ is three pieces of a whole that was cut into four. $\frac{0}{4}$ is none of those pieces. $\frac{3}{3}$ is all of a whole that was cut into three, which is the whole loaf back again. $\frac{5}{4}$ is five pieces of a four-cut loaf: one whole loaf and one extra quarter.

Same amount, different cuts: $\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{6}$. You cut each piece smaller and hold more of them. The loaf did not change. That is an **equivalent fraction**. $\frac{4}{8}$ is the same as $\frac{1}{2}$ because four of eight equal pieces is half the loaf.

To add shares of the same whole, the cuts must match. $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}$: cut the half into two quarters. Two quarters plus one quarter is three quarters: $\frac{3}{4}$.

$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}$ needs a cut that both 2 and 3 share. Six equal pieces work. $\frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{6}$ and $\frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{6}$. Three sixths plus two sixths is five sixths: $\frac{5}{6}$.

$\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8}$. Cut each quarter into two eighths. Two eighths plus one eighth is three eighths: $\frac{3}{8}$.

"Of" a count is a share of that pile. $\frac{1}{4}$ of 20: cut 20 into four equal groups of 5. One of those groups is 5. $\frac{2}{3}$ of 12: cut 12 into three groups of 4. Two of those groups is 8.

**Check.** Draw the loaf. Shade. Count. Then a new case without the drawing in this book: $\frac{2}{5} + \frac{1}{5}$. Same cuts, so add the tops: three fifths, $\frac{3}{5}$. Another: $\frac{3}{4}$ of 16. 16 cut in four is 4. Three of those is 12. Multiply back to see it: $12 \div 16$ should be three quarters of the pile, and $3 \times 4 = 12$.

## 8. Per hundred

17 out of every 100 of the same whole. Write $\frac{17}{100}$, or $0.17$, or $17\%$. The third form is **percent**: per hundred.

100% is the whole pile. 50% is half, because 50 out of 100 is $\frac{1}{2}$. 25% is a quarter. 1% is one hundredth.

A shop takes 20% off a 50-dollar price. 20 out of 100 is $\frac{1}{5}$. One fifth of 50 is 10. The discount is 10 dollars. The new price is 40 dollars.

The same 20% of a different pile is a different amount. 20% of 10 dollars is 2 dollars. The percent was the same cut. The wholes were not.

10% of a pile is one tenth of it, because 10 out of 100 is $\frac{1}{10}$. 1% of 200 is 2, because one hundredth of 200 is 2. 15% of 80: 10% of 80 is 8, 5% is half of that, 4, and $8 + 4 = 12$.

25% of 36 is a quarter of 36, which is 9.

A rise of 10% on 40: 10% of 40 is 4. The new amount is 44. A fall of 10% on 40 lands on 36. Rising 10% and then falling 10% does not return to 40, because the second 10% is 10% of 44, which is 4.4, and $44 - 4.4 = 39.6$. The whole changed between the two cuts.

Do not add percents of different wholes as if they were the same pile. 10% of 100 and 10% of 10 are 10 and 1. The two percents are the same cut. The totals 11 and "20%" name different stories.

**Check.** 20% of 50 two ways: $0.20 \times 50 = 10$, and $\frac{20}{100} \times 50 = 10$. A new case: 15% of 60. 10% of 60 is 6. 5% is 3. The amount is 9. Another: 8% of 25. 1% of 25 is 0.25, so 8% is $8 \times 0.25 = 2$. A third: 100% of 17 is 17. 0% of 17 is 0.

## 9. Units

"3" is not a length. "3 meters" is.

A **unit** is the agreed stick. You may add 3 meters to 2 meters because both counts use the same stick. 3 meters plus 2 seconds is not a length and not a time. Convert before you add. 3 meters plus 50 centimeters: a centimeter is one of a hundred equal cuts of a meter, the same per-hundred cut you already have. 50 of those cuts is half a meter. The sum is 3.50 meters, not 53 of anything.

The living international stick for length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance, and light is the SI. BIPM publishes it as the SI Brochure, 9th edition (2019), updated in 2026, DOI 10.59161/AUEZ1291. Fetch that door. NIST's Metric Program, on the page fetched for this book, states that the SI is the preferred system of weights and measures for U.S. trade and commerce, and that the SI has seven defining constants and seven base units. NIST Special Publications 330 and 811 teach how to write those units.

The United States still uses miles, pounds, and gallons in daily talk. NIST says those customary units are based on the SI. On 1 January 2023 the U.S. survey foot was deemed obsolete, and the international foot superseded it. Fetch the NIST survey-foot door if a map or a deed still says "survey foot." Do not average two feet.

NIST keeps the US legal conversions on the SI units pages for length and mass. Fetch those doors when a number must travel from inches to millimetres, or from pounds to kilograms. This book does not finish those exact factors from memory.

Kilo in front of a unit is a thousand of that unit, the thousand you can already name. Milli is one of a thousand equal cuts. A kilogram is a thousand grams. A millimetre is one of a thousand equal cuts of a metre. Prefix names live on the NIST prefixes door.

A kitchen scale in grams is enough practice for mass. NIST keeps a metric kitchen as a door for cooking with that scale. Read the recipe from the door. Do not invent the gram count.

**Check.** A shelf is 2 feet 6 inches. 2 feet is 24 inches. Total 30 inches. That part is counting. The millimetre count for those 30 inches is a conversion: open the NIST length door, read the factor, multiply on paper, then let the app calculator check the product. A new case you can finish here: 1 metre 25 centimetres. 25 centimetres is 25 of the hundred cuts, which is $0.25$ metres. The length is $1.25$ metres. Another: 3 kilograms and 400 grams. 400 grams is 400 of the thousand cuts of a kilogram, which is $0.400$ kilograms. The mass is $3.400$ kilograms.

## 10. A check you can do

Estimate first. $198 + 203$ is near $200 + 200 = 400$. The careful sum is 401. If your careful sum is 301, a place was lost.

$51 \times 19$ is near $50 \times 20 = 1000$. The careful product: $51 \times 20 = 1020$, then take off one 51 because 19 is one less than 20. $1020 - 51 = 969$. If you wrote 510, you multiplied by 10 and stopped.

Last-digit check: ones of 8 plus ones of 3 are ones of 11, which ends in 1. The sum $198 + 203$ must end in 1. Ones of 7 times ones of 6 are ones of 42, which ends in 2. $47 \times 6$ must end in 2. It is 282.

Undo. Every add has a subtract. Every multiply has a divide. $14 \times 12 = 168$. Then $168 \div 12$ must return 14. $85 - 27 = 58$. Then $58 + 27$ must return 85.

When a line has more than one sign, people need a shared order so $2 + 3 \times 4$ is not a fight. Do the multiply and divide as they appear, left to right. Then do the add and subtract, left to right. Groups in parentheses go first, because they are a pile you wrap. $2 + 3 \times 4 = 2 + 12 = 14$. Not 20. If you mean the other reading, wrap it: $(2 + 3) \times 4 = 20$.

When the number is a constant or a legal conversion, fetch NIST or BIPM. Do not finish that sentence from memory. OpenStax Prealgebra 2e and Elementary Algebra 2e are free walkthroughs for more cases of the same moves. They are doors, not a second memory.

Paper first. The app calculator may check after you have a number. If the machine and the paper disagree, the paper is where you find the lost place.

**Check.** $28 + 35$. Ones 8 and 5 are 13, so the sum ends in 3. Estimate: $30 + 35 = 65$. Careful: 13 ones write 3, carry 1 ten, 2 tens and 3 tens and the carried ten are 6 tens. The sum is 63. Undo: $63 - 35 = 28$. A new case: $5 + 4 \times 2$. Multiply first: $4 \times 2 = 8$. Then $5 + 8 = 13$. If you added first you would wrap it: $(5 + 4) \times 2 = 18$. A third: 12% of 50. 10% is 5. 2% is 1. The amount is 6. Last-digit: 6 ends in 6, and $0.12 \times 50$ on paper is $12 \times 50$ hundredths, which is 600 hundredths, which is 6.
