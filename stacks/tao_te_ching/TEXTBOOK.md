# Tao Te Ching (道德經) — Lao Tzu (Laozi)

A foundational classical text of world philosophy and ontology. Preserved in two traditional books: Book I (The Tao, Chapters 1–37) and Book II (The Te, Chapters 38–81). Accompanied by intuitive conceptual physics framing in the pedagogical style of Brian Greene and Richard Feynman.

---

## 0. Intuitive Foundations & Modern Framing (The Greene / Feynman Method)

Before memorizing ancient Chinese terms or historical commentaries, it is essential to build intuitive physical and mechanical models of the dynamics Lao Tzu describes. In modern physics, computer science, and engineering, the principles of the *Tao Te Ching* re-emerge naturally as fundamental conservation laws, gradient flows, and optimization mechanics.

```
+----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                    INTUITIVE DYNAMICAL MODELS                                      |
+----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  GRADIENT DESCENT (Water)     | Water naturally seeks the lowest place. It expends zero coercive   |
|                               | energy yet erodes solid granite by moving along potential wells.   |
+-------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  FUNCTIONAL CAVITY (The Hub)  | A wheel turns around an empty center; a cup holds tea because of   |
|                               | its hollow interior. Utility lives in boundary constraints.        |
+-------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  UNCARVED POTENTIAL (Pu)      | An uncarved block of timber possesses maximal entropy and potential|
|                               | states. Carving it into spoons creates utility but limits forms.   |
+-------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  DYNAMIC EQUILIBRIUM (Dao)    | Bow-string mechanics: what is high is pulled down, what is low is  |
|                               | raised. Oscillators naturally seek zero-state equilibrium.         |
+-------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  NON-CONTENDING FLOW (Wu Wei) | Navigating with the river's current rather than paddling upstream.|
|                               | Impedance matching: zero reflected energy, maximum transmission.   |
+----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 1. Water as the Principle of Least Action
In classical Lagrangian mechanics, physical systems follow paths that minimize action ($\delta S = 0$). A light beam bends through glass not by fighting the medium, but by taking the path of least time (Fermat's Principle). Water does the same: it does not argue with the boulder; it contours around it, finding the local gravitational gradient until it accumulates sufficient hydrostatic pressure to wash past. Lao Tzu calls this the highest virtue (*Shang Shan Ruo Shui*): benefiting all things without contending, resting in places humans overlook.

### 2. The Mechanics of Emptiness: The Hub and the Vessel
Consider a wheel. Thirty spokes converge upon a single hub, but the carriage rolls only because of the empty hole at the center that accommodates the axle. Consider a bowl. We shape clay into a perimeter, but the bowl functions solely because of the empty void inside. Consider a room. We construct four walls and punch out doors and windows, but the room is habitable only because of the open air enclosed. Material substance provides the boundary condition; empty space provides the functional capability.

### 3. The Uncarved Block (*Pu*) and State Spaces
In computer science, a blank memory buffer has maximal degrees of freedom. The moment you declare a rigid schema, you specialize it: it can now perform one task efficiently, but it can no longer represent anything else. The uncarved block represents primal, unconditioned potential. The sage maintains access to the unconditioned root so as not to become trapped in brittle, over-specialized local minima.

### 4. Non-Contending Action (*Wu Wei*)
*Wu Wei* does not mean lethargy, passive resignation, or doing nothing. In electrical engineering, when the output impedance of a source matches the input impedance of a load, power transfer is maximized and signal reflection drops to zero. *Wu Wei* is behavioral impedance matching: observing the grain of the wood before striking with the chisel, moving with the momentum of the situation rather than exhausting oneself in friction against unyielding surfaces.

## Part I: Chapters 1–10 · The Origin, The Valley, and Non-Contention

### Chapter 1 · The Nameless and the Named (道可道非常道)
The Tao that can be spoken of is not the eternal Tao.  
The name that can be named is not the eternal name.  
The nameless is the beginning of heaven and earth.  
The named is the mother of ten thousand things.  
Ever desireless, one can see the mystery.  
Ever desiring, one can see the manifestations.  
These two spring from the same source but differ in name; this appears as darkness.  
Darkness within darkness. The gate to all mystery.

### Chapter 2 · The Balance of Opposites (天下皆知美之為美)
When the world knows beauty as beauty, ugliness arises.  
When the world knows good as good, evil arises.  
Being and non-being produce each other.  
Difficult and easy complement each other.  
Long and short define each other.  
High and low lean on each other.  
Tone and voice harmonize with each other.  
Front and back follow each other.  
Therefore the sage dwells in the action of non-action (*wu wei*) and teaches without words.  
Things arise and he does not turn them away; he produces but does not possess;  
He acts but does not boast; work is accomplished and he takes no credit.  
Because he takes no credit, it never leaves him.

### Chapter 3 · Quieting the People (不尚賢)
Not exalting the gifted keeps the people from competing.  
Not prizing rare treasures keeps the people from stealing.  
Not displaying desirable things keeps their hearts undisturbed.  
Therefore the sage governs by emptying their minds and filling their bellies,  
Weakening their ambitions and strengthening their bones.  
He keeps the people without cunning and without craving,  
And causes those with knowledge not to dare to force action.  
Practice non-action, and order will naturally prevail.

### Chapter 4 · The Unfathomable Fountain (道沖而用之或不盈)
The Tao is an empty vessel; used, it may never be filled.  
Deep and bottomless, like the ancestor of all things.  
It blunts sharp edges, unravels tangles, softens the glare, and mingles with the dust.  
Hidden deep, yet ever present.  
I do not know whose child it is; it seems to precede the first ancestor.

### Chapter 5 · Bellows of Heaven and Earth (天地不仁)
Heaven and earth are impartial; they treat all creatures like straw dogs.  
The sage is impartial; he treats all people like straw dogs.  
The space between heaven and earth—is it not like a bellows?  
Empty, yet never exhausted; the more it moves, the more comes forth.  
Many words lead to exhaustion; better to keep to the center.

### Chapter 6 · The Spirit of the Valley (谷神不死)
The spirit of the valley never dies; it is called the Mysterious Female.  
The gateway of the Mysterious Female is called the root of heaven and earth.  
Unbroken, like a gossamer thread, it lingers on.  
Draw upon it, and it never runs dry.

### Chapter 7 · Enduring as Heaven and Earth (天長地久)
Heaven and earth endure.  
The reason they endure is that they do not live for themselves;  
Therefore they can endure long.  
Thus the sage puts himself behind, yet finds himself ahead.  
He treats his person as outside, yet his person is preserved.  
Is it not because he has no self-interest that his self-fulfillment is realized?

### Chapter 8 · The Goodness of Water (上善若水)
The highest goodness is like water.  
Water benefits all things and does not contend with them.  
It dwells in the lowly places that all humans detest.  
Therefore it is close to the Tao.  
In dwelling, live close to the ground.  
In thinking, keep to the deep.  
In relationships, be kind and sincere.  
In words, be truthful.  
In governing, be just.  
In business, be competent.  
In movement, choose the right time.  
Because it does not contend, it never meets blame.

### Chapter 9 · Holding to the Full (持而盈之)
To hold until full is not as good as stopping in time.  
Pound and sharpen a blade too keen, and the edge cannot endure.  
Fill a hall with gold and jade, and no one can guard it.  
Wealth and pride bring about their own ruin.  
Withdraw when the work is done: this is the way of heaven.

### Chapter 10 · The Single Center (載營魄抱一)
Can you hold the body and soul in unified embrace without separating?  
Can you concentrate your vital breath and achieve gentleness like a newborn child?  
Can you cleanse your inner mirror until it is free from dust?  
Can you love the people and govern the state without resorting to cunning?  
Can you open and close heaven's gate playing the receptive female role?  
Can you understand all things while remaining unknowing?  
Give birth to them and nourish them.  
Produce without taking possession; act without presuming on merit; lead without ruling over.  
This is called profound virtue (*Xuan De*).

## Part II: Chapters 11–20 · The Hollow Hub, The Senses, and Returning to the Root

### Chapter 11 · The Utility of Nothingness (三十輻共一轂)
Thirty spokes join at the hub; in the center hole lies the utility of the wheel.  
Clay is molded to make vessels; in the hollow interior lies the utility of the pot.  
Doors and windows are cut out to make a room; in the open space lies the utility of the room.  
Therefore, profit comes from what is there; usefulness comes from what is not there.

### Chapter 12 · Restraining the Senses (五色令人目盲)
The five colors blind the human eye.  
The five sounds deafen the human ear.  
The five flavors spoil the human palate.  
Racing and hunting derange the human mind.  
Precious treasures impede the human path.  
Therefore the sage attends to the inner belly and not the outer eye;  
He rejects the outer and chooses the inner.

### Chapter 13 · Honor and Disgrace (寵辱若驚)
Favor and disgrace both cause alarm; prize great hardship as you prize your body.  
What means "favor and disgrace cause alarm"?  
Favor elevates; winning it brings agitation, losing it brings fear. This is called alarming.  
What means "prize hardship as you prize your body"?  
The reason I suffer hardship is because I have a self.  
If I had no self, what hardship could touch me?  
Therefore, whoever values the world as his own body can be entrusted with the world.  
Whoever loves the world as his own body can be given stewardship of the world.

### Chapter 14 · Praising the Formless (視之不見名曰夷)
Look at it, it cannot be seen: it is called formless (*Yi*).  
Listen to it, it cannot be heard: it is called soundless (*Xi*).  
Grasp at it, it cannot be held: it is called intangible (*Wei*).  
These three cannot be analyzed further, so they merge into One.  
Its rising brings no light; its setting brings no darkness.  
Unceasing and continuous, it cannot be named; it returns to the realm of nothingness.  
This is called the shape without shape, the image without form.  
Meet it, you see no front; follow it, you see no back.  
Hold to the ancient Tao to master the present conditions;  
To know the ancient beginning is the thread of the Tao.

### Chapter 15 · The Ancient Masters (古之善為道者)
The ancient masters who understood the Tao were subtle, mysterious, and profound;  
Their depth was beyond comprehension.  
Because they could not be fathomed, we can only describe their appearance:  
Hesitant, like crossing a winter stream;  
Watchful, like one fearing neighbors on all sides;  
Courteous, like an honored guest;  
Yielding, like ice beginning to melt;  
Genuine, like an uncarved block of wood;  
Open, like a hollow valley;  
Murky, like muddy water.  
Who can make the muddy water clear? Settle it gently and it becomes clear.  
Who can remain still for long? Move slowly and life gradually stirs.  
Those who preserve this Tao do not wish to be full;  
Because they are not full, they remain unworn and need no renewal.

### Chapter 16 · Returning to the Root (致虛極守靜篤)
Attain ultimate emptiness; maintain steadfast tranquility.  
All things arise together; I watch their cyclical return.  
Things flourish in endless variety, and each returns to its root.  
Returning to the root is called stillness; stillness is called fulfilling one's destiny.  
Fulfilling destiny is called the eternal; knowing the eternal is called enlightenment.  
Not knowing the eternal leads to reckless blunders.  
Knowing the eternal brings tolerance; tolerance leads to impartiality;  
Impartiality leads to sovereignty; sovereignty leads to heaven;  
Heaven leads to the Tao; the Tao is everlasting.  
Though the body perishes, one suffers no harm.

### Chapter 17 · The Levels of Leadership (太上下知有之)
The highest leaders: the people merely know they exist.  
The next best: the people love and praise them.  
The next: the people fear them.  
The lowest: the people despise them.  
When trust is insufficient, there will be no trust in return.  
Hesitant, weighing their words!  
When their task is accomplished and work is done,  
The people all say: "We did it ourselves!"

### Chapter 18 · The Decline of the Great Tao (大道廢有仁義)
When the great Tao is forgotten, benevolence and righteousness arise.  
When wisdom and cleverness emerge, great hypocrisy appears.  
When family harmony is lost, filial piety and devotion are preached.  
When the country falls into chaos, loyal ministers are honored.

### Chapter 19 · Returning to Simplicity (絕聖棄智)
Discard sagacity, abandon cleverness, and the people will benefit a hundredfold.  
Discard benevolence, abandon righteousness, and the people will return to filial love.  
Discard ingenuity, abandon profit, and thieves and robbers will disappear.  
These three are outer ornaments and insufficient.  
Therefore let people hold to what is genuine:  
Manifest simplicity, embrace the uncarved block, reduce selfishness, and moderate desires.

### Chapter 20 · Distinct from the World (絕學無憂)
Abandon pedantry and be free from care.  
Between "yes" and "yeah", what is the difference?  
Between good and evil, what is the divergence?  
What others fear, one must also fear—how absurd, endless confusion!  
The multitude are merry, as if feasting at a great banquet or climbing towers in spring.  
I alone am tranquil, giving no signs, like an infant who has not yet learned to smile.  
Drifting without a place to rest.  
The multitude have abundance; I alone seem to possess nothing.  
My mind is that of a fool, so untangled and simple!  
Common people are bright and distinct; I alone am dim and obscure.  
Common people are sharp and inquisitive; I alone am blunt and reserved.  
Undulating like the ocean, floating like a wandering gale.  
The multitude have purpose; I alone am clumsy and rustic.  
I alone am different from others, because I value suckling at the mother's breast.

## Part III: Chapters 21–30 · The Shadow of Dao, Stillness, and the Military Arts

### Chapter 21 · The Substance of Virtue (孔德之容)
The greatest virtue follows only the Tao.  
The Tao as a thing is elusive and intangible.  
Intangible and elusive, yet within it are forms!  
Elusive and intangible, yet within it are objects!  
Dark and dim, yet within it is the vital essence!  
This essence is profound and genuine; within it lies truth.  
From ancient times to the present, its name has never departed,  
By which we observe the origin of all things.  
How do I know the origin of all things? By this.

### Chapter 22 · Yielding and Overcoming (曲則全)
Yield and remain whole.  
Bend and become straight.  
Empty and become filled.  
Wear out and become renewed.  
Have little and gain; have much and be confused.  
Therefore the sage embraces the One and becomes the exemplar for the world.  
He does not show off, so he shines.  
He does not assert himself, so he is distinguished.  
He does not boast, so his merit is recognized.  
He is not proud, so his influence endures.  
Because he does not contend, no one in the world can contend with him.  
The ancient saying "Yield and remain whole" was no empty phrase.  
Truly, be whole and return to it.

### Chapter 23 · Spontaneity and Trust (希言自然)
To speak little is natural.  
A fierce squall does not last the morning; a sudden downpour does not last the day.  
Who creates them? Heaven and earth.  
If heaven and earth cannot sustain violent outbursts, how can humanity?  
Therefore, one who aligns with the Tao is accepted by the Tao.  
One who aligns with virtue is accepted by virtue.  
One who aligns with loss is accepted by loss.  
Trust where trust is due; where trust is lacking, there will be no trust.

### Chapter 24 · Unnecessary Additions (企者不立)
One who stands on tiptoe cannot stand firm.  
One who takes long strides cannot walk far.  
One who displays himself does not shine.  
One who justifies himself is not respected.  
One who boasts of merit gains no credit.  
One who is proud cannot lead for long.  
From the standpoint of the Tao, these are like leftover food and painful tumors;  
All creatures detest them.  
Therefore the follower of the Tao does not dwell in them.

### Chapter 25 · The Four Great Entities (有物混成)
There was something formless and complete, born before heaven and earth.  
Silent and boundless, standing alone without changing,  
Operating everywhere without exhaustion, it may be regarded as the mother of the world.  
I do not know its name; I style it "Tao".  
If forced to describe it, I call it "Great".  
Great means flowing out; flowing out means reaching far; reaching far means returning.  
Therefore: Tao is great, Heaven is great, Earth is great, and the Sovereign is great.  
In the universe there are four great powers, and the human sovereign is one.  
Humanity models after Earth; Earth models after Heaven;  
Heaven models after Tao; Tao models after its own spontaneous nature (*Ziran*).

### Chapter 26 · Gravity and Stillness (重為輕根)
The heavy is the root of the light; the still is the master of agitation.  
Therefore the sage travels all day without departing from his provision cart.  
Though surrounded by glorious palaces, he sits serene and untempted.  
Why should a lord of ten thousand chariots act lightly with his own person?  
To be light is to lose the root; to be agitated is to lose the master.

### Chapter 27 · Perfect Movement (善行無轍跡)
A good traveler leaves no tracks or ruts.  
A good speaker makes no slips or errors.  
A good counter uses no abacus or tallies.  
A well-shut door needs no bolts and cannot be opened.  
A well-tied knot needs no ropes and cannot be untied.  
Therefore the sage always excels at saving people, so no person is cast aside;  
He excels at saving things, so nothing is wasted.  
This is called inheriting the inner light.  
Thus the good person is the teacher of the unskillful; the unskillful is the resource of the good.  
If you do not respect the teacher and do not care for the resource,  
Even the clever are deeply confused. This is the profound secret.

### Chapter 28 · Holding the Receptive (知其雄守其雌)
Know the male, but hold to the female: be the ravine of the world.  
Being the ravine of the world, constant virtue will never leave you;  
You return to the state of an infant.  
Know the white, but hold to the black: be the pattern for the world.  
Being the pattern for the world, constant virtue will never falter;  
You return to the infinite boundless.  
Know honor, but hold to humility: be the valley of the world.  
Being the valley of the world, constant virtue will be complete;  
You return to the uncarved block.  
When the uncarved block is cut up, it becomes functional vessels.  
When the sage uses them, he becomes an enduring leader.  
Therefore the great master does not splinter things.

### Chapter 29 · Not Forcing the World (將欲取天下而為之)
Whoever wishes to take over the world and force it to their will: I see they will not succeed.  
The world is a sacred vessel; it cannot be forcibly acted upon.  
Whoever tampers with it ruins it; whoever grasps it loses it.  
Things lead or follow; blow hot or blow cold;  
Are strong or frail; rise up or fall down.  
Therefore the sage discards excess, extravagance, and extremes.

### Chapter 30 · War Without Aggression (以道佐人主者)
One who guides a ruler through the Tao does not conquer the world by force of arms.  
Such things tend to rebound.  
Where troops camp, brambles and thorns grow.  
After great campaigns, bad harvests inevitably follow.  
A good commander achieves results and stops; he dares not rely on armed might.  
Achieve results without bragging; achieve results without gloating;  
Achieve results without arrogance; achieve results only because there is no choice.  
This is called achieving results without forcing.  
When things reach peak vigor through force, they soon grow old;  
This is contrary to the Tao. What is contrary to the Tao meets an early end.

## Part IV: Chapters 31–37 · Weapons of Sorrow, The Uncarved Block, and Dao in Governance

### Chapter 31 · Weapons as Instruments of Grief (夫兵者不祥之器)
Fine weapons are instruments of evil omen; all creatures detest them.  
Therefore the follower of the Tao will not dwell with them.  
In times of peace, the honored place is on the left; in war, the honored place is on the right.  
Weapons are not the tools of a noble person;  
They are instruments of misfortune, to be used only when unavoidable.  
Peace and tranquility are what he prizes most.  
Victory is not a cause for celebration; to rejoice in victory is to rejoice in slaughter.  
One who rejoices in the slaughter of fellow humans cannot have his will in the world.  
The killing of multitudes should be mourned with sorrow and tears;  
A victory should be celebrated as a funeral rite.

### Chapter 32 · The Nameless Block (道常無名樸)
The Tao is eternally nameless and simple.  
Though small, none in the world can subordinate it.  
If lords and kings could hold to it, all things would yield of their own accord.  
Heaven and earth unite to rain sweet dew, falling equally upon all without decree.  
When order was instituted, names arose.  
Once names are established, know when to stop.  
Knowing when to stop prevents peril.  
The Tao flows in the world like streams and valleys into great rivers and the sea.

### Chapter 33 · Self-Mastery and Longevity (知人者智自知者明)
Knowing others is intelligence; knowing oneself is true enlightenment.  
Overcoming others requires physical force; overcoming oneself requires true strength.  
Knowing contentment is true wealth.  
Acting with perseverance requires strong resolve.  
Not losing one's foundation brings endurance.  
To die without perishing is true longevity.

### Chapter 34 · The All-Pervading Dao (大道氾兮其可左右)
The great Tao flows everywhere, to the left and to the right.  
All ten thousand things depend upon it for life, and it refuses none.  
It completes its work without claiming merit.  
It clothes and feeds all things without acting as their master.  
Eternally without desire, it may be called small.  
All things return to it, yet it acts not as their ruler: it may be called great.  
Because it never considers itself great, its greatness is accomplished.

### Chapter 35 · The Great Image (執大象天下往)
Hold to the Great Image, and the whole world will come to you.  
They come and suffer no harm, finding safety, peace, and abundance.  
Music and delicious feasts will make a traveler halt;  
Yet words spoken of the Tao seem bland and tasteless!  
Look at it, there is nothing special to see;  
Listen to it, there is nothing special to hear;  
Put it into practice, and its resources are inexhaustible.

### Chapter 36 · The Subtle Light (將欲歙之必固張之)
What is to be contracted must first be expanded.  
What is to be weakened must first be strengthened.  
What is to be overthrown must first be raised up.  
What is to be taken must first be given.  
This is called the subtle light (*Wei Ming*).  
The soft and yielding overcomes the hard and rigid.  
Fish should not be taken from the deep pool;  
The sharpest instruments of statecraft should not be put on public display.

### Chapter 37 · Non-Interference (道常無為而無不為)
The Tao eternally does nothing, yet nothing is left undone.  
If lords and rulers can hold to it, the ten thousand things will transform naturally.  
If, after transformation, desires should stir again,  
I would quiet them with the nameless uncarved block.  
With the nameless uncarved block, desires cease.  
Without desires, stillness returns, and the world settles itself in peace.

## Part V: Chapters 38–48 · Higher Virtue, The Reversal of Extremes, and Daily Letting Go

### Chapter 38 · Degrees of Virtue (上德不德是以有德)
High virtue is unaware of virtue, therefore it possesses virtue.  
Low virtue never loses sight of virtue, therefore it lacks virtue.  
High virtue acts without motive and has nothing to prove.  
Low virtue acts with intention and always has an agenda.  
High benevolence acts without contrivance.  
High righteousness acts with contrivance.  
High ritual acts, and when no one responds, rolls up its sleeves and forces compliance.  
Therefore: when Tao is lost, virtue appears.  
When virtue is lost, benevolence appears.  
When benevolence is lost, righteousness appears.  
When righteousness is lost, ritual appears.  
Now, ritual is the thin husk of loyalty and good faith, and the beginning of disorder.  
Foresight is the flower of the Tao, and the beginning of foolishness.  
Therefore the mature person dwells in the thick and not the thin;  
In the fruit and not the flower. He discards the latter and chooses the former.

### Chapter 39 · The Unity of Origins (昔之得一者)
Of old, those that attained the One:  
Heaven attained the One and became clear.  
Earth attained the One and became firm.  
Spirits attained the One and became efficacious.  
Valleys attained the One and became full.  
All things attained the One and lived.  
Lords and kings attained the One and set the standard for the realm.  
Without clarity, heaven might crack.  
Without firmness, earth might crumble.  
Without efficacy, spirits might fade.  
Without fullness, valleys might run dry.  
Without life, all things might perish.  
Without noble leadership, rulers might fall.  
Therefore nobility takes humility as its root; high takes low as its foundation.  
Lords call themselves "orphans", "solitary", and "unworthy".  
Do they not understand that their root lies in humility?  
Thus enumerate parts of a carriage, and no carriage exists.  
Do not desire to chime like costly jade, but rumble like common stone.

### Chapter 40 · Reversal and the Generative Void (反者道之動)
Return is the motion of the Tao.  
Yielding is the function of the Tao.  
All things under heaven are born of being;  
Being is born of non-being.

### Chapter 41 · Hearing the Dao (上士聞道勤而行之)
When the highest student hears of the Tao, he practices it diligently.  
When the mediocre student hears of the Tao, he keeps it and loses it.  
When the lowest student hears of the Tao, he laughs out loud.  
If he did not laugh, it would not be the Tao!  
Therefore the ancient poets said:  
The bright path seems dark;  
The forward path seems backward;  
The smooth path seems rough;  
The highest virtue seems like an empty valley;  
The purest white seems tarnished;  
Vast virtue seems deficient;  
Established virtue seems unsteady;  
Simple truth seems changeable;  
A great square has no corners;  
A great vessel is slowly completed;  
A great voice has faint sound;  
A great image has no boundary.  
The Tao is hidden and without name; yet it alone sustains and fulfills all things.

### Chapter 42 · The Stages of Creation (道生一一生二)
The Tao produced the One;  
The One produced the Two;  
The Two produced the Three;  
The Three produced the ten thousand things.  
The ten thousand things carry Yin on their backs and embrace Yang to their chests;  
Blending these vital forces, they attain harmony.  
What humans hate most is being orphaned, solitary, and needy;  
Yet kings and lords take these as their titles.  
For things may be diminished and thereby increase, or increased and thereby diminish.  
What others teach, I also teach:  
"The violent do not die a natural death."  
This shall be the cornerstone of my teaching.

### Chapter 43 · The Incorporeal Penetrates (天下之至柔)
The softest thing in the world overcomes the hardest.  
That which has no substance enters where there is no crevice.  
Through this I know the value of non-action.  
Teaching without words, benefiting without contriving:  
Few in the world can match this.

### Chapter 44 · Fame and Health (名與身孰親)
Fame or health: which is dearer?  
Health or wealth: which is more valuable?  
Gain or loss: which is more harmful?  
Excessive attachment leads to great cost.  
Excessive hoarding leads to heavy loss.  
Know contentment, and you will not suffer disgrace.  
Know when to stop, and you will meet no peril.  
Thus you can endure long.

### Chapter 45 · Great Perfection (大成若缺)
Great perfection seems flawed, yet its use is never impaired.  
Great fullness seems empty, yet its use is never exhausted.  
Great straightness seems bent.  
Great skill seems clumsy.  
Great eloquence seems halting.  
Movement overcomes cold; stillness overcomes heat.  
Pure stillness sets the standard for the universe.

### Chapter 46 · Curbing Ambition (天下有道卻走馬以糞)
When the world possesses the Tao, war horses are retired to fertilize fields.  
When the world loses the Tao, war horses are bred on the borders.  
There is no greater disaster than not knowing contentment;  
There is no greater fault than the desire for gain.  
Therefore the contentment of knowing contentment is eternal contentment.

### Chapter 47 · Knowing Without Travelling (不出戶知天下)
Without going out of the door, one can know the whole world.  
Without looking through the window, one can see the Tao of heaven.  
The further one travels, the less one knows.  
Therefore the sage knows without traveling,  
Names without looking,  
Accomplishes without striving.

### Chapter 48 · Forgetting Daily (為學日益為道日損)
In the pursuit of learning, one gains every day.  
In the pursuit of the Tao, one lets go every day.  
Let go and let go again, until you arrive at non-action (*wu wei*).  
Do nothing, and nothing is left undone.  
To master the world, always take no forced measures.  
If one resorts to forced measures, one is not fit to win the world.

---

## Part VI: Chapters 49–60 · The Sage Mind, Cultivating the Self, and Ruling Like Small Fish

### Chapter 49 · The Heart of the People (聖人無常心)
The sage has no fixed mind; he makes the heart of the people his own.  
To the good, I am good; to the not-good, I am also good: thus virtue is good.  
To the honest, I am honest; to the dishonest, I am also honest: thus virtue is honest.  
The sage lives in the world softly and harmoniously, blending his mind with the realm.  
The people fix their eyes and ears upon him; the sage regards them all as his children.

### Chapter 50 · The Threshold of Life and Death (出生入死)
Coming forth into life, entering into death.  
Three out of ten are companions of life;  
Three out of ten are companions of death;  
Three out of ten move from life to death because they cling to life too fiercely.  
Why is this? Because they live life too intensively.  
Now, I have heard that one skilled in preserving life travels on land without meeting rhinos or tigers,  
And marches into battle without wearing armor or shields.  
The rhino finds no place to thrust its horn; the tiger finds no place to fix its claw;  
The weapon finds no place to drive its blade.  
Why? Because he offers no vulnerable spot for death.

### Chapter 51 · Nourishing Virtue (道生之德畜之)
The Tao gives birth to all things; Virtue nourishes them;  
Matter gives them form; Environment completes them.  
Therefore all things revere the Tao and honor Virtue.  
Reverence for Tao and honor for Virtue are not commanded by decree,  
But arise naturally and spontaneously.  
Tao gives birth, Virtue nurtures, grows, matures, shelters, and sustains.  
To produce without possessing, to act without expecting, to guide without dominating:  
This is called profound virtue (*Xuan De*).

### Chapter 52 · Guarding the Mother (天下有始以為天下母)
The world had a beginning, which may be regarded as the Mother of all things.  
Having found the Mother, we understand her children.  
Knowing the children, return and guard the Mother;  
Though your body perishes, you will be free from danger.  
Block the openings, close the doors: to the end of your days you will not labor.  
Open the gates, multiply your affairs: to the end of your days you will have no salvation.  
Seeing into the small is called clarity; holding to the gentle is called strength.  
Use your light to return to your inner illumination,  
And do not bring ruin upon yourself: this is practicing the eternal.

### Chapter 53 · The Byways (使我介然有知)
If I possessed even a little wisdom, I would walk the broad Tao,  
Only fearing to stray into side paths.  
The great Tao is level and smooth, yet people love the rugged mountain trails.  
The royal courts are magnificent, while the fields are choked with weeds  
And the granaries stand completely empty!  
Wearing ornate robes, carrying sharp swords, gorgeously fed and drunk,  
Possessing riches beyond measure: this is called banditry and plunder.  
How far this is from the Tao!

### Chapter 54 · Cultivating Within (善建者不拔)
What is firmly rooted cannot be uprooted.  
What is tightly embraced cannot slip away.  
Generations will honor this without ceasing.  
Cultivated in oneself, virtue becomes genuine.  
Cultivated in the family, virtue becomes abundant.  
Cultivated in the community, virtue grows lasting.  
Cultivated in the state, virtue flourishes wide.  
Cultivated in the world, virtue becomes universal.  
Therefore: examine the self through the self;  
Examine the family through the family;  
Examine the community through the community;  
Examine the state through the state;  
Examine the world through the world.  
How do I know the condition of the world? By this.

### Chapter 55 · The Sign of Virtue (含德之厚比於赤子)
One who holds full virtue is like a newborn baby:  
Poisonous insects do not sting it; wild beasts do not claw it; birds of prey do not strike it.  
Its bones are soft and sinews supple, yet its grasp is firm.  
It knows not the union of male and female, yet its organ stirs: its vital energy is at its peak.  
It cries all day without growing hoarse: its inner harmony is perfect.  
To know harmony is called the eternal; to know the eternal is called clarity.  
To stimulate life excessively brings misfortune; when the mind drives the vital breath, it is called strain.  
When things reach peak vigor by force, they soon grow old;  
This is contrary to the Tao. What is contrary to the Tao meets an early end.

### Chapter 56 · The Mysterious Union (知者不言言者不知)
Those who know do not speak; those who speak do not know.  
Block the openings, close the doors;  
Blunt the sharp edges, untangle the knots;  
Soften the glare, settle with the dust:  
This is called the mysterious union (*Xuan Tong*).  
Therefore it cannot be won through intimacy, nor repelled through estrangement;  
Cannot be helped through profit, nor harmed through loss;  
Cannot be exalted through honor, nor degraded through disgrace.  
Therefore it is the most honored state in the world.

### Chapter 57 · Governing with Simplicity (以正治國以奇用兵)
Rule a nation with justice; fight a war with surprise; master the world with non-interference.  
How do I know this is so? By this:  
The more prohibitions and taboos in the realm, the poorer the people become.  
The more sharp weapons people possess, the more turmoil strikes the land.  
The more cleverness and craft people display, the stranger the monsters that appear.  
The more laws and decrees are published, the more thieves and robbers multiply.  
Therefore the sage says:  
I practice non-action, and the people transform themselves.  
I love tranquility, and the people correct themselves.  
I engage in no business, and the people enrich themselves.  
I have no desires, and the people return to the uncarved block.

### Chapter 58 · Light and Shadow in Governance (其政悶悶其民醇醇)
When the government is quiet and unobtrusive, the people are simple and genuine.  
When the government is alert and intrusive, the people are restless and cunning.  
Misfortune is what fortune leans upon; fortune is where misfortune hides.  
Who knows the ultimate balance? There is no fixed standard.  
The upright turns into the deceptive; the good turns into the monstrous.  
Humanity has been bewildered for a very long time!  
Therefore the sage is square but does not cut;  
Sharp but does not pierce;  
Straightforward but not rude;  
Radiant but does not dazzle.

### Chapter 59 · Moderation (治人事天莫若嗇)
In governing humans and serving heaven, nothing equals moderation.  
Moderation means preparing early.  
Preparing early means accumulating abundant virtue.  
With abundant virtue, nothing cannot be overcome.  
When nothing cannot be overcome, there are no limits.  
When there are no limits, one can possess the kingdom.  
Possessing the mother of the kingdom, one can endure long.  
This is called deep roots and firm base: the Tao of long life and lasting vision.

### Chapter 60 · Ruling Like Small Fish (治大國若烹小鮮)
Governing a great nation is like frying small fish (do not stir too much or they break apart).  
Approach the world through the Tao, and spirits lose their harmful power.  
Not that spirits lose their power, but their power will not harm people.  
Not only does their power not harm people, the sage does not harm people either.  
When neither does harm, their virtue flows together into one.

## Part VII: Chapters 61–70 · The Low Stream, The Three Treasures, and The Easy Way

### Chapter 61 · The Low Position (大邦者下流)
A great state is like the downstream river: the meeting point of the world, the receptive female of the realm.  
The female overcomes the male through stillness, placing herself in the lower position.  
Therefore, if a great state places itself below a small state, it wins the small state.  
If a small state places itself below a great state, it wins the great state.  
Thus some place themselves below to win; others are already below and win.  
The great state only desires to unite and nurture people;  
The small state only desires to enter and serve.  
Both obtain what they desire; therefore the great must learn to be below.

### Chapter 62 · The Treasure of All Things (道者萬物之奧)
The Tao is the inner sanctuary of all things: the treasure of the good, the refuge of the mistaken.  
Fine words can buy honor; noble deeds can win respect.  
Even if a person is mistaken, should they be abandoned?  
Therefore, when an emperor is crowned or ministers are appointed,  
Rather than presenting disks of jade driven by teams of four horses,  
It is better to sit and present this Tao.  
Why did the ancients prize this Tao so highly?  
Did they not say: "Seek and you shall receive; sin and you shall be forgiven"?  
Therefore it is the most precious treasure under heaven.

### Chapter 63 · Starting with the Easy (為無為事無事)
Act without acting; serve without serving; taste the tasteless.  
Great or small, many or few: respond to injury with virtue.  
Plan for what is difficult while it is still easy;  
Work on what is great while it is still small.  
The hardest tasks under heaven begin with what is easy;  
The greatest undertakings under heaven begin with what is small.  
Therefore the sage never attempts great things, and thus achieves greatness.  
One who makes promises lightly will rarely keep his word;  
One who thinks everything is easy will find everything difficult.  
Therefore the sage treats things with care, and never meets difficulty.

### Chapter 64 · At the Threshold of Action (其安易持)
What is at rest is easy to hold.  
What has not yet appeared is easy to prevent.  
What is brittle is easy to crack.  
What is minute is easy to disperse.  
Act before it happens; order things before disorder breaks out.  
A tree as thick as a person's embrace grows from a tiny sprout.  
A terrace nine stories high rises from a basket of earth.  
A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.  
He who forces it spoils it; he who grasps it loses it.  
Because the sage does not force, he does not spoil; because he does not grasp, he does not lose.  
People often fail on the verge of success;  
Be as careful at the end as at the beginning, and there will be no ruined work.  
Therefore the sage desires to have no desire, and prizes not rare goods;  
Learns what is unlearned, and restores the errors of the multitude;  
Aiding all things in their natural course, never presuming to interfere.

### Chapter 65 · Profound Simplicity (古之善為道者非以明民)
The ancient practitioners of the Tao did not use it to make people cunning,  
But to keep them genuine and simple.  
When people are difficult to govern, it is because they have too much cunning.  
To govern a nation with cunning is the bane of the nation;  
To govern a nation without cunning is the blessing of the nation.  
To know these two is to have a standard; always knowing this standard is profound virtue (*Xuan De*).  
Profound virtue is deep and wide; it returns with all things,  
Until it reaches great harmony.

### Chapter 66 · The King of Rivers (江海所以能為百谷王者)
Why are the sea and rivers kings of a hundred mountain streams?  
Because they excel at lying below them: therefore they can rule over all streams.  
So if the sage wishes to be above the people, he must speak from below them;  
If he wishes to lead the people, he must follow behind them.  
Thus the sage stands above and the people feel no weight;  
He walks ahead and the people feel no obstacle.  
Therefore the whole world delights in praising him without wearying.  
Because he does not contend, no one under heaven can contend with him.

### Chapter 67 · The Three Treasures (天下皆謂我道大)
The whole world says my Tao is great, resembling nothing else.  
Because it is great, it resembles nothing else.  
If it resembled anything else, it would have dwindled away long ago!  
I have three treasures which I hold and guard:  
The first is Compassion (*Ci*);  
The second is Frugality (*Jian*);  
The third is Not Presuming to Be First in the World (*Bu Gan Wei Tian Xia Xian*).  
Through compassion, one can be brave;  
Through frugality, one can be generous;  
Through not presuming to be first, one can develop enduring capacity.  
Now, to abandon compassion and try to be brave,  
To abandon frugality and try to be generous,  
To abandon the rear and try to be first: this is certain death!  
Compassion wins in attack, and stands firm in defense.  
What heaven wishes to save, it protects with compassion.

### Chapter 68 · The Warrior of Non-Contention (善為士者不武)
The skillful commander is not warlike;  
The skillful fighter does not lose his temper;  
The skillful conqueror does not grapple with the enemy;  
The skillful manager places himself below others.  
This is called the virtue of non-contending;  
This is called the power of utilizing people;  
This is called matching heaven, the highest standard of antiquity.

### Chapter 69 · Tactical Restraint (用兵有言)
Military strategists have a saying:  
"I dare not act as host, but prefer to act as guest;  
I dare not advance an inch, but prefer to retreat a foot."  
This is called marching without marching, rolling up sleeves without baring arms,  
Advancing without an enemy, holding weapons without brandishing.  
There is no greater catastrophe than underestimating the enemy;  
Underestimating the enemy risks losing my treasures.  
Therefore when two opposing forces clash,  
The one mourning with sorrow and compassion will emerge victorious.

### Chapter 70 · Easy to Understand (吾言甚易知甚易行)
My words are very easy to understand and very easy to practice;  
Yet none in the world can understand them, none in the world can practice them.  
Words have an ancestor; deeds have a master.  
Because people do not know this, they do not know me.  
Few know me, therefore I am precious.  
Thus the sage wears rough coarse clothing outside, and carries jade within his heart.

## Part VIII: Chapters 71–81 · Knowing Ignorance, Yielding Over Hardness, and Truth Without Adornment

### Chapter 71 · Sickness of Certainty (知不知上)
To know that you do not know is the highest wisdom;  
Not to know and pretend to know is a sickness.  
One who recognizes this sickness as sickness is free from the sickness.  
The sage is free from sickness because he recognizes it as sickness;  
Therefore he is not sick.

### Chapter 72 · Dreading the Awful (民不畏威則大威至)
When the people do not dread authority, greater authority will fall upon them.  
Do not crowd their dwellings; do not burden their livelihood.  
Because you do not oppress them, they will not despise you.  
Therefore the sage knows himself but does not display himself;  
Loves himself but does not pride himself.  
He discards the latter and chooses the former.

### Chapter 73 · The Net of Heaven (勇於敢則殺)
Courage in daring leads to death;  
Courage in caution leads to life.  
Of these two, one brings profit, the other brings harm.  
Who knows why heaven hates what it hates?  
Even the sage finds this difficult to resolve.  
The way of heaven does not contend, yet easily overcomes;  
Does not speak, yet easily answers;  
Does not call, yet all things come naturally;  
Is at ease, yet plans thoroughly.  
The net of heaven is vast, vast with wide meshes; yet nothing slips through.

### Chapter 74 · The Great Executioner (民不畏死奈何以死懼之)
If the people do not fear death, why threaten them with death?  
If you cause people to constantly fear death, and whoever does wrong is seized and killed,  
Who would dare?  
There is always the Master Executioner who kills.  
To kill in the place of the Master Executioner is like hewing timber in place of the master carpenter.  
Whoever hews timber in place of the master carpenter rarely escapes without injuring their own hands!

### Chapter 75 · Starvation from Taxes (民之飢以其上食稅之多)
Why are the people starving? Because their rulers consume too much grain in taxes.  
That is why they starve.  
Why are the people difficult to govern? Because their rulers meddle too much.  
That is why they are difficult to govern.  
Why do the people treat death lightly? Because their rulers demand too much for their own luxury.  
That is why they treat death lightly.  
Only those who do not strain for life are wiser than those who prize it too dearly.

### Chapter 76 · Softness Over Hardness (人之生也柔弱)
A human being is soft and supple in life; at death, rigid and stiff.  
The ten thousand plants and trees are soft and tender in life; at death, dry and withered.  
Therefore the stiff and unyielding are companions of death;  
The soft and yielding are companions of life.  
Thus an army that cannot yield will not conquer;  
A tree that cannot bend will break.  
The hard and mighty are placed below; the soft and supple are placed above.

### Chapter 77 · The Bow of Heaven (天之道其猶張弓歟)
The way of heaven—is it not like drawing a bow?  
What is high is brought down; what is low is raised up.  
What has surplus is diminished; what is deficient is supplemented.  
The way of heaven takes from what has surplus to replenish what is deficient.  
The way of humanity is not so: it takes from what is deficient to offer to what has surplus!  
Who can take their surplus and offer it to the world?  
Only one who possesses the Tao.  
Therefore the sage acts without relying on merit, accomplishes without dwelling on it,  
And has no desire to display his excellence.

### Chapter 78 · Paradox of Water (天下莫柔弱於水)
Nothing in the world is softer or more yielding than water;  
Yet for attacking what is hard and strong, nothing can surpass it.  
There is nothing that can take its place.  
The weak overcomes the strong; the soft overcomes the hard.  
Everyone under heaven knows this, yet none can put it into practice.  
Therefore the sage says:  
Whoever takes upon himself the disgrace of the nation is called the lord of the soil;  
Whoever takes upon himself the calamities of the nation is called the king of the world.  
Straightforward words often sound paradoxical.

### Chapter 79 · Keeping the Tally (和大怨必有餘怨)
When a great dispute is settled, some resentment inevitably lingers.  
How can this be considered good?  
Therefore the sage holds the left tally (the obligation) and makes no demands upon others.  
The virtuous person attends to his duty; the unvirtuous person insists on his claim.  
The way of heaven has no favorites; it always aligns with the good.

### Chapter 80 · The Small State with Few People (小國寡民)
Let there be a small state with few people.  
Though there are tools that can do the work of ten or a hundred men, let them not be used.  
Let the people prize life dearly and not travel to distant places.  
Though there are boats and carriages, there is no occasion to ride them.  
Though there are armor and weapons, there is no occasion to display them.  
Let the people return to knotting cords for reckoning.  
Sweeten their food, adorn their simple clothes, comfort their homes, delight in their customs.  
Though neighboring lands are within sight of each other,  
And the crowing of cocks and barking of dogs can be heard across the border,  
The people may grow old and die without having traveled between them.

### Chapter 81 · Truth Without Adornment (信言不美美言不信)
Sincere words are not beautiful; beautiful words are not sincere.  
The good do not argue; those who argue are not good.  
Those who know are not broadly learned; the broadly learned do not know.  
The sage does not hoard.  
The more he does for others, the more he possesses.  
The more he gives to others, the more he has in abundance.  
The way of heaven benefits and does not harm.  
The way of the sage acts and does not contend.
