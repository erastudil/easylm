# A claim from a sample

You can already use a letter as a blank, read a table, name a percent, and keep a unit on a number. A table and a rate live in the data book. Chance written as a share of a pile lives in the probability book. Name those doors and stop. This book is one object: a claim about a larger pile after you have only seen a smaller pile.

OpenStax *Introductory Statistics 2e* is the long school walk. The details page is the door. Author, ISBN, and update date on that page are unverified here. Open the door. Counts of people and households live at the U.S. Census Bureau. Pay, prices, and job counts live at the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. How far a measured number may wander lives at NIST. The NIST address named for uncertainty was not a living page, so no coverage-factor table is printed from it.

A number that must travel comes from one of those hosts, or the line says unverified. The clinic waits, library days, seed trays, and workshop scores below are invented piles so you can finish every sum by hand.

Public general education.

---

## 1. A sample

Forty people sit in the morning room at Willow clinic. You cannot time all forty before the first appointment. You time eight. Those eight waits, in minutes, are 4, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 18, and 41.

The forty are the pile you care about. The eight are the pile you actually hold. The smaller pile taken from the larger one is a **sample**. The larger pile you want a sentence about is a **population**. The names can wait. What you must not do is talk as if the eight were the forty.

How you pick the eight is part of the claim. If you time the first eight through the door, you have timed the early risers. If you time every fifth name on the sign-in sheet, you have spread the pick through the morning. If you time the eight who sat near you, you have timed your corner. Three different smaller piles. Three different stories about the forty.

A pick where each person in the forty has the same chance to land in the eight is a **simple random sample**. Chance as a share is the probability book's object. This book only needs the warning: a sticky pick is not a fair pick, and a claim from a sticky pick is a claim about the sticky group.

A blank is not a zero. If one of the eight left before you started the clock, you have seven waits, not eight waits with a zero. Write the seven. Name the missing one. Do not pad.

Census work is this object at national size. The decennial count of people and housing is a full pass every ten years. The American Community Survey is a sample run month by month. The Current Population Survey is a sample run each month. Those three programs are listed on the Census homepage as living doors. A number from a monthly sample is not a door-to-door count of every house. Write which one you used.

**Check.** Two hundred books sit on the Reed late shelf. You pull 25 and record days overdue. The 200 are the population for this shelf. The 25 are the sample. A new case: you pull the 25 with the reddest stamps because they are easy to see. Name the pick: the reddest 25, not 25 from the whole shelf. A claim about "Reed late books" from that pick is a claim about red stamps. A second new case: 12 seed trays on a bench, you count sprouts in 4 trays chosen by a fair draw of tray numbers. The 12 are the population of trays on that bench. The 4 are the sample. If tray 3 is empty because it was never planted, that tray is not a zero-sprout tray until you say you planted it.

## 2. Typical

You have the eight Willow waits. You want one number that can stand for them, so you can later say something about the forty.

Add the eight: $4 + 7 = 11$, $11 + 9 = 20$, $20 + 11 = 31$, $31 + 12 = 43$, $43 + 14 = 57$, $57 + 18 = 75$, $75 + 41 = 116$. Share 116 into 8 equal parts: $116 \div 8 = 14.5$. That share is the **mean**. It is the balance point of the eight numbers.

Line the eight from smallest to largest. They already sit in that order. The middle of an even count sits between two numbers. The fourth is 11. The fifth is 12. Halfway: $(11 + 12) \div 2 = 11.5$. That middle is the **median**. Half the sample is at or under it, half at or over it.

The number that shows up most often is the **mode**. Each Willow wait shows up once. There is no repeated wait to name.

The 41-minute wait pulls the mean to 14.5 while the median stays at 11.5. The mean followed the long wait. The median did not. When a pile has a long tail, the two typicals disagree. That disagreement is information. It is not a fight you average away.

On 15 September 2026 the U.S. Census Bureau homepage stated that median household income was $87,460 in 2025. Census printed a median, not a mean. Household money-in has a long upper tail, the same shape as a 41-minute wait in a room of shorter waits. $87,460 \div 12$: twelve times $7,288$ is $87,456$, so about $7,288.33$ a month. That national middle is a Census cell. The Willow 11.5 minutes is an invented clinic. Fetch [census.gov](https://www.census.gov/) or the 15 September 2026 income press release when the $87,460$ must travel.

The typical of the sample is not yet the typical of the forty. It is a candidate. Chapter 6 is the walk that treats it as a guess. Here you only owe a named typical of the eight, and a sentence that says which typical you named.

**Check.** A new pile of five seed counts, sprouts per tray: 4, 6, 7, 8, 10. Sum $4 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 10 = 35$. Mean $35 \div 5 = 7$. Median 7, the third number. Range of typicals: they match. A second new pile: 4, 6, 7, 8, 30. Sum $4 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 30 = 55$. Mean $55 \div 5 = 11$. Median still 7. The 30 pulled the mean. It did not pull the median. If the claim is "a typical tray on this bench sprouts 11," you named the mean and you followed the 30. If the claim is "a typical tray sprouts 7," you named the median. Write the name.

## 3. Spread

Two piles can share a typical and still be different stories.

Pile A: 10, 10, 10, 10, 10. Mean 10. Median 10. Every wait is 10.

Pile B: 2, 6, 10, 14, 18. Sum $2 + 6 + 10 + 14 + 18 = 50$. Mean $50 \div 5 = 10$. Median 10. The waits run from 2 to 18.

A claim "the typical wait is 10 minutes" is true of both as a typical. It is false as a picture of pile B, because most of pile B is not near 10. How far the numbers sit from the typical is **spread**.

The simplest spread is the gap from smallest to largest. That gap is the **range**. Pile A: $10 - 10 = 0$. Pile B: $18 - 2 = 16$. Willow: $41 - 4 = 37$. Range is easy and brittle. One far number owns it.

A spread that ignores the far tail uses the middles of the two halves. For the eight Willow waits, the lower four are 4, 7, 9, 11. Their median is $(7 + 9) \div 2 = 8$. The upper four are 12, 14, 18, 41. Their median is $(14 + 18) \div 2 = 16$. The gap $16 - 8 = 8$ is the **interquartile range**. The 41 still sits in the sample. It no longer owns the spread number.

A spread that uses every number: take each wait minus the mean, square those leftovers so the minus signs do not cancel, then share the squares. Willow mean 14.5. Leftovers: $-10.5$, $-7.5$, $-5.5$, $-3.5$, $-2.5$, $-0.5$, $3.5$, $26.5$. Their sum is 0, as it must if 14.5 is the mean. Squares: $110.25$, $56.25$, $30.25$, $12.25$, $6.25$, $0.25$, $12.25$, $702.25$. Sum of squares $930$.

For a sample, share the 930 into $8 - 1 = 7$ parts, not 8. The extra room is there because the mean was taken from these same eight, so one leftover is already pinned. $930 \div 7$ is $132.\overline{857}$. That share of squares is the **sample variance**. The original unit was minutes. Squares are minutes-squared. The unsquared size is the **sample standard deviation**: the number which, times itself, makes that variance. It is not a whole number here. Leave it under a square root if you do not have a square-root table. The 41 still owns a lot of the 930. That is the mean's spread talking, not the median's.

A cleaner five: 2, 6, 8, 10, 14. Sum $2 + 6 + 8 + 10 + 14 = 40$. Mean 8. Leftovers $-6, -2, 0, 2, 6$. Squares $36 + 4 + 0 + 4 + 36 = 80$. Sample variance $80 \div 4 = 20$. Sample standard deviation $\sqrt{20}$ minutes. Range $14 - 2 = 12$. Two spread stories, one pile.

NIST is the host that owns how far a measured number may wander. The listed uncertainty address was not a living page, so no official coverage factor is printed here. A measured typical without a named spread is a typical with its width torn off.

**Check.** New pile: 8, 10, 10, 12. Sum 40. Mean 10. Median 10. Range $12 - 8 = 4$. Leftovers $-2, 0, 0, 2$. Squares $4 + 0 + 0 + 4 = 8$. Sample variance $8 \div 3$. A second new pile: 8, 8, 8, 8. Mean 8. Range 0. Variance 0. Same kind of typical-work, two different widths. If someone reports only the 8, you cannot tell the piles apart.

## 4. A picture

A list of eight waits is already a picture if you write it. A picture that shows shape without making you add is a second tool.

Put a number line from 0 to 45. Put a dot above 4, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 18, and 41. Seven dots sit left of 20. One dot sits at 41. That is a **dot plot**. You can count the sample size by counting dots. If a picture hides the count, it is hiding the sample.

A **histogram** piles nearby numbers into bins. Bins of width 10 minutes: 0 to 10 holds 4, 7, 9, so three waits. 10 to 20 holds 11, 12, 14, 18, so four waits. 20 to 30 holds none. 30 to 40 holds none. 40 to 50 holds 41, so one wait. Three, four, zero, zero, one. The empty bins are part of the picture. They show the gap before the 41.

A **box** marks the median and the two half-medians from chapter 3. For Willow, the box runs from 8 to 16, with a line at 11.5. A whisker can run to the farthest wait that is not a long tail, and the 41 can sit as a separate mark. The box is the middle half of the sample. It is not the forty.

Do not let a smooth curve pretend you had thousands of waits. Eight dots are eight dots. A later chapter may put a chance-shape over a histogram. That shape is a model. Probability owns the chance-shape. This book owns the eight marks and the claim you will make from them.

A picture that starts the axis at 10, when waits run 4 to 41, cuts the left side off. A picture that stretches the 4-to-18 clump across a whole page hides how far 41 sits. Draw the axis through the whole sample. Put the unit on the axis: minutes, dollars, sprouts, not a bare number.

**Check.** Twelve Oak bus 7 delays, in minutes: 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 8, 11, 19. Bins of width 5: 0 to 5 holds 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, so nine delays. 5 to 10 holds 8, so one. 10 to 15 holds 11, so one. 15 to 20 holds 19, so one. Nine, one, one, one. A new case: the same twelve, bins of width 10. 0 to 10 holds ten delays (0 through 8). 10 to 20 holds two (11 and 19). Two pictures, one sample. If the claim is "almost all delays are under 5 minutes," the width-5 picture supports a count of 9 of 12. The width-10 picture cannot even ask that question, because 8 sits in the first bin with 0. Name the bins before you read a claim off a picture.

## 5. Chance meets a sample

Draw the eight Willow waits again, from the same forty, on a different morning of the same pick rule. You will not get 4, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 18, 41 again. You will get eight numbers. Their median will move. Their mean will move. The moving of the typical, from draw to draw, is **sampling variation**. The sample is a chance machine that emits a typical.

A small bag so you can see every draw. Five chips: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. The bag mean is $(2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 10) \div 5 = 6$. You draw two chips, no putting back, and take their mean. The ten unordered pairs and their means:

| pair | mean |
|---|---:|
| 2, 4 | 3 |
| 2, 6 | 4 |
| 2, 8 | 5 |
| 2, 10 | 6 |
| 4, 6 | 5 |
| 4, 8 | 6 |
| 4, 10 | 7 |
| 6, 8 | 7 |
| 6, 10 | 8 |
| 8, 10 | 9 |

The sample mean is 3 on one pair, 9 on another, 6 on two pairs. The bag mean 6 is the center of those ten means. It is not what every draw returns. If you drew 2 and 4, your sample mean is 3. That 3 is a true mean of the two chips you hold. It is a poor guess of the bag.

The share of pairs whose mean sits at 6 is 2 of 10, which is 0.2. Chance as a share is the probability book's object. Name that door. This book needs the list: the typical you hold is one row of a table you do not get to see.

Spread of one chip in the bag is large: range $10 - 2 = 8$. Spread of the two-chip means is smaller: range $9 - 3 = 6$. A typical built from more draws wanders less. That shrinking is the next chapter's margin.

A sticky draw from the bag, always the two chips nearest the mouth, is not a row from that table. Sampling variation is a fair-draw story. A sticky pick has a different story, usually a shifted typical, and no amount of redrawing the same sticky rule will find the bag.

**Check.** Same bag, now draw one chip. The five possible means are 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. Range of those means: 8, the same as the bag. A new case: draw all five chips. There is one pair, the whole bag, mean 6, range of the mean 0. One draw of one chip wanders as far as the bag. A draw of the whole population does not wander. Sample size sits between those poles. A second new case: chips 1, 1, 1, 1, 9. Bag mean $13 \div 5 = 2.6$. A draw of two that misses the 9 has mean 1. A draw that includes the 9 has mean 5. Two clumps of sample means, not a smooth fan. The shape of the bag is still in the draws.

## 6. An estimate

You timed eight waits. You want a sentence about the forty. The mean of the eight, 14.5 minutes, is your guess for the mean of the forty. A guess of a pile number, built from a sample, is an **estimate**. The 14.5 is a **point estimate**: one number, no width.

Chapter 5 showed that another eight would have moved. An estimate with no width pretends the machine emitted one possible typical. Put a width on it.

A first-look width uses the spread of one wait and the count of waits. Sixteen Oak delays have mean 11 minutes and sample standard deviation 4 minutes. The redraw-spread of the mean, for a fair pick, shrinks with the square root of the count: $4 \div \sqrt{16} = 4 \div 4 = 1$ minute. That 1 minute is the **standard error** of the mean. It is not the spread of one delay. It is how far the typical tends to wander when you redraw 16.

A common school first look puts about two of those redraw-spreads on each side of the estimate. $11 - 2 \times 1 = 9$ and $11 + 2 \times 1 = 13$. The interval 9 to 13 minutes is an **interval estimate**. It is a claim of the form: the pile mean sits in here, as a first look from this sample. The chance that a fair interval of this kind covers the pile mean is a share. Probability owns that share. The exact table that turns a share into a multiplier is unverified here. Do not print 1.96 from memory. Two is a first look, named as a first look.

More draws, tighter interval, if the spread of one stay similar. Twenty-five draws, same 4-minute spread: $4 \div \sqrt{25} = 4 \div 5 = 0.8$ minutes. Two redraw-spreads: $1.6$ minutes. A 100-draw sample would shrink that first-look half-width to $4 \div 10 = 0.4$, then $0.8$ minutes. You cannot buy that shrink with a sticky pick of 100 friends.

The interval is about the pile's typical, not about the next one person. A next Willow patient can still wait 41 minutes even if the forty's mean sits near 14. The interval did not promise that.

Census's $87,460$ is an estimate of a national median from a sample, announced as 2025 income on 15 September 2026. The homepage did not print the margin on that line. Do not invent one. Fetch the income press release when a margin must travel. Hispanic poverty on the same homepage was given as 13.9% in 2025, down 1.2 percentage points from 2024. Those percents are estimates. They are not a door-to-door poverty count of every household.

An estimate names its pile, its pick, its typical, and its width. "About 15 minutes" with no pile and no count is not an estimate. It is a shrug.

**Check.** Twenty-five seed trays, mean 18 sprouts, sample standard deviation 5 sprouts. Standard error $5 \div \sqrt{25} = 1$ sprout. First-look interval $18 \pm 2$, so 16 to 20 sprouts. A new case: four trays, mean 18, same spread 5. Standard error $5 \div \sqrt{4} = 2.5$. First-look interval $18 \pm 5$, so 13 to 23. Same mean, wider claim, because the sample is smaller. A third case: someone reports 18 sprouts, no count, no spread. There is no interval to write. Refuse the width. Keep the 18 as a point and mark the width unverified.

## 7. A comparison

Morning at Willow is one pile. Afternoon is another. You want a sentence about whether they differ.

Nine morning waits: 8, 9, 10, 10, 11, 12, 12, 13, 14. Sum $8 + 9 = 17$, $17 + 10 = 27$, $27 + 10 = 37$, $37 + 11 = 48$, $48 + 12 = 60$, $60 + 12 = 72$, $72 + 13 = 85$, $85 + 14 = 99$. Mean $99 \div 9 = 11$ minutes.

Nine afternoon waits: 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 16, 18, 21. Sum $10 + 12 = 22$, $22 + 13 = 35$, $35 + 14 = 49$, $49 + 15 = 64$, $64 + 16 = 80$, $80 + 16 = 96$, $96 + 18 = 114$, $114 + 21 = 135$. Mean $135 \div 9 = 15$ minutes.

The afternoon typical is $15 - 11 = 4$ minutes larger. That 4 is a **difference of means**. It is itself an estimate: a guess of how far the two population typicals sit apart.

A difference wanders too. If each mean has a redraw-spread, the difference has a redraw-spread of its own, larger than either one, because both sides move. A first look still asks: is the 4 large next to those redraw-spreads, or could redraws of two same piles spit out a 4 this often? The share of redraws that far is a chance. Probability owns the share. This book owns writing both typicals, the gap, the two counts, and the question.

Do not compare a mean to a median and call the gap a change. Morning median of those nine is 11, the fifth number. Afternoon median is 15, the fifth number. Here mean and median match on each side, so the 4 is the same story twice. They will not always match. Name which typical you compared.

A comparison of two times on one pile is the same object. BLS, on its homepage, stated that in August unemployment rates were lower in 8 states and the District of Columbia and stable in 42 states. Nonfarm payroll employment increased in 4 states and was essentially unchanged in 46 states and the District. That is a comparison across places, from samples, with a third bucket named "stable" or "essentially unchanged." Fetch [bls.gov](https://www.bls.gov/) when those counts must travel. Do not turn "stable in 42" into "unchanged in every county of those 42."

A price index is a comparison of two baskets. On 11 September 2026 the BLS homepage stated that the CPI for all items increased 0.4% in August, and that real average hourly earnings for all employees decreased 0.1% in August. Two comparisons, same month, opposite signs: the shopper basket up, the wage after prices down. They are not one number. A claim "prices rose 0.4%" that hides which index and which month is an unfinished comparison.

**Check.** Pine workshop scores out of 20. Morning six: 12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 18. Sum $12 + 14 = 26$, $26 + 15 = 41$, $41 + 15 = 56$, $56 + 16 = 72$, $72 + 18 = 90$. Mean 15. Afternoon six: 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 20. Sum $9 + 10 = 19$, $19 + 11 = 30$, $30 + 12 = 42$, $42 + 13 = 55$, $55 + 20 = 75$. Mean 12.5. Gap $15 - 12.5 = 2.5$ points, morning higher. The afternoon 20 is a long tail that lifted that mean. Afternoon median: 11 and 12, halfway 11.5. Morning median: 15 and 15, so 15. Median gap $15 - 11.5 = 3.5$. Mean gap 2.5. Same two groups, two gaps. Write which. A new case: two groups with means 15 and 15, ranges 2 and 14. The typicals match. The piles do not. A comparison of typicals that skips spread is a half comparison.

## 8. When two numbers move together

Each person at the Pine workshop also logged hours of practice the week before. Six pairs: hours 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and missed notes 18, 15, 13, 10, 8, 6.

Write them as pairs, the way algebra wrote $(x, y)$: $(1, 18)$, $(2, 15)$, $(3, 13)$, $(4, 10)$, $(5, 8)$, $(6, 6)$. Put hours across, missed notes up. The dots fall as you walk right. When one number rises and the other falls, the two **move together** in opposite directions.

A first-look slope from the ends: from $(1, 18)$ to $(6, 6)$, rise $6 - 18 = -12$, run $6 - 1 = 5$, slope $-\frac{12}{5} = -2.4$ missed notes per hour. Algebra's rise over run is the same object. Here the dots are a sample of people, not a rule that must pass through every pair. $(3, 13)$ is a little above a strict straight walk from the ends. The cloud is the object. The slope is a claim about the cloud.

A table of pairs lives in the data book. Name that door. This book owns the claim: in the larger pile of workshop students, more practice hours go with fewer missed notes, as a first look from these six.

The claim is association, not a cause. Maybe people who already miss fewer notes also bother to practice. Maybe a third fact, a teacher at the elbow, raises hours and cuts misses together. The six pairs cannot name the engine. They can name the walk of the dots.

A number that sits still while the other moves is a flat cloud. Hours 1 through 6, missed notes all 10: slope 0 from any pair. No together-walk. A number that jumps with no pattern, 18 then 6 then 17 then 5, is a cloud with no line worth naming. Draw the dots before you name a slope.

Six pairs are a small sample of students. Another six could tilt. The slope $-2.4$ is an estimate, chapter 6's object, for a population slope you did not see. Put a width on it before it travels far. The exact width from a formula is unverified here without a living table. The warning still holds: six dots, one cloud, not the whole workshop.

**Check.** New pairs, weeks of a stretch and sit-ups in a minute: $(1, 12)$, $(2, 14)$, $(3, 13)$, $(4, 17)$, $(5, 19)$. From $(1, 12)$ to $(5, 19)$, rise 7, run 4, slope $\frac{7}{4} = 1.75$ sit-ups per week. The dots generally rise. A pair that fails the rise: $(2, 14)$ to $(3, 13)$ falls by 1. The whole-walk slope can still be positive while one step falls. A second new case: $(1, 20)$, $(2, 20)$, $(3, 20)$, $(4, 20)$. Rise 0, run 3, slope 0. The sit-up count did not move with the week. Do not force a tilt.

## 9. A model as a first look

A straight rule through the practice cloud is a **model**: a machine that emits a missed-note guess from an hour guess. Algebra already wrote $y = mx + b$. Here $x$ is hours, $y$ is missed notes, and the rule is a first look at the six dots, not a law of music.

Try $y = 20 - 2x$. Person minus rule, hour by hour: $18-18=0$, $15-16=-1$, $13-14=-1$, $10-12=-2$, $8-10=-2$, $6-8=-2$. Those leftovers are **residuals**. Negative means the person missed fewer notes than the rule said.

A second rule, $y = 21 - 2.5x$, leaves $-0.5$, $-1$, $-0.5$, $-1$, $-0.5$, $0$. Smaller leftovers as a pile. Better first look for these six, if "better" means smaller leftovers.

One school way to name "smaller" is to square the leftovers and add. Squares forget the sign and punish a leftover of 4 more than four leftovers of 1. The straight rule that makes that sum of squares smallest is a **least-squares** line. Lists and a grid that solve for that line live in the linear-algebra book. Name that door. This book owns the picture: a first-look line, six residuals you can compute by hand, and a refusal to treat the line as the people.

Use the line inside the cloud. Hours in the sample run 1 to 6. Feeding $x = 40$ into $y = 21 - 2.5x$ emits $21 - 100 = -79$ missed notes, which is not a possible count. The model was a first look at a short walk. It is not a license to walk off the page.

A model is a claim. "Missed notes fall about two and a half per extra practice hour, for students in this workshop's hour range" is a claim from a sample. Chapter 10 is how you try to kill it.

**Check.** Four new pairs of weeks and sit-ups: $(1, 12)$, $(2, 14)$, $(3, 16)$, $(4, 18)$. The rule $y = 10 + 2x$ emits 12, 14, 16, 18. Four residuals 0. The line is a perfect first look for these four. A new point $(5, 17)$: the rule emits 20, residual $17 - 20 = -3$. The fifth person sits below the line. Keep the four, add the fifth, and the old line is no longer leftover-free. A second new rule $y = 11 + 2x$ on the original four: residuals $-1, -1, -1, -1$. Same slope, worse intercept. Put the intercept back to 10 if you want those four to close.

## 10. A check on a claim

A claim from a sample has a body you can take apart. Name the pile. Name the pick. Name the sample size. Name the typical and the spread. Look at a picture. Say whether chance was a fair draw. If the claim is a guess, put a width on it. If the claim is a difference, write both sides. If the claim is a together-walk, draw the pairs. Then try to kill the sentence with a number the sentence did not want.

Claim A: "Morning waits at Willow are typically under 10 minutes." Sample of eight, fair-draw story unknown, waits 4, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 18, 41. Mean 14.5. Median 11.5. Both typicals sit over 10. The picture is seven dots and a far 41, with four of eight already over 10. The claim dies in this sample. It can return only with a new pick, a new morning, or a new meaning of "typically" that you write in public.

Claim B: "The typical U.S. household money-in for 2025 was $87,460." Census's median, homepage dated 15 September 2026. The pile is U.S. households. The typical is a median, not a mean. The pick is a national sample, not your street. Width not on that homepage line, so unverified here. The claim may travel as Census's median. It may not travel as "every household took home $87,460."

Claim C: "Shopper prices rose 0.4% in August, so workers went backwards." BLS stated the 0.4% CPI rise and, separately, a 0.1% fall in real average hourly earnings, both dated 11 September 2026 on the BLS homepage. The second number already takes prices off the wage. The first number alone does not prove the "so." A comparison needs the matching pair.

Claim D: "More practice cuts missed notes." Six pairs, slope about $-2.4$ from the ends, a first-look line with leftovers. The together-walk is in the sample. Cause is not. A student who practiced 40 hours is outside the cloud. Kill the 40-hour prediction. Keep the in-cloud association as a first look.

The walk, short:

1. Write the pile you want a sentence about.
2. Write the smaller pile you hold, and how it was picked.
3. Write a typical and a spread, with units.
4. Draw the dots or the bins.
5. Say what chance was asked to do, or say the pick was sticky.
6. If you guess a pile number, write a point and a width, or mark the width unverified.
7. If you compare, write both typicals and the gap.
8. If you fit a line, write residuals for a row you did not use to draw the line.
9. Try to kill the sentence. If it survives, it is a claim from a sample. If it dies, write the number that killed it.

OpenStax *Introductory Statistics 2e* is the next long walk on this object. Census and BLS are the living national samples. NIST is the host for a measured number's width, once you have a living page. Data owns the table and the rate. Probability owns the share. This book owns the estimate, the comparison, and the sample-to-claim walk.
