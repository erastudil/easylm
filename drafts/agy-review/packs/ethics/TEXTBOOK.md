# What a person owes

Study taught you to hold a page. This book is the unpaid piece between two people, and how to walk that debt when two claims pull.

A mood about being good will not do the job. You need an owe you can name, test, and keep.

A survey of schools of thought lives in the living philosophy pack. A written statute lives in the law pack. An office you can walk into lives in the civic_life pack. This book names those doors and stops. It owns the walks for owe, harm, promise, and conflict.

---

## 1. What a person owes

You told Mira you would roll her bicycle back by Thursday night. Thursday comes. Mira has a clinic shift at 07:00 on Friday. The bicycle is still in your hall at 21:00. Her morning plan now sits on your word.

No coins changed hands. The unpaid piece is still there. Mira counted on an act you put in her week. If you keep the bicycle, she walks or she misses the shift. If you roll it over tonight, her Friday morning works as she drew it.

That unpaid piece is an **owe**. People also name it an obligation. The name is a handle for a claim one person has on another person's act. Money can be an owe. So can a returned tool, a kept hour, a true report, a warning before you move a ladder.

An owe has chairs. Who owes. Who is owed. What act would pay it. By when. What happens if it is not paid. Empty chairs are allowed. Write them empty. Do not fill them with a mood.

"Be a better neighbor" has no act and no when. "Roll the bicycle to Mira's stoop by 21:00 Thursday" has both. The second sentence can fail in public. Failure is how an owe earns the next sentence.

If a legislature wrote the bicycle rule, the law pack owns that walk. If a clerk can stop you, the civic_life pack owns that walk. Here the walk is between two people who can still name each other.

You can owe a person who is not in the room, or a group that shares a kitchen. Chapter 9 asks who belongs on the ledger. This chapter asks you to see one unpaid piece and write its chairs.

**Check.** Write the five chairs for Mira's bicycle. Who owes: you. Who is owed: Mira. Act: roll the bicycle to her stoop. When: by 21:00 Thursday. If unpaid: she has no ride at 07:00 Friday. If you wrote "be considerate" in the act chair, you wrote a mood. Put the bicycle in the sentence.

New case: a spare key. You took Noor's spare key on Monday so you could water her plants while she is away three nights. She returns Thursday at 18:00. Write the five chairs. The act is the key back in her hand by 18:00, not a feeling that you meant well. If the plants died and the key is home, you paid the key owe and you still have a harm from chapter 2.

## 2. Harm

The alley behind the bakery holds a wooden ladder. A neighbor left it against the wall to reach a roof patch after the last rain. You need the alley for a cart of flour sacks. You slide the ladder flat along the bricks and push the cart through. You do not see the neighbor. At dusk they come for the ladder and cannot stand it up in time. The patch waits. Rain is due at 22:00.

You made their evening worse. You took a tool out of a plan. You may not have wished them wet. The roof does not care about the wish.

Making someone worse off is **harm**. The name arrives after the roof. Harm can be a bruise, a lost hour, a ruined loaf, a ladder moved, a turn stolen in a line. Harm can be meant. Harm can be a side result you did not aim at. Both sit on the ledger. Aim changes blame. It does not always change the worse-off.

Harm is not the same as a hurt feeling with no lost act. Someone dislikes your hat. Their evening still works. Someone cannot patch a roof. Their evening does not. Write the lost act or the worse body before you use the word.

Harm is also not the same as using a person as a tool. You can harm someone by accident without steering them. You can also steer a person, without their say, so that your plan runs on their body or their time. People name that second move using someone as a mere means. The deontological ethics door in `LINK_INDEX.md` treats that move as a hard stop for one family of views: some choices stay forbidden even when the pile of later goods looks large.

A fresh case, not a hospital. Five fruit stalls on Cedar need ice by 15:00 or the berries collapse. One remaining sack sits in a sixth stall. That stall already paid the ice man. You take the sack because five stalls would keep more berries than one. You made the sixth stall worse, and you ran your plan on goods that stall had already bought. The berry count is a later chapter. The using is already here.

**Check.** You take one jar of jam from a stoop that holds 5 jars a neighbor set out for a sale at 09:00. The neighbor arrives at 08:55 and finds 4. Write the worse-off: 1 jar gone, 1 sale missing if each jar was one sale. Write whether you used their stoop as a tool. If you leave a coin that covers the jar, you may have paid a price and still taken a sale they had not offered you yet.

New case: you tell a true story about Noor's plants dying, in a shop where she was about to be hired to water a greenhouse. The hiring stops. The plants were real. The greenhouse job was a further loss. Name the harm to Noor in one sentence that a stranger could check: the job did not happen after your story. If your sentence is "gossip is bad," you named a fog.

## 3. A promise

Sam keeps a fruit stall on Cedar from 14:00 to 18:00 on weekdays. On Tuesday you say you will stand the stall from 14:00 to 16:00 on Thursday so Sam can take a child to a dentist. Sam books the dentist for 14:15. The booking is now a fact in someone else's week.

Thursday at 13:40 a loading dock offers you paid work for one hour. The pay is better than standing in the sun. The dentist slot is still at 14:15. If you walk to the dock, Sam must choose the stall or the child.

You put a future act into another person's plans, and they moved. That act-in-their-plans is a **promise**. The name is for the moment the other person is entitled to count on you. A hope you kept in your own head is not a promise. "I might be free Thursday" did not book a dentist.

A promise can be small. "I will bring the key at 18:00." A promise can be dated and narrow. "Two hours on the stall, 14:00 to 16:00, Thursday." Size does not make it. Being in someone else's plan makes it.

Breaking a promise harms more than the hour. Sam trusted the booking. The child sat in a chair. The stall sat empty. Other stallholders watched. Next Tuesday, your word weighs less. The later damage is real. One family of views, walked in chapter 5, says the later damage is why the break is wrong. Another family, walked in chapter 4, says the wrong is already in the break: you used Sam's trust as a ladder and then kicked it.

The consequentialism door in `LINK_INDEX.md` states the first family's claim in the old form: classic writers treated a promise-break as wrong because of future effects on other people, not because the promising sat in the past. Jeremy Bentham's book is dated 1789 on that door, John Stuart Mill's 1861, Henry Sidgwick's 1907. The door's own page first went up on 20 May 2003 and shows a substantive revision dated 4 October 2023. If those dates move, keep the door.

You can be released. Sam can say, before 14:00, "the dentist moved, stay at the dock." Then the owe is paid by their say. You cannot release yourself by finding better pay.

**Check.** You promised 2 hours. You arrive at 15:00 and leave at 16:00. Write what you paid: 1 hour. Write what you still owe: the 14:00 to 15:00 hour that held the dentist. If you write "I showed up, so I kept it," you counted presence. The promise counted 2 hours in a named window.

New case: you promised Noor the spare key at 18:00 Thursday. At 17:50 you are 12 minutes away on foot. You send a message: "running late." The key is still not in her hand at 18:00. The message is not the key. Write the unpaid chair. If she answers "come at 18:20," she moved the when. Then 18:20 is the new test.

## 4. A rule that would work for anyone

Four people stand in a line at the clinic on Pine. You have a form that takes two minutes. You walk to the desk and ask to cut, because two minutes is nothing next to their waits. If the clerk lets you through, you are seen at once. Each of the four loses a little time. You gain a lot of convenience.

Now give the same leave to everyone who "only needs two minutes." The line stops being a line. It becomes a scramble of people who have each decided their errand is small. Your private exception, copied, eats the practice that made the exception look cheap.

A test sits here. Take the rule you want to act on. Write it without your name. Ask whether a street could run if anyone may do it. If the street falls apart, you were asking for a private hole in a public floor.

People name that test a **rule for anyone**. In one old vocabulary it is a maxim you could will as a universal law. Immanuel Kant's *Groundwork* is dated 1785 on the deontological ethics door in `LINK_INDEX.md`. That door first went up on 21 November 2007 and shows a substantive revision dated 11 December 2024. The same door says this family of views judges a choice by whether it fits a norm, not by whether the later pile of goods is larger. Some choices stay closed even when the pile looks good.

The clinic cut fails the test. "People with short forms may walk to the front" is a rule that destroys the queue it feeds on. "People wait their turn, and a bleeding patient is taken first by a posted rule" can survive, because the exception is public and named, not a private wink.

A second test from the same family: do not run your plan on another person as a mere tool. The sixth stall's ice in chapter 2 fails that test. The four people in line are not tools if you wait. They become tools if you treat their waiting as a cushion for your hurry.

A posted clinic rule can become policy. Then civic_life or law owns the office that enforces it. The test here runs on a blank page before any office speaks.

**Check.** Write the rule you used if you cut the Pine line with a two-minute form. Strip your name. See whether four other people may use it this hour. If the desk then has five cutters and no line, the rule failed. Rewrite a rule that would still leave a line: wait, unless a posted emergency mark is on the form.

New case: you want to park in a loading bay for 8 minutes to buy bread. The posted limit is loading only. Write the nameless rule. If every bread errand may sit in that bay, the flour cart from chapter 2 has nowhere to stop. The 8 minutes felt small because other people were obeying a rule you were not offering them.

## 5. What happens

Sam left the stall at 14:00 because you said you would stand there. You stand. Two extra customers buy 4 loaves they would have missed. The child reaches the dentist at 14:15. You miss 1 hour of dock pay. The street has more bread, a treated tooth, and less cash in your pocket.

Those later facts are **what happens**. People also name them consequences. The name is for the world after the act, not for the mood before it.

One family of views says the right act is the one that makes the later world best, counted for everyone, not for you alone. The consequentialism door calls the old, strict form classic utilitarianism: an act is right if and only if it does the most net good, good minus bad, compared with the other acts you could do then. The slogan "greatest happiness for the greatest number" sits on that door as a warning. The door says the slogan misleads. An act can please more people and still fail the test, if the smaller group loses more than the larger group gains.

Count a small stall. You could move the last sack of ice from the sixth stall to five others. Five stalls keep berries. One stall loses its paid ice. If the five save 20 boxes and the one loses 8, the net boxes may be up. The using from chapter 2 is still there. This family is willing to talk about the 20 and the 8. The last chapter's family may refuse the move even if 20 beats 8.

Whose good counts in the sum is chapter 9. How sure you are of the 20 and the 8 is part of the walk. A guess is not a count. If you cannot fetch a living number, write unverified and do not build a duty on it.

This family also splits two jobs that people mash together. One job is a standard: what would make an act right. Another is a method you use in the moment. The consequentialism door says the old writers did not ask anyone to add up the whole future before buying bread. A baker can follow a habit that usually lands well, and the standard can still be the later world.

**Check.** Four people wait 10 minutes each if you take your turn. If you cut, you wait 0 and each of the four waits 12 minutes instead of 10. Write the extra wait you laid on them: 4 times 2 minutes is 8 minutes. You saved yourself some unknown number of minutes at the desk. If that unknown is 2, you moved 8 minutes of waiting onto four people to save 2. The net wait rose. If you cannot time the desk, mark your own saved minutes unverified.

New case: you keep the stall the full 2 hours. Bread sold: 4 extra loaves. Dock pay missed: 1 hour. Do not trade a loaf against a wage unless you say in ordinary words what you are counting as good. If you count only your pocket, you dropped Sam's child from the sum. Put the child back, or admit the ledger is partial.

## 6. A habit

Four Thursdays in a row you rolled Mira's bicycle back before dark. The fifth Thursday it rains. Your feet are wet. The bicycle is still in your hall at 20:40. You put on a coat and roll it over. You do not hold a debate in the kitchen. The act is already the sort of thing you do.

A settled way of noticing, caring, choosing, and acting is a **habit** in the ordinary sense, and when it is a moral excellence people name it a **virtue**. The virtue ethics door in `LINK_INDEX.md` says a virtue is an excellent trait of character: a disposition that goes all the way down, unlike the habit of drinking tea. An honest person is not only someone who did not cheat this noon. They see "that would be a lie" as a reason, they prefer honest company, they are not amused by a clever theft, and they teach a child the same.

That door first went up on 18 July 2003 and shows a substantive revision dated 3 May 2026. It frames three major approaches in normative ethics: an emphasis on character, an emphasis on duties or rules, and an emphasis on consequences. It traces the character approach to Plato and Aristotle, and to Mencius and Confucius, and dates a modern revival to the late 1950s, with G. E. M. Anscombe's paper of 1958 as a marker. The living philosophy pack surveys those schools. This chapter needs the working piece: a person you can count on when it rains.

A single returned bicycle does not prove the trait. Four returns and a fifth in the rain are still only a strip of evidence. The door warns against hanging a virtue on one observed act, especially if you do not know the person's reasons. Fear of Mira's anger can roll a bicycle. The virtue is in the reason that would still work if no one saw.

Children can want to help and still spill the flour. Adults who mean well and still spill, because they did not learn how bakeries work, are missing a second piece. People name that piece practical wisdom: knowing how to do the intended good in the actual kitchen. Returning the bicycle to the wrong stoop is not honesty completed.

**Check.** The shared oven on Oak has a lock. You lock it 6 nights. On night 7 you leave it open because you are tired. Write what the 6 nights showed and what night 7 showed. If you call yourself careful on the strength of the 6, you named a streak. The trait would have locked night 7.

New case: you tell the truth about Noor's plants to the greenhouse, and you also stay late to re-pot what can be saved. Truth without repair is one strip. Repair without truth is another. A person with both strips, on more than one week, is closer to the trait. One afternoon is still not the trait.

## 7. Fair share

Three people share a kitchen. Twelve eggs sit in the bowl on Sunday night. By Wednesday, one person has eaten 8. Two people have 4 left to split. If the week was "we share the eggs," 8 is more than a third of 12. A third of 12 is 4. That person took 4 above an equal split.

Equal split is one picture of a **fair share**. It is not the only picture. One of the three bought the eggs. One cooked every night. One was away Monday and Tuesday. Fair can mean equal piles, or piles by need, or piles by who put the eggs there, or piles by a rule you all said out loud on Sunday.

Write the rule before you weigh the 8. If the Sunday rule was "eat what you buy," the 8 may be paid for and the remaining 4 belong to the other buyers. If the Sunday rule was "house eggs, equal," the 8 is a taking. A fight that skips the Sunday rule is two moods colliding.

Fair share also lives above a kitchen. A body that tries to name what belongs to more than one town will publish a list. The UNESCO front door in `LINK_INDEX.md` is such a body for education, science, culture, and communication. That door, fetched for this book, lists 1273 World Heritage Sites, 849 entries for Intangible Cultural Heritage, 797 Biosphere Reserves, and 241 Global Geoparks. It says the World Heritage Committee has 21 members, elected from among 196 countries, and that the 48th session of that committee meets in Busan, Republic of Korea, from 19 to 29 July 2026, to decide which new sites to inscribe and to watch the ones already listed.

Those figures are a public attempt to count places and practices that are not only local property. They are not a kitchen rule. They show the shape: a named group, a count, a date, a door. If the door later shows a different count, keep the door. If you cannot open it, write unverified beside 1273.

A statute that divides a tax or a right is a law-pack walk. A town meeting that votes a share is a civic_life walk. Fair share here is the kitchen object: a pile, a rule you can say, a person who took more or less than the rule allowed.

**Check.** 12 eggs, 3 people, Sunday rule "equal house eggs." One person ate 8. Remaining 4 for 2 people is 2 each if they split what is left. The eater of 8 took 4 above the equal 4. Write those four numbers. If you write "they should be nicer," you dropped the rule.

New case: same 12 eggs, Sunday rule "eat what you buy." The eater of 8 paid for 9. Then 8 is inside the paid pile, and 1 paid egg remains. The other 3 eggs in the bowl belong to the other buyers. Fair share followed the purchase, not the equal third. Change the Sunday rule and the same 8 flips.

## 8. A conflict

Thursday at 13:50 you have two claims on one body. Sam's stall opens at 14:00 and you promised the first 2 hours. At 13:50 Mira falls in the alley. A cut on her hand will not slow. You can stay until a neighbor arrives. The neighbor is 25 minutes away. Or you can go to the stall and be on time. You cannot do both at 13:50.

Two owes, one clock, is a **conflict**. The name is for a joint that will not turn both ways at once. A hard choice between ice cream flavors is not a conflict in this sense. Two real owes that cannot both be paid in the same hour is.

Do not dissolve the joint with a slogan. "Family first" does not tell you whether Mira's cut outranks Sam's dentist. "The greatest good" does not tell you until you count, and even then chapter 4 may refuse the count. "Be virtuous" does not tell you which trait speaks louder when honesty to Sam and care for Mira pull apart.

Write both owes in chairs, as in chapter 1. Mira: stay until the neighbor comes, 25 minutes, or the cut keeps bleeding in an alley. Sam: stand the stall at 14:00, or the dentist slot and the stall collide. Now write what each unpaid chair does to the other person. Then write one act you will do, and which chair remains unpaid.

Sometimes the conflict is only apparent. If the neighbor is 5 minutes away, not 25, you can stay and still walk. If Sam can delay the dentist by a phone call you make at 13:51, you can pay both. Look for the apparent kind first. Do not pretend a 25-minute wait is a 5-minute wait.

Sometimes the conflict is real. Then you pay one, and you owe a repair on the other. You stay with Mira, and you phone Sam at 13:51 with the truth and a replacement if you can find one. The phone call does not keep the original promise. It is the next owe: do not leave Sam's plan in silence.

The virtue ethics door treats clashes of this kind as a shared problem with rule-based views. A wise look may show one demand was only apparent. A remainder may not resolve. The consequentialism door will try to add the later worlds. Keep both owes visible while you choose, so the unpaid one does not vanish into a story about how you had no choice.

**Check.** Neighbor 25 minutes away. Stall due in 10 minutes. Write the overlap: you would need to be in two places for 15 minutes. That 15 is the conflict in a number. If you "solve" it by deciding Sam will be fine, you erased a chair. Keep the 15. Then pick, and name the repair for the unpaid side.

New case: the neighbor is 5 minutes away. Overlap vanishes. You stay 5 minutes, then walk. If you still skip the stall, you did not have a conflict. You had a preference. Write that down so the word conflict does not get spent on a flavor.

## 9. Who counts

The alley also holds a dog that sleeps under the bakery stairs. A child not yet born will eat from next year's stall. A baker two towns over will buy flour from the same mill. A mountain on a public list will still be there when the bakers are gone. If you add only Mira and Sam, the ledger is small. If you add every living thing, the ledger may not close.

**Who counts** names whose harm, whose promise, whose share, and whose future sit on the page before you add. The page is not a census of 196 countries. Write the edge of the ledger.

People disagree at the edge. Some count only the two named adults. Some count every human now alive. Some count future humans. Some count animals that can hurt. Some count living places. The living philosophy pack surveys those disagreements. This chapter asks you to write the edge you are using, so a hidden cut does not look finished.

A public body can count without settling the whole edge. Chapter 7 already copied 1273 World Heritage Sites, 849 intangible practices, 797 biosphere reserves, 241 geoparks, 21 committee members, and 196 countries from the UNESCO front door. Those figures count places and practices a committee named, not voters. They do not tell you whether the alley dog counts. They show that some ledgers already include places and practices, not only persons with a ticket.

If you refuse to count the dog, say so. Then do not smuggle its pain back in as atmosphere. If you count the dog, the ice sack and the ladder have to be walked again with that row filled. If you count only humans, the mountain on the 1273-list still may count as a human share of a place, which is a different row: not the mountain's owe, but ours to each other about the mountain.

Law will say who has standing in a court. Civic_life will say who may vote or hold an office. Those are other books. Here the edge means whose worse-off you treat as harm in chapter 2.

**Check.** Write three rows for the Cedar ice sack: the sixth stallholder, the five other stallholders, the morning customers who will buy berries. Add or refuse a fourth row for the alley dog who eats spoiled fruit. If you add the dog, say what harm to it would look like in a sentence a stranger could check (no fruit on Thursday night, for example). If you refuse the dog, the sentence about spoiled fruit does not enter the sum.

New case: next year's child, not yet born, will shop at the stall. A promise to keep the stall honest is, or is not, an owe to that child. Pick one. Write one chair. If you cannot say what act would pay the child, you do not yet have an owe. You have a mood about the future.

## 10. A check

A night bakery on Oak promised 90 loaves to the morning market by 06:00 Friday. Three bakers work the night: Ren, Ola, and Tess. Each, with the oven free, can shape and finish 30 loaves by 06:00. Tess calls in sick at 21:00 Thursday. The oven still holds one batch at a time.

Owe. The bakery owes the market 90 loaves by 06:00. Ren and Ola owe each other a fair night's work. Tess owes notice; Tess gave it at 21:00. Write those chairs.

Harm. If 90 were promised and 60 arrive, stallholders who planned on 90 are short 30. Customers miss bread. The shortfall is 30 loaves, not a mood about a hard night.

Promise. 90 by 06:00 was put in the market's plan. A sign on the bakery door that says "we try our best" did not book 90. The 90 did.

Rule for anyone. Ren could hide 10 loaves for a friend before dawn. If any baker may hide 10, the market cannot trust a 90. The nameless rule fails. A posted, shared rule that a sick night cuts the order by 30, with a notice at 21:00, can be used by anyone and still leave a market.

What happens. Two bakers at 30 each make 60. Shortfall 30. If they skip braided loaves and each can finish 40, they make 80. Shortfall 10. The extra 20, from 60 to 80, costs decoration and sleep. Count in loaves.

Habit. Ola locks the oven every night for a month, then leaves it open on the sick night because the night is already ruined. The streak did not survive the rain. The trait would still lock.

Fair share. Two people now carry a three-person night. An equal split of remaining work is 45 loaves each if they aim at 90 and cannot reach it. A need split might give more shaping to the faster hands. Write the Sunday-style rule before the dough.

Conflict. At 02:10 a neighbor's child, known to Ola, runs a high fever. Ola could leave 40 minutes to open a medicine cabinet. Forty minutes is part of a baker's 30-loaf night. Stay, and the child waits on someone else. Leave, and some loaves miss 06:00. Write the overlap in minutes. Phone a replacement if one exists. If none exists, pick, and name the unpaid repair.

Who counts. Market stallholders. Morning buyers. Ren, Ola, Tess. The feverish child. The alley dog who eats scrap at 06:30. Write which rows you add. If you drop the child, do not spend the fever as atmosphere while you praise the 80 loaves.

Now open a door. The virtue ethics page in `LINK_INDEX.md` showed a first publication date of 18 July 2003 and a substantive revision dated 3 May 2026. Write the revision date you see. If you see 3 May 2026, you have the living page this book used. If you see a later date, keep the door and mark this book's date as old. If the host is dark, write unverified and do not invent a year.

The UNESCO front door showed 1273 World Heritage Sites. Open that door and copy the number it shows now. If it still shows 1273, the count held. If it shows another number, the door wins.

A food-sale statute and an inspector's office are other books. Name them and stop.

**Check.** Two bakers, 30 loaves each, 90 promised. Write 60 made, 30 short. Then write 40 each if they drop braids: 80 made, 10 short. If those four numbers are not on your paper, you described a hard night and you did not walk the object.

New case: Tess is not sick. All three bakers work. At 03:00 they are on track for 90. Ren hides 10 loaves for a friend. Write the nameless rule, the harm to the market (10 loaves), and the unpaid promise (90 became 80 without notice). If you excuse the 10 because 80 is "close," you moved the when and the count without Sam's equivalent of a release. Close is not 90.
